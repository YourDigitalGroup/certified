# Managed Monthly Websites — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Online Visibility (Room #1 in the Digital House — the foundation everything else stands on)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime target:** ~11.5 minutes + 10-question quiz *(by far the longest in the catalog — see runtime note in Section B, including a split option I'd now recommend taking)*
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.

## SECTION A — The Reusable Tactic Lesson Template
*(Use this exact shape for all ~25 tactic modules. Only the content changes — the beats never do.)*

Two questions before you write: **"What's the plain-English definition?"** and **"What are the 3 core mechanics?"** Everything else follows the beats.

**Voice convention (all modules):**
- **V1 — Guide:** the teaching spine. Carries every segment.
- **V2 — Scene:** short in-the-world lines only — a customer's search, a quoted reaction, a review snippet, and the quiz prompts. Its job is to signal a "mode switch" and keep the ear awake.
- *(Optional V3 later if you want a distinct quiz host — but two voices covers everything below.)*

**On-screen rule — the visual kit (all modules):** keep it clean and real. Three ingredients only:
1. **Animated text** — key words and short lines build/fade in as V1 speaks. This is the workhorse.
2. **Real photos** — actual screenshots of the product (real websites on a phone and a laptop, a real analytics view, a real chatbot conversation) and real photography of people/businesses (a contractor in a truck, a dental office front desk, a shop owner at a laptop). No clip-art, no cartoon figures.
3. **Green highlight box** — a green callout box drawn over a real screenshot to point at a specific section (same style as the sales sheet: box the padlock, box the contact form, box the phone number), with a text label animating in beside it.

**No cheesy line illustrations, icon/emoji cards, or metaphor graphics** (no glowing houses, brick-laying figures, leaky-bucket drawings, construction-worker mascots). Any analogy the Guide speaks stays *spoken only* — we don't draw it. Quiz cards being tappable is UI, not illustration, so that stays.

**Quiz blueprint (same every tactic):** Q1–3 = the 3 mechanics · Q4–6 = terms · Q7 = who it's good for + why · Q8 = why-it-matters scenario · Q9–10 = features/benefits (one may use the Segment-6 payoff).

**Answer placement rule (all modules):** vary which letter is correct — roughly even across A, B, C, and D, with no repeating pattern. If the correct answer sits at B nine times out of ten, trainees stop reading the options and start pattern-matching, and the quiz measures nothing. Also keep the wrong answers plausible; obviously silly distractors have the same effect.

**Payoff rules (Segment 6):** pick the easiest-to-picture business from Segment 3; keep outcomes plain and clearly *illustrative* — no invented numbers, no real names.

## SECTION B — Managed Monthly Websites: Full Script

> **Runtime note:** the beat table in Section A stays the standard for all modules. This one runs about 10:45 — by a wide margin the longest in the catalog — and that's on purpose. It's the first tactic most reps will sell, it's the foundation every other room in the Digital House connects to, and two separate cases have to land airtight: *why they need a good website at all*, and *why buying it monthly is the right way*. The seven segments and the quiz blueprint are unchanged; only the clock stretched. Segments 2 and 4 carry the extra weight.
>
> **If you'd rather split it:** the natural seam is between Segment 3 and Segment 4. Segments 1–3 make the case for needing a website ("Why Every Business Needs a Real Website"); Segments 4–7 cover how it works and how it's sold ("How the Managed Monthly Website Works"). Each half lands near five minutes, which matches the rest of the catalog. The cost of splitting is that the bucket reframe in Segment 2 and the "why monthly" beat in Segment 4 stop reinforcing each other in one sitting — so I'd keep it whole unless completion rates on long modules are a known problem.
>
> **Stat note — verify before recording.** Two figures are spoken in Segment 2: that roughly three out of four Americans use social media, and that a business post typically reaches only a couple percent of its own followers. Both were accurate as of mid-2026 (Pew Research for usage; Facebook page organic reach benchmarks running about 1.65%–5% of followers). The reach figure has been falling for a decade and will keep moving, so re-check it before the module is recorded, and keep the script's wording vague on purpose — "a couple percent," not a decimal. A rep quoting a precise number that's since changed is worse than one making the point directionally.
>
> **Framing note for the writer:** internally this is a recurring-revenue product. On camera, never say it that way. The idea a rep needs to carry into a room is *"a website isn't a thing you buy once, it's a service you keep"* — which happens to be both the honest pitch and the reason the revenue recurs. Segment 4, Mechanic 3 is where that lands, along with the nine-things-into-one argument for the monthly model.

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is *(0:00–1:15)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Real phone screenshot of a rough small-business website — tiny text you'd have to pinch, a stretched logo, a "Copyright 2016" in the footer. A **green box** draws around the copyright year, label *2016*. Then the box moves to a phone number that's clearly outdated. Then a second real screenshot: a business social page being used *as* a website — hours buried in a post from months ago, a **green box** on a comment reading "are you guys open today?" with no reply. Then hard cut to a clean, fast, modern site on the same phone, with a **green box** on a big obvious *Call Now* button. *(Photo option: brief real photo of a person squinting at their phone.)*

**V2 — Scene:** *"Wait, is this place even still open?"*
**V1 — Guide:** That's the question. And it's a terrible question for a customer to be asking about a business that is very much still open, doing great work, with a full schedule.
**V1 — Guide:** But look at what they're looking at. Text so small you've got to pinch to read it. A logo that's been stretched sideways. A copyright at the bottom that says 2016. And a phone number that hasn't been right in three years.
**V1 — Guide:** Now — nothing about that business is bad. The owner's terrific. The work's terrific. But the customer's not looking at the business. They're looking at the website. And the website is telling a story the owner never meant to tell.
**V1 — Guide:** And then there's the other version of this, which honestly you'll run into even more often.
**V2 — Scene:** *"Oh, we don't really have a website — just look us up on Facebook."*
**V1 — Guide:** You are going to hear that sentence a lot. And the owner says it with total confidence, because from where they're sitting it's working. They post. People comment. There's activity. It feels like a website.
**V1 — Guide:** So here's what's actually happening on that page. The hours are in a post from four months ago. Somebody asked "are you open today?" in the comments nine hours ago and nobody's answered. There's no way to book anything. And when a customer scrolls two inches, they're looking at a competitor's ad, because that's what the platform is *for*.
**V1 — Guide:** Hold that one. We're going to come right back to it, because it's the single most common thing standing between you and this sale.
**V1 — Guide:** So let's define it, plainly. A **website** is the one piece of the internet a business actually owns. Not rents, not borrows — owns. Their social page belongs to a social network. Their listing belongs to a search engine. The website is theirs.
**V1 — Guide:** And what we sell isn't a website the way you'd buy a couch. We sell a website that's **managed** — built for them, then hosted, secured, updated, and looked after every single month, by people whose job that is.
**V1 — Guide:** Here's the way to hold it in your head. A website isn't a couch. It's a storefront. Somebody's got to unlock it in the morning, keep the lights on, fix the door when it sticks, and change what's in the window when the season changes. Nobody buys a storefront once and walks away from it forever.
**V1 — Guide:** And if you've never sold a website before and you're a little nervous about it — good, you're normal. This is the easiest one to talk about, because you don't have to explain what a website is. Everybody already knows. You just have to explain why a bad one is costing them.

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters *(1:15–4:00)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of a small-business owner at a laptop (main-street feel). For the rented-land beat: a real business social page with a **green box** on a competitor's sponsored post sitting directly beneath its content, then a box on a months-old post where the hours are buried, then a box on a comment with no reply. Two text words hold on screen side by side — *rented* / *owned*. Then the arithmetic beat builds as three text lines over a real page's follower count, each with a **green box**: *800 followers · town of 40,000 · ~20–30 people see the post*. Then the rest of the sequence with **green boxes**: (1) a site loading slowly on a phone, box on a spinner; (2) a site where the phone number requires scrolling, box on the empty space where a Call button should be; (3) a search results page, box on a listing, then an arrow-free hard cut to the site that listing points to. Stakes text builds in four beats: *they own it → everything lands here → judged in seconds → it rots quietly.*

**V1 — Guide:** So why does a business *need* a good one? Four reasons, and I want you to actually be able to say these out loud in a room.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: it's the only piece they own.** And this is where we come back to "just look us up on Facebook," because you'll hear it in half your meetings and you need an answer ready that doesn't make the owner feel dumb.
**V1 — Guide:** Here's the frame. A social page is rented land. A website is owned land. Both are useful. Only one is theirs.
**V1 — Guide:** On rented land, somebody else sets the rules. The platform decides how many of their own followers actually see a post — and that number has been quietly cut, over and over, for years. The platform decides what appears next to their content, which is frequently a competitor paying to be there. The platform can restrict or lock a page over something automated and wrong, and then there's no phone number to call — businesses have lost ten years of posts and photos in an afternoon that way. And those followers were never really theirs. They can't take them anywhere. They're borrowed.
**V1 — Guide:** There's also a plain practical problem. A social page is a terrible destination for advertising. You can't build a page around one specific offer. You can't put a real quote form on it. It isn't built to get found by search engines and AI tools the way a website is. So an owner running ads *into* a social page is paying to send people somewhere that can't close.
**V1 — Guide:** Now, when they push back with "but everybody's on Facebook" — agree with them. They're right. Roughly three out of four Americans use social media. That's not the argument, and pretending otherwise makes you look like you don't know the landscape.
**V1 — Guide:** Here's the argument. Ask how many followers their page has. Say it's eight hundred, in a town of forty thousand. That's two percent of town before we start. Now the part almost no owner knows: these platforms show a typical business post to only a small slice of its *own* followers — a couple percent is the going rate. So eight hundred followers might mean twenty or thirty people actually laid eyes on that post.
**V1 — Guide:** Twenty or thirty people. In a town of forty thousand. That's the true reach of the thing they believe is their entire marketing plan. And it's not a glitch or something they did wrong — the platforms limit free reach on purpose, because reach is the product they sell. Free posting is the sample.
**V1 — Guide:** So the answer isn't either-or, it's **both**. Every small business needs social, and needs an actual plan for it — that's where people notice them, remember them, and feel like they know them. The website is where somebody goes to *check them out and buy.* Almost nobody spends real money on a local business without looking them up first — reviews, then the site. Social starts the relationship. The website closes it.
**V1 — Guide:** And notice this is the friendliest version of the pitch, not the harshest. You're not telling them to quit anything. You're telling them the two pieces do different jobs, and they've only got one of them.
**V1 — Guide:** Now — and this part matters — do not walk in and tell somebody their Facebook page is worthless. It isn't. Social is genuinely good at what it's good at: staying in front of people who already know you, showing some personality, putting up a photo of the new truck. Keep it. Post on it.
**V1 — Guide:** The line to actually say goes something like: *"Your Facebook page is great — keep it. But you're renting it, those followers aren't really yours, and only a couple percent of them ever see a post. The website is the part you own, and it's where somebody goes to check you out before they spend money. You want both."*
**V1 — Guide:** That's it. You're not taking anything away from them. You're pointing out they've been building on land they don't own. And with an owner who got burned by a social platform once — a lot of them have been — this lands hard.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: everything else lands here.** This is the big one, and it's the one reps miss. Every other thing we sell — the search work, the listings, the ads, the social, even the radio spot — all of it does one job. It sends a human being somewhere. And that somewhere is the website.
**V1 — Guide:** So picture a bucket. You can pour more and more water in the top — that's the advertising. But if the bucket's got holes in it, you're just pouring faster. A slow, confusing, out-of-date website is a bucket with holes. And the owner usually blames the water.
**V1 — Guide:** That reframe is worth memorizing, because it changes what you're selling. You're not selling a website. You're selling the thing that makes everything else they're already paying for actually work.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: people decide in about a second and a half.** Not on the writing. Not on the prices. On whether the thing looks legitimate and loads fast. Fair? No. Real? Completely. An owner who'd never dream of leaving their actual storefront dirty has a website with a broken photo on the homepage, and doesn't know it, because they never look at their own site. Nobody does.
**V1 — Guide:** **Four — and this is the one that pays for the whole conversation: a website rots.** Quietly. Not dramatically. Nobody wakes up to a website on fire.
**V1 — Guide:** What actually happens is small. A little piece of software the site depends on stops getting updates. The security certificate lapses and browsers start warning people away. Somebody adds a photo straight off their phone and the page slows to a crawl. The hours change and nobody updates them. A form quietly stops sending, and for four months every single person who filled it out went absolutely nowhere.
**V1 — Guide:** That last one happens constantly. And the owner's version of it is: *"digital doesn't work for us."*
**V1 — Guide:** So the stakes are pretty simple. A website built once and abandoned isn't an asset anymore — it's a slow leak. Somebody has to be watching it. That's the whole idea.

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For *(4:00–4:45)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Three real photos appear one at a time — an established family business with an obviously dated storefront sign, a business with a radio remote or visible advertising presence, a contractor's truck or a clinic waiting room. Each business type's name and its one-line reason type in beside the photo.

**V1 — Guide:** So who do you picture pitching this to? Three easy ones — and honestly, the first one you'll spot without even trying.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses whose site is old, broken, or nonexistent — including the social-page-only crowd.** The one their nephew built in college. The one that's just a Facebook page with nothing behind it. You don't have to create the pain here — it already exists, and the owner's usually a little embarrassed about it. Be kind about that. "Hey, when's the last time you pulled up your own place on your phone?" is a better opening than anything critical. And with the social-only business, remember you're adding the piece they own, not replacing the one they like.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses already spending money on advertising anywhere.** Radio, search, listings, social, print — doesn't matter. If they're paying to send people somewhere, the somewhere matters. This is your bucket-with-holes conversation, and it's the most natural add-on in the whole catalog.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses where a single customer is worth real money.** Contractors, clinics, dealerships, attorneys, anybody selling a big-ticket service. When one job is worth thousands, a website that turns even a handful more visitors into phone calls is not a hard case to make.
**V1 — Guide:** Notice the thread. Anybody who's either already paying for attention, or has customers worth chasing, needs a place for that attention to land. Which is very nearly everybody — this is the one tactic that fits almost any business you walk into.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics *(4:45–8:20)*
**ON-SCREEN:** One real website screenshot set on screen the whole time; a **green box** moves to the relevant piece for each mechanic. (1) Green box tracks from a search listing, a social post, and a listing profile, all landing on the same homepage → label *the destination*. (2) Green box moves through a fast mobile load, a thumb-sized Call button, a short form, a landing page, then a real chatbot conversation → label *the conversion*; then a real visitor-list view, names blurred → sub-label *knowing who came*. (3) Green box on the padlock, then a text checklist builds — *hosted · secured · updated · accessible · measured · content changed* → label *the upkeep*. Then the "why monthly" beat: the nine required items animate in as a scattered text list, each with its own separate box — then all nine boxes collapse into a single box labeled *one thing, once a month*. (No illustration — this is text and boxes only.) Closes on text: *Where it lands · What it does · Who keeps it running.*

**V1 — Guide:** How does this actually work? Three moving parts. Just three — but there's real substance under each one, so stay with me.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: the destination — this is where everything lands.** Every other room in the Digital House is a hallway. This is the room. Search work, listings, ads, social, the spot on the air — all of it points here.
**V1 — Guide:** Which includes their social page, by the way, and that's a useful thing to point out. Social isn't the competition to a website — it's a hallway *into* one. The post is where somebody notices you. The site is where they do something about it. A social page with nowhere to send people is a hallway that dead-ends.
**V1 — Guide:** Which means two things you can say out loud in a meeting. First, the website is a multiplier — it makes every other dollar work harder or softer. Second, it's the honest first sale. If somebody's site is a mess, selling them more traffic first is pouring water into that bucket. Fix the bucket. That's not a slick sales move, that's just being right, and owners can tell the difference.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: the conversion — the site has to actually *do* something.** A website that exists is not the same as a website that works. Existing is a business card. Working is a salesperson.
**V1 — Guide:** So what makes one work? It's less mysterious than people think. It loads fast, because people leave. It works on a phone with a thumb, because that's where they are. What you want them to do is obvious and near the top — call, book, get a quote — not buried three scrolls down. The form asks for three things instead of eleven, because every extra box loses people. And where there's a specific offer, it gets its own **landing page** — one page, one message, one action, nothing else competing for attention.
**V1 — Guide:** Then there's the part that works the shift nobody wants. A lot of people show up at ten at night with one question standing between them and calling. An **AI chatbot** on the site answers that question at ten at night. Not a gimmick — a night-shift employee who never calls in.
**V1 — Guide:** And here's the piece that'll make a rep's eyebrows go up, so make sure yours do too. Most people who visit a website never fill anything out. They look, they leave, and to the business owner they're a ghost. **Visitor identification** is technology that turns some of those anonymous visitors into actual known contacts — a real business you can follow up with.
**V1 — Guide:** Think about what that means for a contractor. Somebody spent four minutes on the roof-repair page at nine at night and never called. Before, that person never existed. Now there's a name. That's not a better website — that's a different kind of asset.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: the upkeep — this is the part that makes it a service instead of a purchase.** And this is where you earn the whole relationship, so let's be specific about what "managed" actually means.
**V1 — Guide:** It's **hosted** — the site lives on somebody's computer somewhere, and that somebody keeps it fast and online. It's **secured** — the padlock stays valid, the software stays current, and when something out on the internet changes, somebody's watching. It's **updated** — the owner emails "we've got new hours" or "take my old partner off the team page," and a real person who already knows their site makes the change. Not a support ticket into a void. A person.
**V1 — Guide:** It's built to be **accessible** — which means people using screen readers or navigating by keyboard can actually use it. That's the right thing to do, and it's also real legal exposure for a business that ignores it. It's **measured** — the analytics are set up correctly from day one, so there's actual information instead of guessing. And the **content gets changed** when the business changes, because businesses change constantly.
**V1 — Guide:** Now here's the honest comparison, and you don't need to name a single competitor to make it. The alternative isn't "a cheaper website." The alternative is the owner becoming their own IT department. Buying the space to put it. Keeping the software patched. Buying the little add-ons one at a time. Finding somebody when it breaks — and it breaks on a Saturday. Every one of those is a separate bill, a separate login, and a separate thing to remember. Most small-business owners will do that for about five months, and then the site starts to rot.
**V1 — Guide:** So let's answer the question an owner will absolutely ask you: *why monthly?* Because that's the part that trips reps up, and the answer is genuinely good.
**V1 — Guide:** Run through what actually has to happen for a small-business website to work. Somewhere to host it. A security certificate, kept valid. Software kept current. Analytics set up correctly. The structure search engines and AI tools need. Landing pages for specific offers. A chatbot to cover the after-hours questions. Accessibility, so people using assistive technology can use the site — and so the business isn't exposed. And a human being who'll make a change when the business changes.
**V1 — Guide:** Now picture that owner going and getting each one of those separately. Every single item on that list is its own product, from its own company, with its own bill, its own login, and its own renewal date they'll forget. Nine things to buy, nine things to manage, nine things to be wrong. A flooring contractor is not going to do that. Nobody is going to do that. They'd need somebody on staff whose whole job it is — which is exactly why the site their nephew built in 2016 still says 2016.
**V1 — Guide:** So the model does something pretty simple: it takes all nine of those and turns them into one. One predictable amount, once a month. Everything on the list included, not sold back to them piece by piece. Nothing extra to buy, no surprise add-on when they want a page changed.
**V1 — Guide:** And that's what actually puts this in reach. Not because it's cheap — because it's *sized right*. A small business can plan around something predictable every month. What they usually can't do is absorb one big hit up front, and then keep absorbing the little ones forever after. This is a real operating expense, like insurance or the phone bill, instead of a project they'll put off for another two years.
**V1 — Guide:** Then say the quiet part, because it's the strongest thing you've got: because it's monthly, it never stops being ours to look after. A one-time build is finished the day it launches, and from that day forward it's the owner's problem. This one has somebody watching it in month twenty-four the same as month one.
**V1 — Guide:** So the pitch isn't "our website is prettier." It's: one thing, one bill, handled forever, by people who already know your site. The owner goes back to running their business.
**V1 — Guide:** That's it. Where it lands, what it does, who keeps it running.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms *(8:20–9:10)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and its one-line definition animate in as text, one at a time. **Green box** on the real screenshot where the term is visible — the address bar for domain, the padlock for SSL, a real landing page, a completed form confirmation for conversion. Hosting is text-only.

**V1 — Guide:** Five terms so you sound like you know your stuff — because now you do. And the first two get mixed up constantly, including by people who've been doing this a while, so get these two clean.
**V1 — Guide:** **Domain** — the address. The words people type. That's it. You don't own it forever; you keep it registered, like a mailbox you keep paying for.
**V1 — Guide:** **Hosting** — the land. Every website lives on an actual computer somewhere, running around the clock. Hosting is that space, and keeping it fast and online. Domain is the address, hosting is the lot the building sits on. Different things, and an owner who mixes them up will assume one covers the other.
**V1 — Guide:** **SSL** — the little padlock in the address bar. It scrambles the information going back and forth so it can't be read in transit. No padlock, and some browsers will warn people off before they ever see the homepage.
**V1 — Guide:** **Landing page** — one page, built for one offer, with one action. No menu full of distractions. You send an ad or a campaign to a landing page instead of the homepage, because the homepage tries to do nine things and this page only does one.
**V1 — Guide:** **Conversion** — when a visitor does the thing you wanted. Calls, fills out the form, books the appointment. Traffic is people showing up. Conversion is people acting. Those are two totally different conversations, and knowing the difference is most of what separates a rep who sounds green from one who doesn't.

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff *(9:10–11:00)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature names animate in as a text list, green box ticking to each on the real screenshots. Then four benefit lines build as text: *Look legitimate · Turn visitors into leads · Never think about it again · Know who's actually showing up.* For the payoff: two real screenshots of a flooring-company site side by side — **before** (dated, tiny type, buried phone number) and **after** (fast on a phone, obvious Call button, a real landing page, a chatbot conversation open) — with a real photo of a contractor at a job site. A small text tag reads *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** So what's actually *in* this? Start with the site itself — pages built for that specific business, sized to what they actually need, not a template with their logo dropped on it. Secure hosting with the padlock included, not sold separately. Ongoing content support, meaning a real person makes the changes when the business changes. Analytics set up properly from day one. The structure search engines and AI tools need in order to find and quote them — built in, not bolted on later.
**V1 — Guide:** Then the pieces that do the selling: targeted landing pages for specific offers. An AI chatbot answering questions around the clock. Accessibility built in so people using assistive technology can actually use the site. Email addresses at their own domain, which quietly makes a two-person operation look like a real company. Modules for the things local businesses always end up wanting — testimonials, a newsletter signup, photo and video galleries. And visitor identification, turning anonymous traffic into contacts worth following up on.
