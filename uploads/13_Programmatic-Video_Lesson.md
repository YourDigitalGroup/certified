# Programmatic Video Ads — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Targeted Digital Marketing (Room #20 in the Digital House)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime:** 1366 counted spoken words ≈ **9:06** at 150 wpm
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.
**Spine — the one sentence a trainee must repeat cold:** *They already chose to watch something. Your ad is what plays first.*

## SECTION A — The Reusable Tactic Lesson Template
*(Identical across all modules. Only the content changes — the beats never do.)*

**Voice convention:** **V1 — Guide** carries the teaching. **V2 — Scene** delivers only in-world lines and the quiz prompts.

**On-screen kit:** animated text · real photos and product screenshots · green callout boxes. **No line illustrations, icon cards, mascots, or drawn metaphors.** Spoken analogies stay spoken.

| # | Segment | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Hook + What It Is** | Relatable moment → one-sentence definition |
| 2 | **Why It Matters** | Why an advertiser should do this + the evidence |
| 3 | **Who It's Great For** | 3 categories, one reason each + who it's wrong for |
| 4 | **How It Works — 3 Core Mechanics** | Exactly 3, roughly 4 beats each |
| 5 | **Common Terms** | Four to five terms maximum |

## SECTION B — Programmatic Video: Full Script

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is
**ON-SCREEN:** Real screenshot of somebody clicking a video on a news site, then a local business's video ad playing before it — **green box** on the countdown timer. Then three panels side by side, each with a green label: *website video · streaming TV · social feed.*

**V2 — Scene:** *"Fine. I'll watch the ad. I want to see the video."*
**V1 — Guide:** That sentence is the entire tactic, and it's a better deal than it sounds. That person just agreed to pay attention — not because they wanted your ad, but because they wanted what's behind it. And they'll sit there for it.
**V1 — Guide:** So, plainly: **programmatic video** is video ads that play around online video content — on websites and in apps, on whatever device somebody's using. Before the video, in the middle of it, or after it.
**V1 — Guide:** Now, we sell video in three different places, and reps mix them up constantly. So forget the product names for a second and think about **where the ad plays**.
**V1 — Guide:** **On websites and apps** — that's this one. Somebody clicked play on a video and your ad runs first. **On the television** — that's streaming TV, playing during a show somebody's watching in the living room. **In a social feed** — that's video inside a social ad, appearing while somebody scrolls past.
**V1 — Guide:** Website. Television. Feed. Three different moments — waiting for a video, watching TV, scrolling. Same video can serve all three, but never describe one as another.
**V1 — Guide:** It's bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis**, and here one impression means the video ad played for one viewer. And the line to carry: **they already chose to watch something — your ad is what plays first.**

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters
**ON-SCREEN:** Three statistics build as large animated numbers. Then a real photo of somebody watching video on a laptop, then the same person on a phone, **green bracket** linking them. Then text: *video isn't a format — it's how people watch the internet.*

**V1 — Guide:** So why should an advertiser do this? Three reasons.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: video is simply where attention lives now.** Nearly all adults who are online — something like ninety-five percent — watched online video in the past month. American adults now spend more than sixty percent of their daily screen time on video. Video isn't a format a client can opt out of anymore. It's how people use the internet.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: video is how people learn what something is.** Around ninety-six percent of people say they've watched an explainer video to understand a product. That's the part that matters for a local business with something to demonstrate — a process, a facility, a before and after, a room full of equipment. You can't put that in a banner. You can show it in fifteen seconds.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: the attention is borrowed, but it's real.** Every other digital format is competing for a distracted person. Here, the viewer has already committed to watching something and your ad stands between them and it. That's not a trick — it's the same bargain broadcast has always run on. If you've sold TV, you already understand this: people accept the commercial to get the program.
**V1 — Guide:** One honest note. That bargain has limits. People will wait through your ad, but they aren't rooting for it — so the first few seconds are the only part you can count on. That's a creative problem, not a media problem, and we'll come back to it.

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For
**ON-SCREEN:** Three real photos, one at a time — a trade school welding bay or nursing lab, a landscaping before-and-after, a business with a complicated service being demonstrated. Each type's name and reason type in beside it.

**V1 — Guide:** Three easy targets.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses that need to be seen to be believed.** Trade schools, med spas, landscapers, remodelers, equipment dealers, gyms. A description undersells them and a photo doesn't do it either. Fifteen seconds of the actual thing does the whole job.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses with something to explain.** Anything where the customer doesn't quite understand the service — a process, a treatment, a program, a piece of machinery. Video teaches; nothing else we sell really does.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses that need to look substantial.** Video reads as investment. For a local business competing against a regional or national name, a good video ad closes a credibility gap that no banner ever will.
**V1 — Guide:** And the honest flip side: this needs video. If a client has none and no budget to make any, that's a real barrier — production is available, but it's a separate conversation and you should have it upfront rather than after they've said yes. And a business selling something instantly obvious doesn't need fifteen seconds to explain it.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics
**ON-SCREEN:** (1) One video timeline with three labeled slots — *before · middle · after* — each highlighted in turn with a **green box** → *the three placements*. (2) Audience and geography options building as text beside a phone, tablet, and laptop linked by a green bracket → *aimed, on every device*. (3) A fifteen-second spot with the first three seconds boxed in green, plus a muted-sound icon → *the creative rules*. Closes on text: *Three placements · Every device · First three seconds.*

**V1 — Guide:** Three moving parts.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: the three placements.** These have names a client will hear, and each one is a different moment.
**V1 — Guide:** **Pre-roll** plays before the video somebody clicked. That's the strongest position, because the viewer is waiting and hasn't started watching anything yet. **Mid-roll** plays partway through — and it's better than it sounds, because somebody four minutes into a video is invested and will wait it out. **Post-roll** plays after the content ends, which is the weakest of the three, because the reason they were there is over.
**V1 — Guide:** If you've sold television, this maps almost exactly onto positions in a commercial pod. Same instinct: where in the content the spot sits changes what it's worth.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: aimed, and on every device.** This isn't a broad video buy. Audience segments get identified, geography gets set, and it runs **cross-device** — the phone, the tablet, the laptop, treated as the same person rather than three strangers.
**V1 — Guide:** That's worth contrasting with streaming TV, which lives on one screen in the living room. This one follows somebody through the day, which makes it the more flexible of the two — and the two together cover a lot of ground.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: the creative rules — this is where campaigns are won or lost.** Two things to tell every client.
**V1 — Guide:** **Front-load it.** The first few seconds are the only part you're guaranteed. Name the business, show the thing, say the point — then elaborate. A video that builds slowly to a reveal is a video most people never see the end of.
**V1 — Guide:** **Assume the sound is off.** A great many people watch with no audio, especially on a phone. If the message only works when you can hear it, most of the audience gets nothing. Put it on the screen.
**V1 — Guide:** Then the housekeeping: production if they need it, a daily ad schedule, a conversion pixel on the site, weekly refinement, and reporting.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and definition animate in as text, with green boxes on the video timeline positions and the linked devices.

**V1 — Guide:** Four terms and you're fluent.
**V1 — Guide:** **Pre-roll, mid-roll, and post-roll** — before, during, and after the video content. Pre-roll is the premium position; post-roll is the weakest.
**V1 — Guide:** **In-stream** — the ad plays inside a video player, wrapped around content somebody chose to watch. That's what makes this different from an ad sitting beside an article.
**V1 — Guide:** **Ad-supported platform** — any site or app that carries advertising in exchange for free content. That's where these run, and it's most of the internet.
**V1 — Guide:** **Cross-device** — you've met this one. The phone, the tablet, and the laptop in one household treated as the same person rather than three separate ones.

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature list animates in. Four benefit lines: *Borrowed attention that's real · Show what can't be described · Follows them across devices · Looks like an investment.* Payoff: a real trade-school welding bay photo beside a video ad playing before a news clip. Small tag: *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** What's in it? Pre-roll, mid-roll, and post-roll video ads created for the client and placed across ad-supported sites and apps. Audience segments identified. Geographic targeting. A daily ad schedule. Cross-device delivery on all devices. Conversion pixel tracking. Weekly refinement. Regular reporting. And video production if they don't have anything usable.
**V1 — Guide:** What does the owner get? **Borrowed attention that's real**, because the viewer is waiting for something. The ability to **show what can't be described.** Delivery that **follows the customer across devices** instead of one screen. And an ad that **reads as an investment**, which closes a credibility gap.
**V1 — Guide:** So picture a small technical college. Welding, diesel, nursing — genuinely excellent programs, and their advertising has always been a list of program names in a newspaper ad. Which tells a seventeen-year-old absolutely nothing.
**V1 — Guide:** After: fifteen seconds of sparks coming off a weld, a diesel engine coming apart on a lift, and a nursing lab — running before videos on sites where parents and teenagers in their county were already watching something. Name on screen in the first two seconds, because most of them had the sound off.
**V2 — Scene:** *"My son watched about four seconds of it and said 'I want to do that.'"*
**V1 — Guide:** — and that's what video does that nothing else we sell can. You can't put sparks in a banner ad. *(Illustrative — not a promise of specific numbers.)*

### SEGMENT 7 — Recap + Quiz Bridge
**ON-SCREEN:** Recap text — 1: three placements · 2: every device · 3: first three seconds. *Quiz →* button slides up.

**V1 — Guide:** Three parts. One: the three placements — pre-roll before, mid-roll during, post-roll after, in descending order of value. Two: aimed by audience and geography, running across every device as one person. Three: the creative rules — front-load the message, and assume the sound is off.
**V1 — Guide:** And two things to carry in. **They already chose to watch something; your ad is what plays first.** And keep the three video products straight: this one runs around web video, streaming TV runs on the television, and social video runs in the feed.
**V1 — Guide:** That's programmatic video. Quick ten-question check. No pressure — you already know this.

## SECTION D — AE Field Notes *(not narrated)*

**How the advertiser pays.** Bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis**. One impression means the video ad played for one viewer. Impression arithmetic is taught once, in Targeted Display.

**Keep the three video products straight — this is the most common confusion in the 201 block. Contrast them by *where the ad plays*, never by product name, because two of our product names begin with "Social" and that muddies it instantly.**
- **Programmatic video (this) — websites and apps:** around online video, on every device. The viewer clicked play on something else.
- **Streaming TV (Room 23):** on the television during a streaming show. One screen, whole room. Room 17 is the same delivery with social-style creative instead of a commercial.
- **Video in social ads (Room 15) — the feed:** inside a social feed, while somebody scrolls.

Same creative can serve all three. Describing one as another is how a client ends up disappointed by something they didn't actually buy.

**How it's sold.** Best opener is a question: *"What do you do that people would understand better if they could see it?"* If the answer is anything real — a process, a facility, a transformation — this is the tactic. If the answer is nothing, sell something else.

**Bundles with:**
- **Streaming TV (Room 23)** — the same video on the living room screen. Together they cover the day and the evening.
- **Social media ads** — same creative in the feed. One production, three placements.
- **Targeted display and geofencing** — same buying basis and machinery; video does the convincing, display does the reminding.
- **Website / landing pages** — the pixel needs a site, and a video-specific landing page carries the momentum.

**Objections and responses:**
- *"Everybody skips those ads."* → Be straight: many do, which is exactly why the first few seconds carry the message. Then point at the trade: people accept the ad to get the content, same bargain television has always run on.
- *"I don't have a video."* → Production is available, but have that conversation upfront. Also check for an old TV commercial — it may need trimming, not making.
- *"Isn't this the same as the streaming TV you sold me?"* → No. That's the television during a show. This is around video on websites and apps, on whatever device they're holding.
- *"Can I use my thirty-second commercial?"* → Often yes, but it usually wants trimming and the message moved to the front. Don't promise a straight lift without seeing it.

**Lines for the room:**
- *"They already chose to watch something. Your ad is what plays first."*
- *"You can't put sparks in a banner ad."*
- *"What do you do that people would understand better if they could see it?"*
- *"The first three seconds are the only part you're guaranteed. Spend them well."*

**Retention note.** Two risks. The usual measurement problem — set the pixel and site traffic as the agreed measure at the first meeting, not the click column. And **creative decay**: a single video running for months stops working, and viewers who've seen it four times start skipping on sight. Rotating creative is both good practice and a natural recurring conversation.

**Writer's notes:**
- **Statistics sources:** roughly 95% of online adults having watched online video in the past 30 days is from **DataReportal's 2026 global overview**, drawing on GWI survey data. The figure that US adults spend over 60% of daily screen time on digital video is **Statista**. The ~96% who have watched an explainer video to learn about a product is a widely reproduced marketing-research figure and the softest of the three — **it's the one to drop first if you want tighter sourcing.** Attribute as "research shows" and re-verify annually. All three prove one idea: *video is how people watch the internet, and how they learn what something is.*
- **Deliberately excluded:** the CPM figure, the completion-rate estimate printed on the sheet, the monthly minimum, the contract term, and the production quote. The completion figure was excluded per the standing rule on ad-performance metrics; the script makes the same point honestly through the front-load-the-message beat instead, which is more useful to a rep anyway.
- **The sound-off guidance** is deliberately stated without a percentage. Figures circulate in the 80–90% range for mobile sound-off viewing but sourcing is inconsistent, so the script says "a great many people" rather than quoting a number. Confirm if you want a figure attached.
- **Payoff vertical (technical college)** is new. Updated list: diner, roofer, flooring co., hearing clinic, insurance agency, storage co., garden center, medical clinic, auto repair shop, pest control co., RV dealership, used-car lot, physical therapy clinic, wedding/event venue, veterinary clinic, private school, clothing boutique, brewery/taproom, home builder, paint store, coffee drive-thru, **technical college**.
