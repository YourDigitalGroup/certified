# YouTube TV Ads — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Targeted Digital Marketing (Room #24 in the Digital House)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime:** 1282 counted spoken words ≈ **8:33** at 150 wpm
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.
**Spine — the one sentence a trainee must repeat cold:** *These viewers cancelled cable. A cable buy will never reach them.*

## SECTION B — YouTube TV Ads: Full Script

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of a family watching a live ballgame on a smart TV. Then a real YouTube TV channel guide with local and national channels visible, **green box** on a local station and on a sports channel. Then a real TV ad with a **green box** on a QR code in the corner.

**V2 — Scene:** *"We dropped cable two years ago. We've got YouTube TV — we still get the local news and the games."*
**V1 — Guide:** Hear what that person said. They didn't stop watching television — they stopped paying a cable company for it. And for years that made them unreachable. Same local news, same live sports your clients have always wanted to be in, but a cable buy doesn't touch them.
**V1 — Guide:** So, plainly: **YouTube TV ads** run on YouTube TV — a live television service delivered over the internet. Local channels, national channels, live sports, breaking news — the same lineup people used to get from cable, streamed to a smart TV, phone, tablet, or computer. And it's now the largest pay-television subscription service in the United States. Bigger than any cable company.
**V1 — Guide:** One thing to keep straight, because we sell two YouTube products. **YouTube TV is live television — channels, news, sports.** The other one, plain **YouTube**, is the video platform where people watch how-to videos and creators. Two different products, two different sheets. Don't blend them.
**V1 — Guide:** It's bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis**, and one impression here means the ad played during live programming on somebody's screen. The line to carry: **these viewers cancelled cable — a cable buy will never reach them.**

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters
**ON-SCREEN:** Two statistics build as large animated numbers. Then a real living room with several people watching one screen, **green bracket** around the group. Then a channel guide, **green box** on a local news channel. Then text: *the audience moved. The advertising didn't.*

**V1 — Guide:** So why should an advertiser do this? Three reasons.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: this is where the cable audience went.** Nielsen has cable down to roughly a fifth of all TV viewing while streaming pushes half. People didn't stop watching TV — they changed how they pay for it. And YouTube TV is now the largest pay-TV provider in the country, past the big cable companies. So a client buying cable for twenty years is missing a large and growing share of the audience he thinks he's buying.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: it's television that can be aimed.** A cable buy gets a program and everyone watching. This narrows it — channels that fit the business, geography that matters, and demographics on top if the client wants it. Same living room, same live game, far less waste.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: one impression can be a whole room.** Television is the last shared screen. An ad during a ballgame isn't reaching one person on one phone — it's reaching everybody sitting there. That's **co-viewing**, and it's a real advantage over every personal-device tactic we sell.
**V1 — Guide:** One honest note on budget. Live television is the most expensive thing per thousand in our catalog, for a good reason — biggest screen, during programming people schedule their evening around. This is a real-budget tactic. A client who can't fund it properly should buy something else, and saying so is what makes you trustworthy.

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For
**ON-SCREEN:** Three real photos, one at a time — a credit union or bank branch, a hospital or clinic exterior, a business with an obviously established local presence. Each type's name and reason type in beside it.

**V1 — Guide:** Three easy targets.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses that live or die on trust.** Banks and credit unions, medical, legal, insurance, funeral homes. When somebody's deciding who to trust with their money or their health, appearing during the evening news does something no banner will. Television confers seriousness.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses already buying broadcast or cable.** This is the easiest conversation you'll have all week: they already believe in television. You're not selling them a new idea — you're telling them a chunk of their audience moved and this is how to follow.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses that want to reach whole households.** Anything decided jointly — a vehicle, a vacation, a contractor, a school, a big appliance. Co-viewing means both decision-makers are in the room.
**V1 — Guide:** Honest flip side: a small budget spread across live TV reaches almost nobody. A modest budget is better served by our other video products — say so plainly rather than selling a campaign that disappears.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics
**ON-SCREEN:** (1) A real channel guide with specific channels checked, **green box** on them, beside a map radius → *choose channels and geography*. (2) A demographic panel building as text, with a green label *optional* → *narrow it further*. (3) A TV screen with a **green box** on the QR code, then a phone scanning it → *give them a way to respond*. Closes on text: *Channels and geography · Demographics if needed · A way to respond.*

**V1 — Guide:** Three moving parts.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: choose the channels and the geography.** The ads run on channels related to the business or the campaign, rather than scattered across everything. A farm supply store during agricultural programming. A credit union during local news. A tire shop during live sports.
**V1 — Guide:** And geography gets set so the money stays inside the area they actually serve — something traditional television handles badly, because a signal doesn't stop at a service boundary.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: narrow it further if it's worth it.** There's optional demographic targeting on top of the channel and geographic selection, drawing on **Google's first-party data** — meaning what Google knows about its own logged-in users, not information bought from somewhere else.
**V1 — Guide:** Be straight about the trade: narrowing the audience raises what it costs to reach a thousand people. Sometimes worth it, sometimes the channel choice already did the work. Having that out loud makes you a better partner than a rep who adds every option.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: give them a way to respond.** The oldest problem in television is that you can't click a TV. There's an optional fifteen- to thirty-second ad with a **QR code** on screen — somebody points their phone at it and lands on the client's website, from the couch.
**V1 — Guide:** That's the difference between "we ran some TV" and knowing something happened. For a client who's always liked television but could never prove it did anything, this is the beat that closes it.
**V1 — Guide:** Then the management: campaign management throughout, and regular reporting on how it delivered.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and definition animate in as text, with green boxes on the channel guide, a living room with several viewers, and the QR code.

**V1 — Guide:** Four terms and you're fluent.
**V1 — Guide:** **YouTube TV** — a live television service delivered over the internet, with local channels, national channels, sports, and news. It's a cable replacement, and it's the largest pay-TV service in the country. Not the same thing as YouTube itself.
**V1 — Guide:** **Cord-cutter** — somebody who cancelled cable or satellite and gets television another way. These are the people a cable buy no longer reaches.
**V1 — Guide:** **Co-viewing** — more than one person watching the same screen. Normal on a television, almost unheard of on a phone, and it means one impression can reach a household.
**V1 — Guide:** **First-party data** — here it means Google's own information about its logged-in users, used for the optional demographic targeting. Their data, not purchased from a third party.

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature list animates in. Four benefit lines: *Reach the audience cable lost · Television that can be aimed · One impression, whole household · A QR code off the screen.* Payoff: a real credit union branch photo beside a TV ad with a QR code during local news. Small tag: *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** What's in it? Ads on YouTube TV across channels relevant to the business. Geographic targeting. Optional demographic targeting using Google's first-party data. An optional fifteen- to thirty-second ad with a QR code linking to their website. Full campaign management. And reporting.
**V1 — Guide:** What does the owner get? They **reach the audience cable lost** — viewers no traditional TV buy can touch. They get **television that can actually be aimed** at channels and geography instead of blanketed. **One impression reaches a whole household**, not one person on one phone. And the **QR code gives people a way off the screen** and onto their site.
**V1 — Guide:** So picture a community credit union. Three branches, better rates than the regional bank, and a persistent problem — people assume a small credit union is less legitimate than a bank with a tower downtown.
**V1 — Guide:** After: their spot running during local news and evening sports on YouTube TV, inside their three counties, with a QR code on screen linking straight to the auto loan page. Same programming their members already watch. Same screen the big bank buys.
**V2 — Scene:** *"I saw you during the news, same as the bank. And my wife scanned the code before the game came back on."*
**V1 — Guide:** — and notice both halves of that. Television made them look like an equal. The QR code turned it into an application. *(Illustrative — not a promise of specific numbers.)*

### SEGMENT 7 — Recap + Quiz Bridge
**ON-SCREEN:** Recap text — 1: channels and geography · 2: demographics if needed · 3: a way to respond. *Quiz →* button slides up.

**V1 — Guide:** Three parts. One: choose the channels and the geography, so the ad runs where it fits and inside the area they serve. Two: narrow further with optional demographic targeting, knowing it raises the cost to reach a thousand people. Three: give them a way to respond, with a QR code straight off the screen.
**V1 — Guide:** And two things to carry in. **These viewers cancelled cable — a cable buy will never reach them.** And keep the two YouTube products separate: this is live television with channels and sports; the other is the video platform.
**V1 — Guide:** That's YouTube TV. Quick ten-question check. No pressure — you already know this.

## SECTION D — AE Field Notes *(not narrated)*

**How the advertiser pays.** Bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis**. One impression means the ad played during live programming on one screen. Optional demographic targeting carries a higher rate. Impression arithmetic is taught once, in Targeted Display.

**⚠️ This is not the same product as YouTube Video Ads. Two sheets, two buys.**
- **YouTube TV Ads (this):** the live television subscription service — local channels, national channels, live sports, breaking news. **A television buy.**
- **YouTube Video Ads (separate):** the YouTube video platform — creators, how-to videos, reviews. Channel and topic targeting against that content.

A client who wants "YouTube" may mean either. Ask which they're picturing: the channel guide, or the search bar. That question sorts it in one sentence.

**Budget reality — lead with it, don't bury it.** This is the highest cost per thousand in the catalog, and for a defensible reason: it's the largest screen, during appointment programming. **An underfunded live-TV campaign reaches almost nobody and the client will rightly cancel.** If the budget won't support it, recommend one of the other video products and keep the relationship. That recommendation is worth more than the sale.

**How it's sold.** The best opener is a question to any current television advertiser: *"How many of your customers still have cable?"* Owners genuinely don't know, and the honest answer is fewer every year. That's the whole pitch — the audience moved and the buy didn't follow.

**Bundles with:**
- **Broadcast and cable television** — the same spot, following the audience that left. For a TV seller this is additive, not competitive: it protects the client's reach as cable erodes.
- **YouTube Video Ads** — sold as a distinctly separate line item. Same platform family, completely different inventory.
- **Website / landing pages** — the QR code has to land somewhere good. A campaign-specific page makes this materially stronger and it's the natural attached sale.
- **Reputation management** — somebody who sees the ad and then looks the business up will find their reviews. A weak rating undoes an expensive impression.

**Objections and responses:**
- *"I already buy cable."* → "How many of your customers still have it? YouTube TV is now the biggest pay-TV service in the country. Those households aren't in your cable buy."
- *"Television is too expensive for me."* → Be honest. It is the most expensive thing per thousand that we sell. If the budget won't carry it, say so and recommend something else — don't shrink the campaign into invisibility.
- *"Isn't this the YouTube thing you already pitched me?"* → "Different product. That one's the video platform — how-to videos and creators. This is live TV with local channels and sports."
- *"How would I know it worked?"* → The QR code. That's the first time television has been able to answer that question directly.
- *"Can I be in the local news?"* → Often yes, and that's the best question a client can ask on this product. Channel relevance is the whole mechanic.

**Lines for the room:**
- *"These viewers cancelled cable. A cable buy will never reach them."*
- *"They didn't stop watching television. They stopped paying a cable company for it."*
- *"How many of your customers still have cable?"*
- *"Television is the last shared screen. One impression, the whole room."*
- *"Same programming your members already watch. Same screen the big bank buys."*

**Retention note.** Two risks, and the first one is the big one. **Budget mismatch** — an underfunded live-TV campaign produces nothing visible, and the client concludes streaming TV doesn't work when the real problem was weight. Prevent that at the first meeting by being candid about cost per thousand relative to the other video products. Second, **measurement** — make QR scans and site traffic the agreed measure from day one, because there is no click on a television.

**Writer's notes:**
- **Statistics sources:** cable's decline to roughly a fifth of US TV viewing while streaming approaches half is **Nielsen's The Gauge** (cable 20.4%, broadcast 19.2%, streaming 48.6% as of May 2026). YouTube TV being the largest pay-TV subscription service in the United States is stated on the sales sheet and is corroborated by 2026 industry reporting on subscriber counts overtaking the major cable operators. **Nielsen survives a challenge; cite it by name. Re-verify both before recording — the Gauge publishes monthly and the pay-TV ranking is recent enough to still be moving.**
- **Deliberately excluded:** the CPM figure, the additional demographic-targeting rate, the completion-rate estimate, the monthly minimum, and the contract term. The *relative* cost position is described directionally and without figures, because a rep who doesn't know this is the priciest product per thousand will sell it at a budget that guarantees failure.
- **Nielsen's YouTube TV-share data belongs here, not in the YouTube Video Ads module.** Module 22 was originally written using that data and has been corrected — it no longer positions the video platform as a television buy. Keep that separation if either module is revised.
- **Payoff vertical (community credit union)** is new. Updated list: diner, roofer, flooring co., hearing clinic, insurance agency, storage co., garden center, medical clinic, auto repair shop, pest control co., RV dealership, used-car lot, physical therapy clinic, wedding/event venue, veterinary clinic, private school, clothing boutique, brewery/taproom, home builder, paint store, coffee drive-thru, technical college, senior living community, farm equipment dealer, **community credit union**.
