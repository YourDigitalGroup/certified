# Social Display Ads — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Targeted Digital Marketing (Room #16 in the Digital House)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime:** 1446 counted spoken words ≈ **9:38** at 150 wpm
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.
**Spine — the one sentence a trainee must repeat cold:** *It looks like a social post. It runs everywhere else.*

## SECTION B — Social Display Ads: Full Script

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is
**ON-SCREEN:** Real screenshot of a news article with a standard rectangular banner ad beside it — **green box** on the banner, label *ignored*. Then the same slot filled with a real social display ad: profile name, follower count, photo, caption, like and comment counts, and a *Shop Now* button. Green boxes on the follower count and the like count.

**V2 — Scene:** *"Wait — is this an Instagram post? On a news site?"*
**V1 — Guide:** Sort of. What she's looking at is an ad. But it doesn't look like an ad — it looks like a post. Profile name at the top, a photo, a caption, and down at the bottom, the number of likes and comments.
**V1 — Guide:** So, plainly: **social display ads** are ads built to look and feel like real social media posts, running on regular websites all over the internet.
**V1 — Guide:** Now, we just covered social media ads, so keep these two straight. Those run **inside** the social platforms — in the feed, where people scroll. These take that same look and run it **outside** — on news sites, weather sites, recipe sites, wherever people spend the rest of their day.
**V1 — Guide:** Which means the name can trip people up, so get ahead of it. We call it *Social* Display because the ad is **built to look like a social post.** It does **not** run on social platforms. If a client hears "social" and pictures Facebook, correct that on the spot — this runs on the open web.
**V1 — Guide:** That's the sentence: **it looks like a social post, and it runs everywhere else.**
**V1 — Guide:** Here's the analogy. Everybody has learned to ignore the rectangle in the corner of a website. Nobody has learned to ignore a post. This borrows the shape people still look at and puts it where the rectangle used to be.
**V1 — Guide:** And like the rest of the display family, it's bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis.**

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters
**ON-SCREEN:** A real banner ad beside a real social display ad, both in the same page slot. Then three statistics build as animated numbers. Then text: *people don't ignore advertising — they ignore things shaped like advertising.*

**V1 — Guide:** So why should an advertiser do this? Three reasons.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: people have trained themselves to ignore the banner shape.** It's got a name — banner blindness — and studies put it at roughly two-thirds of people or higher, depending on who's counting. Not that they dislike the ad. They don't *see* it. Their eyes skip the rectangle the way you skip a legal disclaimer.
**V1 — Guide:** But look at what happens when the same message wears different clothes. Around three-quarters of consumers say they trust the content of ads built in a native style — ads that match what's around them — compared with roughly half for a conventional display ad. And people consistently rate that format as the least annoying of the options.
**V1 — Guide:** So here's the idea to carry out of this: **people don't ignore advertising. They ignore things that look like advertising.**
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: the ad carries its own proof.** This is the part that makes this product genuinely different, so slow down on it. A social display ad shows the real follower count, and the real number of likes and comments — and those update *live.* Somebody seeing that ad on a weather site isn't just seeing a claim from a business. They're seeing that four hundred other people liked it.
**V1 — Guide:** Every other ad a client runs has to argue for itself. This one arrives with evidence attached.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: it stretches work they've already paid for.** The client already has social content — the posts that did well, the photos they're proud of. Those get turned into ads instead of starting over. New creative without a new photoshoot.
**V1 — Guide:** One honest note: this only looks good if their social content looks good. A page with three blurry photos and eleven followers is not a candidate, and the follower count showing on the ad would work against them. That's a real qualifier, and it's worth checking before you pitch.

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For
**ON-SCREEN:** Three real photos, one at a time — a brewery taproom or bakery case, a boutique or salon interior, a business with an obviously active social page on screen. Each type's name and reason type in beside it.

**V1 — Guide:** Three easy targets.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses already doing social well.** If they've got a real following and posts that get engagement, this takes that work and puts it in front of a much bigger audience. The better their page, the better this performs — which makes it the natural next sale after social management.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses whose product photographs beautifully.** Breweries, bakeries, boutiques, salons, restaurants, remodelers. The format is built around a photo, so a good photo is most of the job.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses that need to reach past the platforms.** Some customers barely use social. Some are on it but never see the client's ads. This is how a business gets the social look in front of people who aren't scrolling a feed.
**V1 — Guide:** And the honest flip side: a weak social presence disqualifies this for now. So does a business with nothing visual to show. Both of those are better served somewhere else — and saying so is how you keep the account.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics
**ON-SCREEN:** (1) A real post transforming into an ad unit — profile name, caption, engagement counts, then a *Shop Now* button appearing that the original post didn't have, **green box** on the button → *build it like a post*. (2) The same ad appearing on three different real websites → *run it on the open web*. (3) The like count ticking upward on the live ad, then a weekly adjustment view → *keep it alive*. Closes on text: *Build it like a post · Run it everywhere · Keep it live.*

**V1 — Guide:** Three moving parts.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: build it like a post.** We take an existing post — organic or boosted, doesn't matter — and rebuild it as an ad unit. The profile name, the photo or video, the caption, the follower count, the engagement. It looks like what it came from.
**V1 — Guide:** But then we add two things a real post can't have. A proper **call to action** — an actual Shop Now or Book Now button — and control over the caption, so the wording can be written for advertising rather than for the feed. Same look, better job.
**V1 — Guide:** And the format follows the content: a single image, a carousel somebody can swipe, or video. Whatever the original was.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: run it on the open web.** This is the part that separates it from the last lesson. These ads go onto ordinary websites — not into the social feeds.
**V1 — Guide:** And because it's running on the open web, it uses the same targeting you already know from targeted display: audience targeting by who somebody is, behavioral targeting by what they've been doing, keyword and contextual targeting by what page they're on, and retargeting for people who already visited the client's site. Same toolbox, social-shaped creative.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: keep it live.** Here's the mechanic nobody expects. The likes and comments on that ad are **not a screenshot.** They update in real time. If the original post keeps collecting engagement, the ad reflects it — so the ad gets more persuasive on its own while it runs.
**V1 — Guide:** Then the usual management: audience segments, a daily ad schedule, ad set strategy, weekly refinement, and reporting. There's also an option to measure on-site visits, so a client can see whether people who saw the ad turned up in person.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and definition animate in as text, with green boxes on the engagement counts and on the ad appearing across different sites.

**V1 — Guide:** Four terms and you're fluent.
**V1 — Guide:** **Social display ad** — an ad built to look like a social post, running on regular websites instead of inside the social platforms.
**V1 — Guide:** **Native format** — any ad designed to match the look of the content around it, so it doesn't read as an interruption. This is one kind of native advertising, and it's the word clients will hear.
**V1 — Guide:** **Social proof** — the visible evidence that other people approve. The follower count, the likes, the comments. It's the reason this format persuades without arguing.
**V1 — Guide:** **Open web** — everything outside the social platforms. Every news site, weather site, and recipe blog people spend their day on. That's where these run.

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature list animates in. Four benefit lines: *Gets seen instead of skipped · Arrives with proof attached · Reaches beyond the feeds · Reuses content they already have.* Payoff: a real brewery social post beside the same content as a social display ad on a news site — plus a real photo of a taproom. Small tag: *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** What's in it? Existing posts converted into ad units that keep the social look. A real call-to-action button and controlled caption settings. Single image, carousel, or video, matching whatever the original was. Live engagement data on the ad. Audience segments identified. The full targeting toolbox — audience, behavioral, keyword and contextual, and retargeting. A daily ad schedule. Ad set strategy and campaign management with weekly refinement. Optional on-site visit measurement. And reporting.
**V1 — Guide:** What does the owner get? Their ad **gets seen instead of skipped**, because it doesn't wear the shape people ignore. It **arrives with proof attached** — real likes, real followers, updating live. It **reaches past the feeds**, onto the rest of the internet. And it **reuses content they already paid for.**
**V1 — Guide:** So picture a small-town brewery. Great taproom, genuinely good social page — a couple thousand followers, and photos of the seasonal releases that people actually comment on. All of it living inside one app, seen by people who already know them.
**V1 — Guide:** After: that same fall-release post, running as an ad on the regional news site and the weather app — with the follower count and the comment thread visible, and a Get Directions button the original post never had.
**V2 — Scene:** *"It looked like a post from a place I should already know about. So I looked them up."*
**V1 — Guide:** — and that's exactly the trick. The format bought her attention. The likes bought her trust. *(Illustrative — not a promise of specific numbers.)*

### SEGMENT 7 — Recap + Quiz Bridge
**ON-SCREEN:** Recap text — 1: build it like a post · 2: run it everywhere · 3: keep it live. *Quiz →* button slides up.

**V1 — Guide:** Three parts. One: build it like a post — from real content, plus a call-to-action button and caption control a real post can't have. Two: run it on the open web, with the same targeting as targeted display. Three: keep it live — the likes and comments update while the ad runs.
**V1 — Guide:** And the two things to carry in. **It looks like a social post, and it runs everywhere else.** And the reason it works: **people don't ignore advertising, they ignore things that look like advertising.**
**V1 — Guide:** That's social display. Quick ten-question check. No pressure — you already know this.

## SECTION D — AE Field Notes *(not narrated)*

**How the advertiser pays.** Bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis**, like the rest of the display family. On-site visit measurement is an optional add-on. Impression arithmetic is taught once, in Targeted Display.

**How it's sold.** This is the natural third sale in the social sequence: management builds the page, social ads reach strangers inside the platforms, social display takes the same content everywhere else. **Qualify the page first.** Pull up the client's social account in the meeting — if the follower count and engagement look healthy, this sells itself on sight, because the ad will display those numbers. If the page is thin, sell management first and come back.

**The demo is the pitch.** Show a banner ad and a social display ad side by side in the same slot. Nobody needs the explanation after that.

**Bundles with:**
- **Social media management** — a prerequisite in practice. Better page, better ad, since the ad shows the page's numbers.
- **Social media ads** — the pair: inside the feeds and outside them. Same creative working two places.
- **Targeted display** — identical targeting toolbox and buying basis; often one program.
- **Reputation management** — social proof on the ad and star ratings in search reinforce each other.

**Objections and responses:**
- *"Isn't this the same as the social ads you sold me?"* → "Same look, different real estate. Those run in the feed. These run on news sites, weather apps, everywhere else people spend their day."
- *"I've tried banner ads and got nothing."* → "You ran the shape people have learned to skip. This wears the shape they still look at — and it shows your likes and followers right on it."
- *"I don't have creative for this."* → "You already do. Pick your best post from the last few months."
- *"My follower count isn't impressive."* → Be honest. The count shows on the ad, so a thin page argues against the business. Fix the page first; that's the management conversation.

**Lines for the room:**
- *"It looks like a social post. It runs everywhere else."*
- *"People don't ignore advertising. They ignore things that look like advertising."*
- *"Every other ad you run has to argue for itself. This one shows up with four hundred likes attached."*
- *"Your best post from last month can be working on the news site by next week."*

**Retention note.** Same click-column risk as all display. Two items specific to this tactic: **creative freshness** — the whole premise is content that feels current, so recycling one post for six months undercuts it, and rotating in new posts is a natural recurring conversation. And **page health** — if the client's social engagement declines, the ad's social proof declines with it, which quietly weakens the product. That's a genuine reason to keep management running alongside.

**Writer's notes:**
- **Statistics sources:** banner blindness figures range from about 68% to 86% across studies (Infolinks eye-tracking work is the most-cited origin; 2026 display compilations report the lower end) — the script says "roughly two-thirds of people or higher, depending on who's counting," which is honest about the spread. The trust comparison (about three-quarters trusting native-style ad content versus roughly half for conventional display) is from an Outbrain consumer survey; note that Outbrain is a native advertising vendor, so treat it as directional rather than independent. The "least annoying format" finding comes from native format-perception research. **Attribute as "research shows" and re-verify annually.** All three prove one idea: *the format, not the message, is what gets ignored.*
- **Deliberately excluded:** the CPM figures, the on-site-visit add-on rate, the click-through comparison printed on the sheet, the required creative fee, the monthly minimum, and the contract term. Note that the sheet's benefit line quotes a click-through comparison against a national average — excluded per standing rules, and it's the kind of figure a rep shouldn't be repeating anyway.
- **⚠️ Ambiguity on the sales sheet — confirm before recording.** The description says these ads "boost campaign performance across major platforms like Facebook, Instagram, X, LinkedIn, Pinterest, Snapchat, and TikTok," which reads as though they run *inside* those platforms. But a benefit line says "target your desired audience on websites all over the web," and the targeting capabilities listed are open-web programmatic capabilities. **This script is written on the reading that the ads mimic the look of those platforms and run on the open web.** If that's wrong, the whole distinction from Room #15 collapses and the module needs rewriting. Please confirm.
