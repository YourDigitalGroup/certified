# Geofence Targeting Ads — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Targeted Digital Marketing (Room #14a in the Digital House)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime:** 1375 counted spoken words ≈ **9:10** at 150 wpm
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.
**Spine — the one sentence a trainee must repeat cold:** *Where somebody has been tells you what they need.*

## SECTION B — Geofence Targeting: Full Script

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is
**ON-SCREEN:** Real map view with a **green boundary** drawn around a single building — a competitor's parking lot. Then a real phone showing a display ad. Then the same map with boundaries around three places: a competitor, a fairground, a medical office.

**V2 — Scene:** *"I'm already standing in their parking lot."*
**V1 — Guide:** Think about what that tells you. That person isn't curious or researching. They drove somewhere, parked, and walked in — they're actively spending money on this exact thing today.
**V1 — Guide:** Now imagine advertising to that person. Not their town. Not their zip code. *Them*, because of where their feet are.
**V1 — Guide:** So, plainly: **geofence targeting** is drawing a boundary around a real physical place and serving ads to the phones that come inside it. A competitor's lot. A ballpark during a tournament. The county fairgrounds. A hospital campus. Any place your client's future customers are standing.
**V1 — Guide:** Here's the analogy, and you'll use it. This is handing out flyers in a parking lot — except you don't have to stand there, nobody can throw it away, and you can keep reaching them for weeks after they drive off.
**V1 — Guide:** That last part is the piece most reps miss, so hang onto it. And on the business side, this is bought the same way as the rest of our display products: **on a cost-per-thousand impression basis** — the advertiser pays for the ad being served, not clicked.

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of somebody in a parking lot on a phone. Then a real map with a boundary around a competitor. Then three statistics build as animated numbers. Then text: *location is intent.*

**V1 — Guide:** So why should an advertiser do this? Three reasons.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: standing somewhere is the strongest signal a person can give.** Everything else in digital guesses at intent from what somebody typed or read. This doesn't guess. Somebody in a competitor's showroom is shopping right now — there is no interpretation required.
**V1 — Guide:** And the research lines up. Roughly two-thirds of consumers say a relevant location-based offer makes them more likely to visit a store, and about half say they've actually done it. Around eight in ten would rather see advertising tied to their own area than something generic. Useful doesn't feel intrusive — it feels convenient.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: it puts a small business in front of a crowd it could never afford to buy outright.** A tournament weekend brings four thousand people into town. Advertising to a whole market to reach those four thousand is wasteful. Fencing the building isn't.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: it's the one tactic that can point at a competitor.** This is called **conquesting**, and it's the conversation owners lean forward for. If the shop across town is somebody's problem, this is the tool that does something about it — reaching their customers, in their parking lot, before the sale happens.
**V1 — Guide:** One honest caution on that: this isn't a magic wand pointed at a competitor. It's an introduction at a good moment. Say it that way and you'll never overpromise.

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For
**ON-SCREEN:** Three real photos, one at a time — a competitor's storefront across a street, a fairgrounds or tournament field with a crowd, a hospital or medical office campus. Each type's name and reason type in beside it.

**V1 — Guide:** Three easy targets.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses with a direct competitor nearby.** Auto, furniture, powersports, tire shops, pharmacy. Anywhere customers physically shop around, fencing the competition puts the client in the consideration set at the last possible second.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses that live off events.** Restaurants, hotels, retail near a fairground, tournament field, or arena. Events create a temporary crowd of exactly the right people, and the fence is how you talk to them.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses whose customers come *from* somewhere identifiable.** A physical therapy clinic near an orthopedic office. A tax preparer near a big employer. A daycare near a hospital campus. If you can name the building their customers walk out of, you can fence it.
**V1 — Guide:** And the honest flip side: if a client's customers aren't concentrated anywhere physical, this isn't the tactic. A business selling statewide online has no building worth fencing. Say so.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics
**ON-SCREEN:** (1) A map with a boundary being drawn tight around one building, then a second drawn too wide with a **green box** on the wasted area → *draw the fence*. (2) A timeline: enters the fence · served while there · still reachable for weeks after → *the tail*; then a phone, laptop, and tablet linked by a **green bracket**, sub-label *cross-device matching*. (3) A report showing visits to the client's location → *prove it*. Closes on text: *Draw the fence · Keep the tail · Prove the visit.*

**V1 — Guide:** Three moving parts.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: draw the fence.** We pick actual physical places and draw a boundary around each one. Any place is fair game — a competitor's lot, an event venue, an employer, a medical campus, a neighborhood. In the trade, any of those is called a **point of interest.**
**V1 — Guide:** The craft is tightness. Snug around one building reaches the people who went inside. Loose around a whole shopping district reaches everybody who drove past on the highway — impressions aimed at people who never got out of the car. Tight fences, chosen deliberately. That's the work.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: keep the tail.** This is the part that makes it a campaign instead of a stunt, and it's the thing to say out loud in a meeting.
**V1 — Guide:** When somebody enters the fence, they become eligible to see the ads while they're there. But they also stay reachable for **up to about thirty days after they leave.** So a person who walked a competitor's lot on Saturday keeps seeing your client's ad the following week, and the week after that, while they're still deciding.
**V1 — Guide:** That matters because almost nobody buys a big-ticket item in the parking lot. They shop, they go home, they think about it. The tail is what keeps the client in that conversation.
**V1 — Guide:** And here's the piece that makes the tail actually work: **cross-device matching.** The fence catches a *phone* — that's what people carry into a parking lot. But that same person does their real shopping at home, on a laptop or a tablet on the couch. Cross-device matching connects those devices to the same household, so the ad follows the *person* instead of just the phone.
**V1 — Guide:** So you fence them in a parking lot on Saturday and reach them on the big screen Sunday night. And it's household-level — we're connecting devices, not identifying people.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: prove the visit.** This is the part that separates geofencing from most advertising, and reps under-sell it badly.
**V1 — Guide:** Because we know where the phones were, we can also measure whether people who saw the ad later showed up at the client's own location. That's called **offline visit attribution** — it's an optional add-on, and it's about the closest that advertising gets to counting a walk-in.
**V1 — Guide:** Then the usual housekeeping: a daily schedule, conversion tracking, and weekly refinement — dropping fences that aren't producing and weighting the ones that are.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and definition animate in as text, with green boxes on the map boundaries and the visit report.

**V1 — Guide:** Five terms and you're fluent.
**V1 — Guide:** **Geofence** — a virtual boundary drawn around a real physical place. Phones that cross into it become part of the audience.
**V1 — Guide:** **Point of interest** — any location worth fencing. A competitor, a venue, an employer, a campus, a neighborhood.
**V1 — Guide:** **Conquesting** — fencing a competitor's location specifically. Same technology, different intent, and it's the phrase that gets an owner's attention.
**V1 — Guide:** **Geo-retargeting** — continuing to serve ads to somebody after they've left the fenced area, for up to about thirty days. The tail.
**V1 — Guide:** **Offline visit attribution** — measuring how many people who saw the ad later showed up at the client's physical location. Optional, and remarkable when a client wants proof.

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature list animates in. Four benefit lines: *Reach people at peak intent · Borrow a crowd · Stay with them for weeks · Measure actual visits.* Payoff: a real map with a tight boundary around a medical office, beside a real ad on a phone — plus a real photo of a physical therapy clinic. Small tag: *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** What's in it? Fence locations chosen with the client. Ads served inside the fence, plus retargeting for up to about thirty days after they leave. Cross-device matching across all their screens. A daily schedule. Conversion tracking. Optional offline visit measurement. Weekly refinement of which fences stay. And reporting.
**V1 — Guide:** What does the owner get? They **reach people at peak intent** — physically shopping, right now. They **borrow a crowd** they could never afford to buy a whole market to reach. They **stay with those people for weeks** while the decision actually gets made. And they can **measure real visits**, not just clicks.
**V1 — Guide:** So picture a small physical therapy clinic. They'd always relied on doctors sending patients over — completely dependent on whoever happened to mention them.
**V1 — Guide:** After: tight fences around the orthopedic offices and the hospital's outpatient entrance. Anybody who walked out of those buildings started seeing the clinic's ad — that week, and for the next few weeks, which is exactly when somebody's actually scheduling therapy.
**V2 — Scene:** *"I'd seen your name a few times, so when they gave me the list, I picked you."*
**V1 — Guide:** — and that's what this does. It doesn't replace the referral. It makes sure the client is a name the patient already recognizes when the list comes out. *(Illustrative — not a promise of specific numbers.)*

### SEGMENT 7 — Recap + Quiz Bridge
**ON-SCREEN:** Recap text — 1: draw the fence · 2: keep the tail · 3: prove the visit. *Quiz →* button slides up.

**V1 — Guide:** Three parts. One: draw the fence — real places, drawn tight, chosen on purpose. Two: keep the tail — they stay reachable for up to about thirty days after they leave, and cross-device matching means you reach them on the laptop at home, not just the phone that walked the lot. Three: prove the visit — optional offline measurement that counts people who actually showed up.
**V1 — Guide:** And the line to carry in: **where somebody has been tells you what they need.** No guessing required. Also remember it's bought on a cost-per-thousand impression basis, same as the rest of display.
**V1 — Guide:** That's geofence targeting. Quick ten-question check. No pressure — you already know this.

## SECTION D — AE Field Notes *(not narrated)*

**How the advertiser pays.** Bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis**, same as the rest of the display family — paying for the ad to be served, not clicked. Offline visit measurement is an optional add-on. The impression arithmetic is taught once, in the Targeted Display module; here it's a one-line statement.

**How it's sold.** Usually an add-on to a display or search program, but it's one of the few tactics that can lead a conversation — because the pitch is concrete. Pull up a map in the meeting and ask, "if you could advertise to everybody who walks into that building, would you?" Owners answer that question immediately.

**Bundles with:**
- **Targeted display** — same machinery, same buying basis. Frequently one campaign with several audience types.
- **SEM** — the fence builds familiarity; search captures them when they finally look.
- **Reputation management** — a fenced prospect who searches the client's name will land on their reviews. A weak star rating undoes the fence.
- **Website / landing pages** — the click needs somewhere useful to land.

**Objections and responses:**
- *"Isn't this creepy?"* → "Nobody gets a name, a number, or an identity. It's a device that entered an area, and we cap how often the ad shows. Most people find useful local advertising convenient — the research says so."
- *"Can you really target my competitor?"* → Yes, and say what it is honestly: an introduction at a good moment, not a magic wand. Never promise you'll take their customers.
- *"They'll be gone by the time the ad runs."* → That's what the tail is for — up to about thirty days after they leave, which is when big decisions actually get made.
- *"How do I know it worked?"* → This is the tactic with the best answer to that question. Optional offline visit measurement is the closest advertising gets to counting a walk-in.

**Cross-device matching, in one line for a client.** "We catch the phone in the parking lot and reach the same household on the laptop at home." That's the honest version — it's device-to-household linking, not identifying individuals, which is the same distinction to hold everywhere in this tactic.

**Lines for the room:**
- *"Where somebody has been tells you what they need."*
- *"It's handing out flyers in a parking lot — except you don't have to stand there, and you can keep reaching them for weeks after they drive off."*
- *"If you can name the building your customers walk out of, we can advertise to them."*
- *"We catch the phone in the parking lot and reach the same household on the laptop at home."*
- *"A tournament weekend brings four thousand of the right people into town. Let's talk to those four thousand instead of the whole county."*

**Retention note.** Same click-column risk as all display — pre-frame that this is an impression product measured on visits and leads. Where possible, sell the offline visit measurement, because it's the strongest retention tool in the display family: it gives the client a number that isn't a click.

**Writer's notes:**
- **Statistics sources:** roughly two-thirds of consumers more likely to visit a store after a relevant location-based offer, and roughly half reporting they have done so, appear across 2026 geofencing and location-based marketing compilations (WiFiTalents; OmniFunnel location-based marketing analysis). The ~80% preference for locally tailored advertising appears in PPC/location benchmark compilations. **All are survey findings, not audited measurements — attribute as "research shows" and re-verify annually.** Chosen because all three prove one idea: *location is intent.*
- **Deliberately excluded:** the CPM figures, the additional offline-visit CPM, the click-through estimate, the ad-set design fee, the monthly minimum, and the contract term. Also excluded: geofencing click-through comparisons and ROI multiples that circulate widely — both are ad-performance metrics and several trace to vendor marketing.
- **The thirty-day window is kept** because it's a product capability, not a billing term, and it's the strongest differentiator in the tactic. Confirm the current window before recording in case the platform has changed it.
- **Compliance note:** the script deliberately avoids any claim that individuals are identified. Reps should say "a device entered an area," never "we know who went there." That distinction matters if a client or a consumer ever asks.
- **Payoff vertical (physical therapy clinic)** is new. Updated list: diner, roofer, flooring co., hearing clinic, insurance agency, storage co., garden center, medical clinic, auto repair shop, pest control co., RV dealership, used-car lot, **physical therapy clinic**.
