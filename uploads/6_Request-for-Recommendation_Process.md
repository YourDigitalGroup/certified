# Request for Recommendation — Process Script
**Pillar:** Process · Block 8 of 11
**Audience:** Account executives and agency staff — internal
**Format:** Motion graphics over real screen capture · AI voice read (two voices) · animated text · green callout boxes
**Runtime:** 1830 counted spoken words ≈ **12:12** at 150 wpm
**Spine — the one sentence somebody must repeat cold:** *Three things make it mandatory. Everything else, ask anyway.*

---

## PRODUCTION NOTES — READ BEFORE RECORDING

**Two voices.** **V1 — Guide** carries all teaching. **V2 — Rep** asks what the viewer is thinking. Distinctly different ElevenLabs voice.

**Written for an AI read.** Short sentences. No semicolons, no parentheticals.

**The humour is recognition, not jokes.** Nothing depends on a pause or a dry delivery.

**⚠️ This block is a decision, not a procedure.** Every other block in the pillar teaches a sequence. This one teaches a choice: build the plan yourself, or ask our strategy team to build it. The three mandatory triggers are the only part that is not a judgment call, so state them flatly.

**Tone.** Asking for help must never sound like admitting weakness. Frame it as using a resource that is already paid for and sitting there. **Reps who feel judged for asking will stop asking.**

**No illustrations, no icon cards, no mascots.**

## SEGMENT 1 — WHERE THIS FITS

**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of a rep at a desk with notes and a laptop. Then the eleven-block pillar with a **green box** on block eight. Then two paths animate as text: *pitch it yourself* and *request a recommendation*.

**V2 — Rep:** *"I think I already know what they need. Do I have to send this in?"*

**V1 — Guide:** No. And that is genuinely the whole block — working out when you should anyway.

**V1 — Guide:** You have done the prospect research. You have had the conversation. You have run the audit. You are now sitting on more information about that business than they have probably ever written down about themselves.

**V1 — Guide:** So there are **two ways forward**, and both are legitimate. You build the recommendation and pitch it yourself. Or you send everything to our strategy team and they build it for you.

**V1 — Guide:** That is what a **request for recommendation** is. An RFR. You hand over what you learned, and people who do this all day come back with a plan built for that specific business.

**V1 — Guide:** But before any of that, there are **two requirements**, and neither is negotiable.

**V1 — Guide:** **One. A completed needs analysis.** Not a rough one, not a summary. Completed.

**V1 — Guide:** **Two. A budget the client gave you.** Monthly or annual, either is fine — but it has to be a number that came out of their mouth during that needs analysis conversation.

**V2 — Rep:** *"What if they would not tell me?"*

**V1 — Guide:** Then you do not have what you need yet, and the answer is to go back and ask again rather than send this in hopefully. We will not build a recommendation without a budget, and we will not build one without a needs analysis.

**V1 — Guide:** Look at the name of the thing you submit on the resource site — **client needs analysis, completed, and request for recommendation.** One item, both halves. That requirement is built into the name because it is built into the process.

**V1 — Guide:** And the line for the block: **three things make it mandatory. Everything else, ask anyway.** But nothing happens at all without a needs analysis and a budget.

## SEGMENT 2 — WHY THIS STEP EXISTS

**ON-SCREEN:** Two columns build as text — *assembled from memory* versus *built by the strategy team* — with a **green box** on the second. Then a real Trello card in the recommendations column.

**V1 — Guide:** Two reasons, and one clarification that saves confusion later.

**V1 — Guide:** **One. It is expertise you already have access to.** Our strategy team looks at these businesses all day, across dozens of markets. They know which combinations work for a roofer in a town of nine thousand, because they have built it forty times.

**V1 — Guide:** You are not asking a favour and you are not being tested. This is a resource sitting there, already included, and the reps who use it most are generally the ones selling most.

**V1 — Guide:** **Two. A generic plan gets politely declined.** You did all that work — the research, the conversation, the audit — precisely so the recommendation would fit. A plan that could have been handed to any business in town wastes the thing that made you credible.

**V1 — Guide:** And what happens if you skip it when you should not have? You present something that does not quite fit, they say they want to think about it, and the real problem is invisible. Nobody tells you the mix was wrong. They just do not sign.

**V1 — Guide:** Which is also the reason for the needs analysis requirement. Ask our team to build a plan for a business nobody has actually spoken to, and they are guessing — at the profit centers, at the audience, at what the owner is even trying to do. **A recommendation built on guesses is exactly the generic plan you were trying to avoid.**

**V1 — Guide:** So the requirement is not paperwork. It is the only thing that makes a tailored recommendation possible.

**V1 — Guide:** And the budget requirement is the same argument, sharper. A plan is a set of choices about where money goes. **With no number, there are no choices to make** — our team either builds something enormous that gets refused, or something small that leaves opportunity on the table. Either way you have wasted the meeting you worked for.

**V1 — Guide:** Monthly or annual is genuinely up to the client. Some owners think in months, some think in a yearly figure. **What matters is that it came from them, not from you.**

**V1 — Guide:** Now the clarification, because some of you will have heard a similar term. **Elsewhere in this industry, an RFP — request for proposal — usually means a client asking several vendors to bid.** That is not this. **Here, you are the one asking, and you are asking your own team.** Same three letters, opposite direction.

## SEGMENT 3 — THE WALKTHROUGH

**ON-SCREEN:** Four stages animate as text, then each with real screen capture.

**V1 — Guide:** Four stages. Decide whether you need one, submit it, know what happens next, then handle what comes back.

### One — decide

**ON-SCREEN:** Three mandatory triggers appear as animated text with a **green box** on each.

**V1 — Guide:** First, the two gates. **Has the needs analysis been done, and do you have a budget from the client?**

**V1 — Guide:** If either answer is no, there is nothing to decide yet. Go and get it. A request missing either one comes straight back to you, and you have lost two days for nothing.

**V1 — Guide:** Assuming it has, **three things make an RFR mandatory.** No judgment call, no exceptions.

**V1 — Guide:** **An e-commerce site.** **A custom website.** **Or a fully custom campaign.**

**V1 — Guide:** If any of those three are in play, it goes to the strategy team. Those builds have moving parts that need scoping properly, and a promise made in a meeting without that scoping is a promise somebody has to break later.

**V1 — Guide:** Everything else is your call. And for something straightforward — a client who wants email marketing and nothing else — you may genuinely not need one.

**V2 — Rep:** *"So when would I send one in if it is not required?"*

**V1 — Guide:** Most of the time, honestly. Two reasons. It is a second opinion on a plan you are about to put your name on. And you will learn something from every one that comes back — after ten of them you will be building better recommendations yourself.

**V1 — Guide:** The reps who treat this as optional-but-usually-yes get further than the ones who treat it as a last resort.

### Two — submit it

**ON-SCREEN:** Green box on the submission item in step two of the resource site, then on each field as it is named.

**V1 — Guide:** It lives in **step two** of your resource site, under the completed needs analysis and request for recommendation. Same place you submit the CNA, because they go in together.

**V1 — Guide:** You can either **upload your completed needs analysis** or type the information in. Then the business name, your contact details, the **digital budget**, the date you did the needs analysis, and the date you are presenting back.

**V1 — Guide:** Two things to get right here.

**V1 — Guide:** **The budget field is required, and it must be the client's number.** Not a guess, not a range you assumed, and not left blank. Monthly or annual, whichever way they think about it.

**V1 — Guide:** And to be completely clear, because people try this: putting *to be confirmed* in that field does not work either. It comes back. If you do not have the number, the next step is a phone call to your client, not a submission.

**V1 — Guide:** **And put the real presentation date in.** That date is what our team schedules around, and it is why the next part matters.

### Three — what happens next

**ON-SCREEN:** Green box on the recommendations column in Trello, then a card appearing with a comment thread.

**V1 — Guide:** Your submission goes to the strategy team and a card appears on your Trello board in the **recommendations** column. It stays there until the deal is sold or put on hold, so you always know what is live.

**V1 — Guide:** The strategists meet **every day** to work through pending requests, so nothing sits in a queue for a week. But **allow at least two business days**, and more if you can. A rush job is a worse plan.

**V2 — Rep:** *"What if they need something from me?"*

**V1 — Guide:** They will ask on the card. That is one of the reasons everything lives in Trello. And it works both ways — if you remember something after you submitted, add it to the card yourself. More context makes a better plan.

### Four — handle what comes back

**ON-SCREEN:** Green box on a completed recommendation, then on a comment being added to the card.

**V1 — Guide:** Now the part that nobody usually explains, and it matters.

**V1 — Guide:** **A recommendation is a recommendation. It is not an instruction.**

**V1 — Guide:** So if the budget changed since you submitted, or you learned something new, or there is something on there you are genuinely not comfortable presenting — **say so on the card.** Ask for the change.

**V2 — Rep:** *"Does that look like I am second-guessing them?"*

**V1 — Guide:** It looks like you know your client, which you do. You were in the room and they were not. Our strategists would far rather adjust a plan than have you walk into a meeting presenting something you do not believe in — because a client can hear that, every time.

**V1 — Guide:** Once you and the plan agree, you build your proposal and go present it.

## SEGMENT 5 — WHERE THIS GOES WRONG

**ON-SCREEN:** Each mistake appears as text beside the real screen element it relates to.

**V1 — Guide:** Six things, and the first one stops the process dead.

**V1 — Guide:** **One. Submitting without a completed needs analysis, or without a client-provided budget.** Either one stops it. It comes back to you, and you have burned days you did not need to burn. Both of those come out of the same conversation, so get both while you are in the room.

**V1 — Guide:** **Two. Skipping it on a custom build.** E-commerce, a custom website, or a fully custom campaign. Those three are not optional, and going it alone on one of them usually ends with a commitment somebody else has to unwind.

**V1 — Guide:** **Three. Putting your own number in the budget field.** The plan gets built around whatever is in there. Invent it and you get a plan for an imaginary business — and you will be the one presenting it.

**V1 — Guide:** **Four. Leaving fields blank.** Every gap is something our team has to either ask about, which costs a day, or assume, which costs more.

**V1 — Guide:** **Five. Submitting the day before you present.** Strategists work through requests daily, but two business days is the minimum and it is a minimum for a reason. If your meeting is Thursday, do not send this Wednesday afternoon.

**V1 — Guide:** **And six. Presenting a plan you do not believe in.** If something looks wrong to you, it will sound wrong when you say it. Ask for the change on the card first.

**V1 — Guide:** And the handoff. Once you and the recommendation agree, you build the proposal and present it. When they say yes, that becomes an insertion order — which is the next block, and the moment it all becomes real.

No client-provided budget, no recommendation.** Then decide — and remember the three that make it mandatory. Submit it in step two with your needs analysis, with a real budget and a real presentation date. Watch the card in the recommendations column and allow two business days at minimum. **V1 — Guide:** And the lines to keep. **No needs analysis and no budget means no recommendation.** And **three things make it mandatory — everything else, ask anyway.**

**V1 — Guide:** One last thing worth saying plainly. Using this is not a sign you do not know your job. It is a sign you are using everything available to you, which is a different thing entirely.

## TRAINER NOTES *(not narrated)*

**Where this sits.** Block 8 of 11. **Conditional, not sequential** — it is the fork after the needs analysis and audit. Feeds the proposal, then the insertion order.

**⚠️ Two hard prerequisites. Both are absolute.**
1. **A completed CNA.** Not partial, not summarised.
2. **A budget the client provided** — monthly or annual, captured during that CNA conversation. **Not the rep's estimate, not a range, not "to be confirmed."**

Both come out of the same meeting, which is the practical instruction: get both while you are in the room.

**Why the budget gate exists:** a plan is a set of choices about where money goes. With no number there are no choices — the team builds something too big and it gets refused, or too small and it leaves opportunity behind. Either outcome wastes the meeting the rep worked for.

**⚠️ Note for anyone revising this script:** an earlier draft told reps to mark the budget as unknown if they did not have it. **That is wrong and has been corrected.** There is no "unknown" path.

**On the CNA prerequisite specifically:** The strategy team will not build a plan for a business nobody has spoken to. **This is stated four times in the script** — as a gate in Segment 1, as a reason in Segment 2, as the first check in the walkthrough, and as the first mistake. It is also visible in the resource site item name: *Client Needs Analysis (Completed) & Request for Recommendation.*

**Why it is worth this much emphasis.** A rep under time pressure will try to short-cut straight to the RFR, because asking somebody else to build the plan feels faster than booking the conversation. It is not — the request bounces and they lose days. **Ask about this in the weekly meeting: has the CNA been done on everything you have submitted?**

**The three mandatory triggers, verbatim:** an **e-commerce site**, a **custom website**, or a **fully custom campaign.** Everything else is the AE's judgment, with a strong recommendation to submit anyway.

**⚠️ Tone is the risk in this block.** If asking for a recommendation ever feels like admitting you cannot do your job, reps will stop doing it and the quality of what they pitch will drop invisibly. The script frames it as a resource already included and notes that the heaviest users tend to sell the most. **Reinforce that in the weekly meeting — ask how many RFRs somebody has submitted, positively.**

**The "handle what comes back" stage was missing from the original script and matters.** A rep who feels locked into a plan they do not believe in will present it badly, and the client will hear it. **The rule: a recommendation is a recommendation, not an instruction. Ask for changes on the card.**

**The mistakes, with the symptom somebody would notice:**
| Mistake | What it looks like when it goes wrong |
|---|---|
| Submitting with no CNA or no budget | Request bounces; days lost; rep concludes the process is slow |
| Entering their own budget estimate | Plan built for an imaginary business — and the rep has to present it |
| Skipping it on a custom build | A commitment made in a meeting that scoping cannot support |
| Guessing at the budget | A plan built for a business that does not exist |
| Leaving fields blank | A day lost to questions, or assumptions baked into the plan |
| Submitting the day before | Rushed plan, or no plan in time for the meeting |
| Presenting a plan they doubt | Client hears the hesitation and declines |

**Operational facts:** strategists review pending requests **daily** · allow **two business days minimum** · the card lives in the **recommendations** column until the deal is sold or put on hold · questions from the team appear on the card, and reps can add context there too.

**Suggested work product for this block.** One submitted RFR on a real opportunity, with the budget field either accurate or honestly marked unknown. Review what came back at the weekly account manager meeting — that review is where the learning actually happens.
