# Addressable Targeting Ads — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Targeted Digital Marketing (Room #14d in the Digital House)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime:** 1284 counted spoken words ≈ **8:34** at 150 wpm
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.
**Spine — the one sentence a trainee must repeat cold:** *You're not buying a person. You're buying an address — and everybody in it.*

## SECTION A — The Reusable Tactic Lesson Template
*(Identical across all modules. Only the content changes — the beats never do.)*

**Voice convention:** **V1 — Guide** carries the teaching. **V2 — Scene** delivers only in-world lines and the quiz prompts.

**On-screen kit:** animated text · real photos and product screenshots · green callout boxes. **No line illustrations, icon cards, mascots, or drawn metaphors.** Spoken analogies stay spoken.

## SECTION B — Addressable Targeting: Full Script

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of a residential street. Then a real map with **green boxes** on four specific houses and the ones between them left plain. Then the same ad shown on a phone, a tablet, and a laptop, with a **green bracket** linking all three.

**V2 — Scene:** *"I want to reach these forty streets. Not the whole county — these streets."*
**V1 — Guide:** Every media AE has heard some version of that. And for most of what we sell, the honest answer has been "close, but not exactly."
**V1 — Guide:** So, plainly: **addressable targeting** means advertising to specific homes, chosen by street address. You hand over a list of addresses — or we build one to your specifications — and the ads get served to the devices inside those houses. The house next door gets nothing.
**V1 — Guide:** Here's the analogy, and it's the one to use in a room: **this is direct mail, without the mail.** Same idea, same kind of list. It just arrives on their screens instead of in their mailbox — and unlike a postcard, it can show up more than once.
**V1 — Guide:** Now, we've got a few tactics in this family, so let's keep them clean. Geofencing targets devices that *go* somewhere. CRM targeting uses email addresses and phone numbers to reach specific *people.* This one targets *homes* — and everybody in them.
**V1 — Guide:** That's the sentence to remember: **you're not buying a person, you're buying an address, and everybody in it.** And like the rest of the display family, it's bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis.**

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of a couple looking at something together on a laptop at a kitchen table. Then a map showing a whole-market buy versus a selected-household buy, **green box** on the second. Then text: *nobody buys a furnace alone.*

**V1 — Guide:** So why should an advertiser do this? Three reasons.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: nobody buys a furnace alone.** This is the argument, and it's the one reps miss.
**V1 — Guide:** The average American household has more than two people in it, and for big-ticket decisions — a roof, a school, a vehicle, a remodel — consumer research is consistent that those choices get made jointly rather than by one person. There's even a field experiment showing that reaching *both* partners in a household outperforms reaching the same number of unrelated individuals, and the reason is lovely: when two people in a house both see it, they talk to each other about it.
**V1 — Guide:** So think about what one-device advertising does. You reach the husband on his phone. The wife never sees it. He mentions it at dinner, badly, and it dies there. Reaching the whole household means the conversation happens on its own.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: it's the least wasteful money in the catalog.** A market-wide buy pays to reach everybody — renters when you sell roofs, people with no kids when you sell schooling, the whole county when the client serves four neighborhoods. Here, they pay for the houses they chose. That's it.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: a client doesn't need a list to start.** This is the part reps forget to say and it opens a lot of doors. If a client has their own addresses, terrific, we use them. If they don't, we can build a list to specification — geography, household characteristics, whatever fits who they're trying to reach. Nobody gets turned away for not having a spreadsheet.

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For
**ON-SCREEN:** Three real photos, one at a time — a home exterior with new windows or siding, a school or daycare entrance, a service van in a specific neighborhood. Each type's name and reason type in beside it.

**V1 — Guide:** Three easy targets.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses selling something attached to a house.** Windows, siding, roofing, HVAC, solar, lawn care, pest control, security. The product goes on a *building*, so a building is the right unit to advertise to — and you can pick the neighborhoods and skip the apartments.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses whose customers are defined by who lives there.** Private schools, daycares, orthodontists, tutoring, youth programs. "Households with school-age children within fifteen minutes" is a list you can actually build, and it's a much better buy than everybody in the market.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses with a tight service area.** Anybody who only serves certain routes, certain townships, certain sides of town. If driving there costs them money, advertising there is worth avoiding.
**V1 — Guide:** And the honest flip side: if a client's customers are scattered everywhere and defined by nothing geographic, a household list won't help much. Also worth saying — this reaches homes, so a business selling to other businesses is a different conversation.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics
**ON-SCREEN:** (1) Two paths building as text — *their addresses* / *a list built to spec* → *pick the households*. (2) One house with a phone, tablet, and laptop inside it, linked by a **green bracket** → *everybody under the roof*. (3) A map with selected homes lit and the rest dark, then a schedule and report → *no waste, kept current*. Closes on text: *Pick the homes · Reach everyone in them · Waste almost nothing.*

**V1 — Guide:** Three moving parts.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: pick the households.** Two ways in, and knowing both makes you useful.
**V1 — Guide:** The first is the client's **own address list** — past customers, service records, whatever they've got. The second is a **curated list**, built to a specification: these zip codes, these neighborhoods, homes with these characteristics, households that look like this. The client describes who they want to reach and the list gets constructed around it.
**V1 — Guide:** And this is where a rep earns their keep, because the specification *is* the strategy. "Everybody in town" is not a specification. "Owner-occupied homes over thirty years old in these six neighborhoods" is.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: reach everybody under the roof, on every screen.** The ads go to the devices at that address — phones, tablets, laptops, all of them. This is **household-level targeting**, and it's a genuinely different thing from reaching one person.
**V1 — Guide:** Which matters for exactly the reason we covered: the people who decide together should see it together. And practically, it means consistent messaging — the same ad, the same offer, in front of everyone who's going to be part of that conversation.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: waste almost nothing, and keep the list current.** The efficiency argument here is the cleanest of any tactic we sell. Every impression is served into a house that was deliberately chosen. Nobody outside the list gets served, and the client isn't paying to reach them.
**V1 — Guide:** Then the housekeeping: cross-device delivery, a daily ad schedule, conversion tracking, and weekly refinement. And one thing specific to this tactic — addresses change. People move. A list built two years ago has drifted, so refreshing it periodically is part of keeping the campaign honest.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and definition animate in as text, with green boxes on the selected homes and the linked devices.

**V1 — Guide:** Four terms and you're fluent.
**V1 — Guide:** **Addressable targeting** — advertising to specific homes chosen by physical street address. The house is the target.
**V1 — Guide:** **Household-level targeting** — reaching every device inside that address rather than one individual. The unit is the roof, not the person.
**V1 — Guide:** **First-party address list** — addresses the client provides out of their own records. Theirs, collected by them.
**V1 — Guide:** **Curated list** — a list built to the client's specification when they don't have one of their own. Geography, household characteristics, whatever the target calls for.

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature list animates in. Four benefit lines: *Choose the exact homes · Reach every decision-maker · Almost no wasted spend · Works with or without their list.* Payoff: a real map with selected households lit up beside the same ad on three screens — plus a real photo of a school entrance. Small tag: *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** What's in it? A campaign built around physical addresses — either the client's own list or one curated to their specification. Ads served to all devices at each address. Cross-device delivery. Message consistency across every screen in the home. A daily ad schedule. Conversion tracking. Weekly refinement. And regular reporting.
**V1 — Guide:** What does the owner get? They **choose the exact homes** they want to reach. They **reach every decision-maker** under that roof instead of one of them. They spend with **almost no waste.** And it **works whether or not they have a list**, which removes the most common reason a client says no.
**V1 — Guide:** So picture a small private school. Enrollment's been flat, and they've been buying market-wide advertising for years — reaching retirees, college students, and every household in the county without a child in it.
**V1 — Guide:** After: a curated list of households with school-age children inside a reasonable drive. Same ad, running on the phone, the tablet, and the laptop in each of those homes. Both parents seeing it, in the months when families actually decide about next year.
**V2 — Scene:** *"We'd both been seeing your ad, and we finally just said — let's go look at it."*
**V1 — Guide:** — and notice the word *both.* That's the whole tactic. One of them seeing it is a maybe. Two of them seeing it is a conversation. *(Illustrative — not a promise of specific numbers.)*

### SEGMENT 7 — Recap + Quiz Bridge
**ON-SCREEN:** Recap text — 1: pick the homes · 2: reach everyone in them · 3: waste almost nothing. *Quiz →* button slides up.

**V1 — Guide:** Three parts. One: pick the households — their own address list, or one built to specification, and the specification is the strategy. Two: reach everybody under that roof on every screen, because the people who decide together should see it together. Three: waste almost nothing — and refresh the list, because people move.
**V1 — Guide:** And the two lines to carry in. **This is direct mail, without the mail.** And **you're not buying a person, you're buying an address, and everybody in it.**
**V1 — Guide:** That's addressable targeting. Quick ten-question check. No pressure — you already know this.

## SECTION D — AE Field Notes *(not narrated)*

**How the advertiser pays.** Bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis**, like the rest of the display family. Two supply paths exist — the client's own addresses, and a curated list — and they carry different rates. Reps name the model, not the rate. Impression arithmetic is taught once, in Targeted Display.

**How it's sold.** One of the strongest openers in the catalog because it answers a question AEs already hear: *"can you just hit these neighborhoods?"* For years the answer was approximate. Now it's exact. Lead with the map — ask the client to point at where their best customers live, then tell them that's the buy.

**The direct-mail conversation.** Any client who has run direct mail already understands this product; they just don't know it exists. "Same list you mailed to, except it shows up on their screens and you can run it more than once" closes that gap in one sentence. Worth asking every prospect whether they've ever done a mail drop.

**Bundles with:**
- **Geofence and event targeting** — same buying basis and machinery. Fence where they go, address where they live.
- **CRM targeting** — the natural pair: reach known customers by email and phone, reach chosen households by address. Same client, two audiences.
- **Targeted display** — addressable folds in as one audience within a broader program.
- **Website / landing pages** — a neighborhood- or offer-specific landing page strengthens this considerably.

**Objections and responses:**
- *"I don't have a list of addresses."* → "You don't need one. Tell me who you're trying to reach and we'll build it — neighborhoods, home characteristics, households with kids, whatever fits."
- *"Isn't this the same as the geofencing you sold me?"* → "Geofencing reaches devices that *go* somewhere. This reaches homes where people *live*, whether they go anywhere or not."
- *"How do you know who lives there?"* → Careful and accurate: we're targeting an address, and the ad is served to devices at that address. Nobody's name or personal information is used or displayed. Don't go further than that.
- *"Won't I be wasting money on the whole household?"* → That's the feature. The people who decide together should see it together — one person seeing an ad is a maybe, two is a conversation.

**Lines for the room:**
- *"This is direct mail, without the mail."*
- *"You're not buying a person. You're buying an address, and everybody in it."*
- *"Point at where your best customers live. That's the buy."*
- *"Nobody buys a furnace alone."*
- *"One of them seeing it is a maybe. Two of them seeing it is a conversation."*

**Retention note.** Same click-column risk as all display. Specific to this tactic: **list decay.** Households move, and a stale list quietly wastes money while looking fine in a report. Build the refresh into the relationship at the first meeting — it's both good practice and a natural recurring conversation.

**Writer's notes:**
- **Evidence is thinner on this tactic than most — read this before recording.** The three supports used are: (1) average US household size being above two people, which is Census-level and safe; (2) consumer-behavior research that major household purchases are typically decided jointly — the literature is solid but much of it is academic and older, and the most-cited survey figure on joint decision-making dates to 2008, which is why the script states it qualitatively rather than quoting a percentage; and (3) a peer-reviewed field experiment finding that exposing ads to both partners in a household outperformed exposing the same number of unrelated individuals, because it prompted them to discuss it. That study reported a conversion-rate multiple, which is deliberately **not** quoted, per the standing rule against ad-performance metrics — the script describes the finding without the number. **If a stronger, current, citable source on joint household decision-making surfaces, swap it in; this is the weakest evidence base in the 201 block.**
- **Deliberately excluded:** both CPM figures, the click-through estimate, the ad-set design fee, the monthly minimum, and the contract term.
- **Compliance note:** reps target *addresses*, not people. The language is "ads are served to devices at that address," never "we know who lives there." Same discipline as geofencing and CRM. This matters more here than elsewhere because the target is somebody's home.
- **Confirm before recording:** what household characteristics can actually be specified on a curated list. The script says "geography, household characteristics, whatever the target calls for," which is deliberately general. If the available selects are narrower than that implies, tighten the wording so reps don't promise a specification we can't build.
- **Payoff vertical (private school)** is new. Updated list: diner, roofer, flooring co., hearing clinic, insurance agency, storage co., garden center, medical clinic, auto repair shop, pest control co., RV dealership, used-car lot, physical therapy clinic, wedding/event venue, veterinary clinic, **private school**.
