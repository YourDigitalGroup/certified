# Streaming TV Advertising (OTT/CTV) — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Targeted Digital Marketing (Room #23 in the Digital House — the primary streaming TV product)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime:** 1377 counted spoken words ≈ **9:11** at 150 wpm
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.
**Spine — the one sentence a trainee must repeat cold:** *The shows they already watch — aimed at the zip codes you actually serve.*

## SECTION A — The Reusable Tactic Lesson Template
*(Identical across all modules. Only the content changes — the beats never do.)*

**Voice convention:** **V1 — Guide** carries the teaching. **V2 — Scene** delivers only in-world lines and the quiz prompts.

**On-screen kit:** animated text · real photos and product screenshots · green callout boxes. **No line illustrations, icon cards, mascots, or drawn metaphors.** Spoken analogies stay spoken.

| # | Segment | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Hook + What It Is** | Relatable moment → one-sentence definition |
| 2 | **Why It Matters** | Why an advertiser should do this + the evidence |
| 3 | **Who It's Great For** | 3 categories, one reason each + who it's wrong for |
| 4 | **How It Works — 3 Core Mechanics** | Exactly 3, roughly 4 beats each |
| 5 | **Common Terms** | Four to five terms maximum |
| 6 | **Features, Benefits & The Payoff** | Included → benefits → one payoff story |
| 7 | **Recap + Quiz Bridge** | Restate the 3 mechanics, warm handoff |

**Quiz blueprint:** Q1–3 the three mechanics · Q4–6 terms · Q7 fit · Q8 scenario · Q9–10 features/benefits. Vary the correct letter across A–D.

## SECTION B — Streaming TV: Full Script

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of a family on a couch watching a smart TV. Then a real streaming service ad break with a local business's spot, **green box** on a QR code in the corner. Then a wall of real network and service logos filling the screen.

**V2 — Scene:** *"Put on that show we were watching."*
**V1 — Guide:** That's a family about to watch television. Same couch, same living room, same hour of the evening people have always watched television. The only thing that changed is where it's coming from.
**V1 — Guide:** So, plainly: **streaming TV advertising** — you'll also hear it called **OTT** and **CTV** — is running video ads inside streaming services. The big ones people actually use, and the networks whose shows they're actually watching. On the television, and on phones and tablets too.
**V1 — Guide:** Two acronyms you'll hear. **OTT** is over-the-top: video delivered over the internet instead of through a cable box. **CTV** is connected TV — the actual smart television it plays on. When a client says "streaming ads," this is what they mean.
**V1 — Guide:** It's bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis**, and one impression here means the ad played on one screen during somebody's show. Not a banner that loaded — a video that ran during programming.
**V1 — Guide:** And two things make it different from any television buy that came before. The ads generally **can't be skipped.** And it can be aimed — by what people actually watch, and by geography right down to the zip code. Which is the line to carry: **the shows they already watch, aimed at the zip codes you actually serve.**

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters
**ON-SCREEN:** Three statistics build as large animated numbers. Then a real map with specific zip codes highlighted in green beside a broadcast coverage map that spills far past them. Then text: *the same show, without paying for three counties you don't serve.*

**V1 — Guide:** So why should an advertiser do this? Three reasons.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: this is where television went.** Nielsen measures how Americans watch, and streaming is now pushing half of all TV viewing — it passed broadcast and cable combined. Cable's down around a fifth. Broadcast's around a fifth. And better than nine in ten American households have a connected TV device.
**V1 — Guide:** And here's the number that answers the objection you'll hear every single time — "everybody pays to skip the ads." Roughly three-quarters of American homes have at least one ad-supported streaming service, and ad-supported viewing is still around three-quarters of all TV watching. The ads moved along with the audience.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: it's television without the waste.** This is the argument for anybody who's ever bought broadcast or cable. A signal doesn't stop at a service boundary. If a client serves four towns and the station covers twelve counties, they've been paying for eight counties of people who will never drive that far.
**V1 — Guide:** This gets aimed by zip code. Same show, same screen, same evening — just not the three counties they can't serve.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: it can't be skipped.** On these ad-supported services the commercial runs. That's the trade a viewer makes for a cheaper subscription, and it means the spot a client paid to produce actually gets watched rather than clicked past.
**V1 — Guide:** One honest note on budget. Television inventory costs considerably more per thousand than a banner on a website — that's what a big screen during real programming is worth. So set expectations early: this is a presence tactic that needs a real budget, not a small add-on line.

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For
**ON-SCREEN:** Three real photos, one at a time — a furniture showroom, a hospital or professional office, a business with a tight multi-town service area. Each type's name and reason type in beside it.

**V1 — Guide:** Three easy targets.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses selling something a household decides together.** Furniture, vehicles, home improvement, vacations, appliances, schools. Television is the last shared screen — when the ad runs, everybody in the room sees it, and that's exactly who's making the decision.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses that need to look established.** Medical, financial, legal, anybody handling something important. There is no cheap seat on a television screen. A local business in the same commercial break as national brands borrows that seriousness.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses with a tight service area who've been overpaying for reach.** Anybody who's bought broadcast and quietly known that most of the signal lands where they can't serve. Zip code targeting is the fix, and they'll understand it immediately.
**V1 — Guide:** And the honest flip side: a small budget spread across television reaches almost nobody, so a client who can't fund it properly is better served elsewhere. And a business with no video and no budget to produce any isn't ready — production is available, but have that conversation first.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics
**ON-SCREEN:** (1) A wall of real service and network logos, **green box** sweeping across them → *where it runs*. (2) A smart TV recognizing on-screen content, then a Census-style demographic panel, then a zip code map → *how it's aimed*. (3) A TV screen with a **green box** on the QR code, then a phone scanning it, then a report → *how it's measured*. Closes on text: *Premium networks · Aimed three ways · Measured off the screen.*

**V1 — Guide:** Three moving parts.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: where it runs.** The ads go inside the streaming services people actually use, and against the networks whose programming they're actually watching — entertainment, news, sports, lifestyle, home improvement, the whole spread.
**V1 — Guide:** That matters more than it sounds. This isn't leftover inventory on a channel nobody's heard of. It's premium content, and the ad sits in the break of a show somebody chose to watch. On the big screen, and on phones and tablets as well.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: how it's aimed — and there are three ways.** This is the part worth slowing down on, because it's what separates this from every television buy your clients have made before.
**V1 — Guide:** **ACR data.** That stands for automatic content recognition, and it's the interesting one. Smart televisions can recognize what's actually on the screen — so the targeting can be built around what households genuinely watch, rather than what a survey says they probably watch.
**V1 — Guide:** **Census data**, for demographic targeting — the household characteristics of an area. And **audience segments**, the same behavioral targeting we use across the rest of the display family.
**V1 — Guide:** Then geography on top: **zip code level.** Not a market, not a signal footprint. The actual zip codes the client serves.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: how it's measured.** Television has never been able to answer "what did that do?" — and this is where that changes.
**V1 — Guide:** A **QR code** can sit on screen, so somebody points a phone at it and lands on the client's site from the couch. There's **conversion tracking** through a pixel on their website. And optionally, **offline visit tracking** — whether people who saw the ad actually turned up in person.
**V1 — Guide:** Then the management: a daily ad schedule, weekly refinement, and reporting. And video production if they need it.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and definition animate in as text, with green boxes on a smart TV, an ad break, and a zip code map.

**V1 — Guide:** Four terms and you're fluent.
**V1 — Guide:** **OTT and CTV** — over-the-top means the video comes over the internet instead of a cable box. Connected TV is the smart television it plays on. Both mean streaming ads, and clients use them interchangeably.
**V1 — Guide:** **ACR** — automatic content recognition. Smart TVs identifying what's actually on screen, which lets targeting be based on real viewing rather than estimated viewing. If you learn one new acronym today, make it this one.
**V1 — Guide:** **Non-skippable** — the ad runs; the viewer can't click past it. That's the trade for a cheaper or free streaming tier, and it's why the spot gets watched.
**V1 — Guide:** **Cord-cutter** — somebody who dropped cable or satellite and watches another way. These are the households a traditional TV buy no longer reaches.

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature list animates in. Four benefit lines: *Premium shows, local budget · Zip code precision · The spot actually gets watched · Measurable off the screen.* Payoff: a real furniture showroom photo beside a streaming ad with a QR code. Small tag: *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** What's in it? Video ads running across the major streaming services and networks. Targeting built on ACR data, Census data, or audience segments. Zip code level geographic targeting. A daily ad schedule. Conversion tracking through a pixel. Optional offline visit measurement. Weekly refinement. Regular reporting. And video production if they need it.
**V1 — Guide:** What does the owner get? **Premium programming on a local budget** — real shows, real networks. **Zip code precision**, so they stop paying for counties they can't serve. A spot that **actually gets watched**, because it can't be skipped. And it's **measurable off the screen**, which television has never been.
**V1 — Guide:** So picture a family furniture store. Two locations, forty years in business, competing against a big regional chain — and their television money has always gone to a station covering nine counties when they realistically serve four towns.
**V1 — Guide:** After: the same spot running during shows those households already watch, inside the zip codes they actually deliver to, with a QR code on screen linking to the current sale. Both people on the couch seeing it at once.
**V2 — Scene:** *"We were watching a show, saw your sectional, and my husband scanned the code right there."*
**V1 — Guide:** — and notice all three pieces. The right households. Both decision-makers in the room. And a way to act without getting off the couch. *(Illustrative — not a promise of specific numbers.)*

### SEGMENT 7 — Recap + Quiz Bridge
**ON-SCREEN:** Recap text — 1: premium networks · 2: aimed three ways · 3: measured off the screen. *Quiz →* button slides up.

**V1 — Guide:** Three parts. One: where it runs — the streaming services and networks people actually watch, on the big screen and on mobile. Two: how it's aimed — ACR data, Census data, or audience segments, plus zip code geography. Three: how it's measured — a QR code, conversion tracking, and optional offline visits.
**V1 — Guide:** And two things to carry in. **The shows they already watch, aimed at the zip codes you actually serve.** And when somebody says everybody skips the ads: three-quarters of American homes have an ad-supported streaming service, and on those tiers the ad can't be skipped.
**V1 — Guide:** That's streaming TV. Quick ten-question check. No pressure — you already know this.

## SECTION D — AE Field Notes *(not narrated)*

**How the advertiser pays.** Bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis**. One impression means the ad played on one screen during programming. Offline visit measurement is an optional add-on. Impression arithmetic is taught once, in Targeted Display.

**Where this sits in the video family — this is the primary streaming TV product.**
- **Streaming TV (this, Room 23):** the standard OTT/CTV buy — a produced spot running across the major streaming services and networks.
- **Hulu (Room 26):** sold separately and **not included in this buy.** If a client wants Hulu specifically, that's its own product.
- **Social OTT/CTV (Room 17):** the same streaming TV delivery, but the creative is **repurposed social video** kept in its authentic social look rather than a produced commercial. Same screen, different creative approach — sell it when the client has good social video and no commercial.
- **YouTube TV (Room 24):** the live television service specifically — local channels, national channels, live sports, breaking news.
- **YouTube Video Ads (Room 22):** the YouTube video platform, not television at all.
- **Programmatic video (Room 20):** video around online video on regular websites and apps.

If a client asks the difference between this and Room 17, the honest answer is the creative: *this one runs your commercial, that one runs your social video.*

**Budget reality.** Television inventory costs considerably more per thousand than web display. Say so early. **An underfunded streaming campaign is invisible and gets cancelled**, and that failure is entirely preventable at the first meeting.

**How it's sold.** For any current broadcast or cable advertiser, the opener writes itself: *"How much of that signal lands where you can't serve?"* Pull up a coverage map and their service area side by side. The waste is visible in about four seconds, and zip code targeting is the answer.

**Bundles with:**
- **Broadcast and cable television** — additive rather than competitive. Their spot follows the audience that left, without abandoning the audience that stayed.
- **Social OTT/CTV** — same delivery, different creative. Useful when a client has both a commercial and good social video.
- **YouTube TV** — for a client who also wants live news and sports. Separate line item.
- **Website / landing pages** — the QR code needs somewhere good to land, and a campaign-specific page is the natural attached sale.
- **Geofencing** — pair the offline visit measurement conversations; both answer "did anybody actually come in?"

**Objections and responses:**
- *"Everybody skips the ads now."* → "Three in four American homes have at least one ad-supported streaming service, ad-supported is still about three-quarters of all TV viewing, and on those tiers the ad can't be skipped."
- *"I already buy cable / broadcast."* → "Good — you believe in television. This is how you keep the part of that audience that cancelled, and stop paying for the counties you can't serve."
- *"How would I know it worked?"* → The QR code, the pixel, and optional offline visits. This is the most measurable television has ever been.
- *"I can't afford TV."* → Be honest about cost per thousand relative to display, and don't promise television on a display budget. If it won't work, recommend something else.
- *"How do you know what people watch?"* → ACR — smart televisions recognizing on-screen content. Nothing about a person's identity; it's household-level viewing data.

**Lines for the room:**
- *"The shows they already watch — aimed at the zip codes you actually serve."*
- *"How much of that signal lands where you can't serve?"*
- *"There's no cheap seat on a television screen."*
- *"Television is the last shared screen. One impression, everybody in the room."*
- *"For seventy years the problem with TV was that you couldn't click it."*

**Retention note.** Two risks. **Budget mismatch** — the biggest one, and preventable by being candid about cost per thousand upfront. And **measurement** — agree at the first meeting that this is judged on QR scans, site traffic, and store visits rather than a click column, because there is no click on a television.

**Writer's notes:**
- **Statistics sources:** streaming approaching half of all US TV viewing and surpassing broadcast plus cable combined is **Nielsen's The Gauge** (48.6% in May 2026, with cable at 20.4% and broadcast at 19.2%); the ad-supported share of roughly three-quarters of viewing is **Nielsen's Ad Supported Gauge**. Connected TV household penetration above nine in ten is reported across 2026 CTV compilations, and the figure that about three-quarters of US homes have at least one ad-supported streaming service comes from 2024–2026 industry reporting. **Nielsen is the strongest sourcing available here — cite it by name. Re-verify before recording; the Gauge publishes monthly.**
- **Deliberately excluded:** the completion-rate estimate on the sheet, the offline-visit add-on rate, the monthly minimum, the contract term, and the production quote. The completion figure was excluded per the standing rule on ad-performance metrics — the script makes the same point through "non-skippable," which is more useful to a rep anyway. Note this sheet lists no base CPM; the *relative* cost position is still described directionally, because a rep who doesn't know television costs more per thousand than display will sell it at a failing budget.
- **⚠️ ACR is worth verifying before recording.** Automatic content recognition is real and widely used in CTV targeting, but availability varies by platform and by television manufacturer, and privacy rules around it continue to evolve. The script describes it as household-level viewing data with no personal identity involved, which is the accurate and defensible framing. Confirm the current capability with the platform, and keep reps away from any claim that we know *who* is watching.
- **⚠️ Sales sheet discrepancy — needs fixing on the collateral.** Hulu is sold as a **separate product (Room 26)** and is **not** part of this buy. The current sales sheet for this product still lists Hulu among the example services. **The sheet needs updating**, because a rep holding it will name Hulu to a client and create a promise we can't deliver. This script deliberately never names individual services for that reason — it says "the major streaming services and networks" instead — but the sheet is what a client actually sees.
- **Payoff vertical (family furniture store)** is new. Updated list: diner, roofer, flooring co., hearing clinic, insurance agency, storage co., garden center, medical clinic, auto repair shop, pest control co., RV dealership, used-car lot, physical therapy clinic, wedding/event venue, veterinary clinic, private school, clothing boutique, brewery/taproom, home builder, paint store, coffee drive-thru, technical college, senior living community, farm equipment dealer, community credit union, **furniture store**.
