# Client Needs Analysis — Process Script
**Pillar:** Process · Block 6 of 11
**Audience:** Account executives and agency staff — internal
**Format:** Motion graphics over real screen capture and real photography · AI voice read (two voices) · animated text · green callout boxes
**Runtime:** 1764 counted spoken words ≈ **11:46** at 150 wpm
**Spine — the one sentence somebody must repeat cold:** *Know the form well enough that you never have to read it.*

---

## PRODUCTION NOTES — READ BEFORE RECORDING

**Two voices.** **V1 — Guide** carries all teaching. **V2** is either **the rep** asking what the viewer is thinking, or **the client** delivering a real in-world line. Distinctly different ElevenLabs voice.

**Written for an AI read.** Short sentences. No semicolons, no parentheticals.

**The humour is recognition, not jokes.** Nothing depends on a pause or a dry delivery.

**This block teaches a conversation, not a tool.** It is the only block in the pillar where the skill is interpersonal. Lean on real photography of two people across a desk rather than screen capture, and use screen capture only for the form itself and the submission step.

**The verbatim lines in Segment 3 are the most valuable content in this block.** They are the words for the questions reps are most reluctant to ask. Do not paraphrase them in production — a rep should be able to lift them exactly.

**No illustrations, no icon cards, no mascots.**

## SEGMENT 1 — WHERE THIS FITS

**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of two people across a desk, one holding a printed form and looking down at it. Then the eleven-block pillar with a **green box** on block six.

**V2 — Rep:** *"So do I just go through the questions?"*

**V1 — Guide:** Please do not. That is the one way to get this wrong.

**V1 — Guide:** The **client needs analysis** is a conversation with a business owner about their business. There is a form behind it, and the form is genuinely good. But the moment you start reading it aloud, you stop being a person they are talking to and become somebody processing them.

**V1 — Guide:** So here is the line for the whole block. **Know the form well enough that you never have to read it.**

**V1 — Guide:** And you are not arriving cold. You already ran the prospect research, so you know their site is slow, you know they show up for almost nothing, and you know who is beating them. You are walking in informed. This conversation is where you find out what to do about it.

**V1 — Guide:** One more thing before we start, and it takes the pressure off. **You are not selling in this meeting.** You are learning, and you are earning the right to come back with something specific. That is the entire job today.

## SEGMENT 2 — WHY THIS STEP EXISTS

**ON-SCREEN:** A flow builds as text — *CNA → profit centers → keywords → the audit* — with a **green box** travelling along it. Then the same flow with the CNA box empty and the rest greyed out.

**V1 — Guide:** Two reasons, and the second one is the one nobody tells you.

**V1 — Guide:** **One. You cannot recommend what you do not understand.** A recommendation built on a guess is a recommendation that gets politely declined. Every question on that form exists because somebody needed the answer to build a strategy that actually fit.

**V1 — Guide:** **Two, and this is the important one. The digital audit runs on this conversation.**

**V1 — Guide:** When you ask a business about their **profit centers** — the things that actually make them money — those answers become the **keywords** the audit measures against. That is how we find out whether they show up when somebody searches for the thing they most want to sell.

**V2 — Rep:** *"And if I leave that thin?"*

**V1 — Guide:** Then we guess the keywords. And an audit built on guessed keywords looks exactly as authoritative as a real one, right up until you put it in front of somebody and they say *"nobody searches for us that way."*

**V1 — Guide:** So a weak needs analysis does not just produce a weak recommendation. **It quietly corrupts the audit you are going to present.**

**V1 — Guide:** And there is a harder consequence for skipping it altogether. **Our strategy team will not build a recommendation without a completed needs analysis.** Not a partial one, not a summary — a completed one.

**V2 — Rep:** *"So I cannot skip ahead and just ask them for a plan?"*

**V1 — Guide:** You cannot. And that is not us being difficult. They would be guessing at the profit centers, the audience, and what the owner is actually trying to do — which produces exactly the generic plan nobody signs.

**V1 — Guide:** So this conversation is worth doing properly even when you are busy. Everything after it is waiting on it.

## SEGMENT 3 — THE WALKTHROUGH

**ON-SCREEN:** Four numbered stages animate as text, then each with real photography or screen capture.

**V1 — Guide:** Four stages. Prepare, open it properly, work through four areas of questions, then close and book the next meeting.

### One — prepare

**ON-SCREEN:** Green box on the blank CNA form in step one of the resource site, then on the MRR findings report.

**V1 — Guide:** The blank form lives in **step one** of your resource site. Print it or pull it up, and **read it before you go** — not in the meeting, before.

**V1 — Guide:** Bring your prospect research findings too. Not to lead with, just so you already know the landscape while they talk.

**V1 — Guide:** And know the form well enough to hold a conversation without it. That is the whole skill. Somebody who knows the questions can follow whatever the client wants to talk about and still come away with everything.

### Two — open it properly

**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of a relaxed conversation, no paperwork visible.

**V1 — Guide:** Build some rapport first. You know how to do this; it is the part of the job you have always been good at.

**V1 — Guide:** Then tell them exactly what is about to happen, because that removes all the awkwardness. Something like this.

**V1 — Guide:** *"I'm going to ask you a bunch of questions about your business today. I'm not selling you anything in this meeting. I want to understand how this works so I can go do some research, run an analysis on how you show up online, and come back with something built for you rather than something generic."*

**V1 — Guide:** That sentence does a lot of work. It tells them why you are asking, it promises a second meeting, and it takes selling off the table so they relax and actually answer.

### Three — the four areas

**ON-SCREEN:** Four labelled areas build as text with a **green box** moving between them.

**V1 — Guide:** The form covers four areas. Do not work through them in order if the conversation goes elsewhere. Just make sure you leave with all four.

**V1 — Guide:** **Area one, and do not treat this as the boring bit.** Two things here, and you cannot leave without either of them.

**V1 — Guide:** **The budget**, and **the date you are presenting back.** Get that date on the calendar before you leave — it gives our team a deadline and it gives you a reason to return.

**V1 — Guide:** And the budget is not optional. **Our strategy team will not build a recommendation without a number the client gave you.** Monthly or annual, either is fine. But it has to come from them.

**V2 — Rep:** *"That is the question I hate asking."*

**V1 — Guide:** Everybody does at first. So do not ask for a number. Ask for a **format**, then a range.

**V1 — Guide:** *"Do you think about advertising as a monthly number or a yearly one?"*

**V1 — Guide:** Almost nobody refuses that, because you are not asking how much — you are asking how they think. Then follow it with: *"and roughly where does that land?"*

**V1 — Guide:** And if you want to make it easy on both of you, give them the reason. *"I ask because I'm not going to come back with a plan you can't afford. I'd rather build something that fits than waste your time."*

**V1 — Guide:** That lands well every time, because it is true.

**V1 — Guide:** **Area two, who they are.** Their background, who their customer actually is, what makes them different from the business down the road. And the best question on the entire form, which is: **what do people get wrong about you?**

**V2 — Client:** *"Everybody assumes we only do commercial. Half our work is residential."*

**V1 — Guide:** There it is. That is a positioning problem, a marketing problem, and a whole campaign, and you got it out of one question. Ask it every time.

**V1 — Guide:** **Area three, the money.** Their profit centers, roughly what share of sales each one brings, and where they want to grow. This is the part reps skip, so let us deal with it.

### The questions people are reluctant to ask

**ON-SCREEN:** The verbatim lines appear as animated text, one at a time, with a **green box** around each.

**V1 — Guide:** These feel nosy. They are not, and there are words that make them land easily.

**V1 — Guide:** For profit centers, try this. *"Can I ask you something that sounds like an odd question? Of everything you sell, what actually makes you the most money? Not what you sell the most of — what you make the most on."*

**V1 — Guide:** Two things happen. They usually smile, because most people have never been asked to separate volume from margin. And you get a much better answer than "we do a bit of everything."

**V1 — Guide:** For the share of sales, defuse it upfront. *"Rough thirds is fine. I'm not after your books — I just need to know where to point the advertising."*

**V1 — Guide:** That is the whole trick. **You are not asking for financials. You are asking where to aim.** Say that and almost nobody hesitates.

**V1 — Guide:** **Area four, what they are doing now.** Their website, social media, email, listings, reviews, and anything already running. And ask for something specific: *"If you've got reports from whoever's running that, I'd love to see them."*

**V1 — Guide:** People hand those over more often than you would think. And a competitor's report tells you what is working, what is not, and exactly where you can do better.

### Four — close and submit

**ON-SCREEN:** Green box on the completed form, then on the submission item in step two of the resource site, then on a Trello card appearing.

**V1 — Guide:** Confirm the date you are presenting back. Thank them. Leave.

**V1 — Guide:** Then submit it. The completed form goes back in through **step two** of the resource site, together with your request for a recommendation. Same document you picked up in step one — you collect it in step one and return it in step two.

**V1 — Guide:** From there our strategy team builds the recommendation, and you will see the card appear on your Trello board. That is where any questions from them will show up.

## SEGMENT 5 — WHERE THIS GOES WRONG

**ON-SCREEN:** Each mistake appears as text alongside real photography of the moment it describes.

**V1 — Guide:** Six things.

**V1 — Guide:** **One. Reading the form out loud.** It turns a conversation into an interview, and people give short answers to interviews. Learn the questions instead.

**V1 — Guide:** **Two. Selling.** The temptation is enormous, especially when they describe a problem you can fix in one sentence. Do not. You said you were not selling today, and keeping that promise is worth more than the sentence.

**V1 — Guide:** **Three. Skipping the money questions.** This is the most common one, and it is the one that does the damage — because those profit centers become the keywords the audit runs on. Skip them and you have quietly broken the next two steps.

**V1 — Guide:** And do not try to skip the whole conversation. Without a completed needs analysis there is no recommendation, no useful audit, and nothing for our team to work from.

**V1 — Guide:** **Four. Leaving without a budget.** This is the one that stops everything downstream. No client-provided number means no recommendation gets built, and you will be booking a second call to ask a question you could have asked while you were sitting there.

**V1 — Guide:** **Five. Not asking for their existing reports.** Free intelligence, and most reps never ask.

**V1 — Guide:** **And six. Only ever doing this with new prospects.** Run it again with existing clients **at least once a year.** Businesses change, their goals move, and a needs analysis a year old is out of date. It is also the single easiest reason to be back in a room with a client who is happy with you.

**V1 — Guide:** And the handoff. The completed form goes in with your recommendation request, and the profit centers you captured become the keywords for the digital audit — which is exactly the next block.

## SEGMENT 6 — RECAP AND WHERE THIS LEADS

**ON-SCREEN:** The four stages recap as text, then the pillar advances with a **green box** from block six to block seven.

**V1 — Guide:** Four stages. Read the form beforehand and bring your research. Open by telling them you are not selling today. Work through the four areas in whatever order the conversation takes. Then confirm the next meeting and submit it in step two.

**V1 — Guide:** And do not leave without **a budget and a presentation date.** Everything downstream is waiting on those two.

**V1 — Guide:** And the line to keep. **Know the form well enough that you never have to read it.**

**V1 — Guide:** Remember what rides on it. The profit centers you wrote down become the keywords for the audit. Which is next — and it is the thing you take back into the room.

**V1 — Guide:** Take the quiz and we will see you there.

## TRAINER NOTES *(not narrated)*

**Where this sits.** Block 6 of 11. Follows MRR Prospect Research, feeds the Digital Audit and the Request for Recommendation. **This is the hard dependency in the whole pipeline.** The audit cannot run properly without it, and **the strategy team will not build a recommendation without it at all.**

**The verbatim lines are the deliverable.** Five of them, and they should be drilled rather than watched:
- *"Do you think about advertising as a monthly number or a yearly one?"* — then *"and roughly where does that land?"*
- *"I ask because I'm not going to come back with a plan you can't afford. I'd rather build something that fits than waste your time."*
- *"I'm going to ask you a bunch of questions about your business today. I'm not selling you anything in this meeting."*
- *"Of everything you sell, what actually makes you the most money? Not what you sell the most of — what you make the most on."*
- *"Rough thirds is fine. I'm not after your books — I just need to know where to point the advertising."*

**The best question on the form is "what do people get wrong about you?"** It surfaces positioning problems in one sentence and it is easy for an owner to answer. Worth asking reps in the weekly meeting what answers they got — the good ones become campaigns.

**The mistakes, with the symptom somebody would notice:**
| Mistake | What it looks like when it goes wrong |
|---|---|
| Reading the form aloud | Short, flat answers. The meeting feels like paperwork |
| Selling during the CNA | Client goes guarded; you lose the honest answers you came for |
| Skipping the money questions | Audit runs on guessed keywords; client says "nobody searches for us that way" |
| Leaving without a budget | No recommendation can be built; a second call needed to ask one question |
| Not asking for existing reports | Free competitive intelligence left on the table |
| Never revisiting with clients | Recommendations drift out of date; an easy retention conversation never happens |

**The annual revisit is a retention mechanism, not admin.** It was one line in the original script. It is the easiest legitimate reason to be in front of a happy client, and it generates upsell without a pitch. Push it.

**Suggested work product for this block.** One completed needs analysis on a real prospect, brought to the weekly account manager meeting. **Check two things specifically: is there a client-provided budget, and are the profit centers specific enough to become keywords?** Those are the two fields the rest of the pipeline depends on.

Note on the first: "General contracting" is not a keyword. "Metal roofing" is.
