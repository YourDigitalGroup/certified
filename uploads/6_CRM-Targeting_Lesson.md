# CRM Targeting Ads — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Targeted Digital Marketing (Room #14c in the Digital House)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime:** 1317 counted spoken words ≈ **8:47** at 150 wpm
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.
**Spine — the one sentence a trainee must repeat cold:** *Email marketing lands in their inbox. CRM targeting uses that same list to reach them everywhere else.*

## SECTION B — CRM Targeting: Full Script

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is
**ON-SCREEN:** Real screenshot of a spreadsheet of customer names, emails, and phone numbers — details blurred. **Green box** on the row count. Then a real display ad on a phone, then the same ad on a laptop. Text: *same list, different place.*

**V2 — Scene:** *"We've got about three thousand of these in the system. Haven't done anything with them."*
**V1 — Guide:** Every business you walk into has one of these — a spreadsheet, a scheduling system, a shoebox — full of people who have already paid them money at least once.
**V1 — Guide:** So, plainly: **CRM targeting** takes that list of email addresses and phone numbers and turns it into an advertising audience. Those specific people — and nobody else — start seeing the client's ads as they move around the internet.
**V1 — Guide:** And here's the one thing to get straight, because reps mix this up constantly. This is **not email marketing.** Nobody's being sent an email. We're using the address as a *name tag* — a way to recognize somebody and show them a display ad.
**V1 — Guide:** Say it this way and you'll never confuse a client: **email marketing lands in their inbox. CRM targeting uses that same list to reach them everywhere else.** Two different tactics, same list, and they work beautifully together.
**V1 — Guide:** Here's the analogy. Most advertising is casting a net. This is calling people by name. And like the rest of our display family, it's bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis** — the advertiser pays for the ad being served.

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of a shop owner at a laptop with a customer list open. Then three statistics build as animated numbers. Then two text columns: *stranger* / *already bought from you*, with a **green box** on the second.

**V1 — Guide:** So why should an advertiser do this? Three reasons.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: the best audience they'll ever buy is the one they already own.** The research isn't close. The probability of selling to an existing customer runs around sixty to seventy percent. For a stranger, five to twenty. Several times more likely, same ad, same money.
**V1 — Guide:** Add to that: acquiring a new customer generally costs somewhere between five and twenty-five times what it costs to keep one. And for most businesses, roughly two-thirds of revenue comes from people who already bought.
**V1 — Guide:** Now put that next to what every owner actually does, which is spend nearly all their advertising money chasing strangers while the list of people who already like them sits in a drawer.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: it's the cure for the customer who quietly stopped coming.** Nobody fires their vet or their HVAC company — they drift. They get busy, they forget. Those people aren't lost, they're *lapsed*, and they're the easiest business in the building to get back.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: nearly every impression lands on somebody who matters.** Most advertising accepts waste as the cost of doing business. Here the audience is a list of known customers — about as little waste as advertising gets.
**V1 — Guide:** One honest caution: it isn't magic and it isn't complete. Not every address on a list can be matched to a device, so the audience will always be smaller than the list. More on that in a minute, and it's worth being upfront about with a client.

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For
**ON-SCREEN:** Three real photos, one at a time — a veterinary or dental front desk, an HVAC or plumbing van, a boutique or jewelry counter. Each type's name and reason type in beside it.

**V1 — Guide:** Three easy targets.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses built on repeat visits people skip.** Vets, dentists, salons, auto service, chiropractors. There's a natural rhythm, and a big chunk of the list has fallen out of it.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses with a long gap between purchases.** HVAC, roofing, flooring, furniture, powersports. Somebody who bought a furnace eleven years ago is exactly the right person to talk to now, and the client already has their name.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses with something new to tell existing customers.** A new service, a new location, an expanded product line. The cheapest people to sell a second thing to are the ones who already bought the first thing.
**V1 — Guide:** Honest flip side: if a client keeps no customer records, there's nothing to target. That's a real no — and often the start of a different conversation about getting them collecting.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics
**ON-SCREEN:** (1) A blurred customer list, then a match process, then a smaller matched audience with a **green box** showing the difference → *the list becomes an audience*. (2) The same list splitting into three labeled groups — active, lapsed, high-value — with different ads beside each → *speak to them like you know them*. (3) Devices linked across a household, plus a schedule and a report → *reach them everywhere, prove it*. Closes on text: *Match the list · Segment the message · Reach every screen.*

**V1 — Guide:** Three moving parts.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: the list becomes an audience.** The client provides email addresses, phone numbers, or both, out of their own records. Those get matched against devices and households, and the ones that match become the audience.
**V1 — Guide:** Two things to be straight about. First, **not everything matches.** Some addresses are old, some can't be tied to a device. So three thousand names becomes an audience of something less — that's the **match rate**, and telling a client to expect it makes you sound like you know the business instead of like you oversold it.
**V1 — Guide:** Second, and this matters: it has to be *their* list. Their customers, their records, collected legitimately. A purchased list of strangers isn't a CRM audience and shouldn't be treated like one. If a client offers you a list they bought, that's a conversation to bring back, not to run with.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: speak to them like you know them.** This is where the tactic earns its money, and where a lazy campaign wastes it.
**V1 — Guide:** Because the audience is known customers, the ad can say something a stranger's ad can't. Split the list — recent visitors, people gone two years, best customers — and write for each. The lapsed customer gets "it's been a while." The good customer gets the new service. Same budget, three conversations.
**V1 — Guide:** A generic brand banner pointed at a customer list is the most common way this gets wasted. If the ad could have run to strangers, we didn't use the advantage.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: reach them on every screen.** The list gets matched across devices — **cross-device matching**, same as our other targeting products. Somebody who gave an email at the front desk gets reached on their phone, tablet, and home laptop, because those are linked to one household.
**V1 — Guide:** Then the usual housekeeping: a daily ad schedule, conversion tracking so we can see what the campaign produced, and weekly refinement — shifting weight toward the segments that are actually responding.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and definition animate in as text, with green boxes on the blurred list, the matched audience, and the match-rate figure.

**V1 — Guide:** Four terms and you're fluent.
**V1 — Guide:** **CRM** — customer relationship management. In plain terms, whatever system a business keeps its customer records in. Could be real software, could be a spreadsheet.
**V1 — Guide:** **First-party data** — information a business collected itself, from its own customers. Theirs, not rented or bought. It's the most valuable data any small business has and almost none of them realize it.
**V1 — Guide:** **Audience match** — the process of taking that list and connecting it to real devices so ads can be served to those specific people.
**V1 — Guide:** **Match rate** — the share of the list that successfully matched. Always less than a hundred percent, always worth mentioning up front.

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature list animates in. Four benefit lines: *Advertise to known customers · Win back the ones who drifted · Almost no wasted impressions · Every screen in the household.* Payoff: a blurred client list beside two different real ads — one saying "it's been a while," one promoting a new service — plus a real photo of a veterinary front desk. Small tag: *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** What's in it? The CRM audience built from the client's own records. Ad content tailored to that audience rather than the general public. Cross-device delivery. A daily ad schedule. Conversion tracking. Weekly refinement across segments. And reporting.
**V1 — Guide:** What does the owner get? They **advertise to known customers** instead of to strangers. They **win back the ones who quietly drifted away.** They spend with **almost no wasted impressions.** And they reach those people on **every screen in the house**, not just one.
**V1 — Guide:** So picture a veterinary clinic. Years of records, thousands of pets and owners — and like every clinic, a long list of people who came faithfully and then just stopped. Not angry, not gone to a competitor. Life happened.
**V1 — Guide:** After: that list got split. Current clients saw ads about the new dental service. The ones who hadn't been in over a year saw something gentler — a reminder about annual exams, with the clinic's name and face attached. And it followed them onto the laptop at home, not just the phone.
**V2 — Scene:** *"I kept seeing you and realized it had been almost two years. I felt terrible."*
**V1 — Guide:** — and that's the whole tactic in one sentence. Those people didn't need convincing. They needed reminding. *(Illustrative — not a promise of specific numbers.)*

### SEGMENT 7 — Recap + Quiz Bridge
**ON-SCREEN:** Recap text — 1: match the list · 2: segment the message · 3: reach every screen. *Quiz →* button slides up.

**V1 — Guide:** Three parts. One: the list becomes an audience — matched to devices, never a hundred percent, and it has to be their own list. Two: segment the message — lapsed customers and good customers should not see the same ad. Three: reach them on every screen, with tracking and weekly refinement.
**V1 — Guide:** And the two lines to carry in. **Email marketing lands in their inbox; CRM targeting uses that same list to reach them everywhere else.** And the one that opens the conversation: *the best audience you'll ever buy is the one you already own.*
**V1 — Guide:** That's CRM targeting. Quick ten-question check. No pressure — you already know this.

## SECTION D — AE Field Notes *(not narrated)*

**How the advertiser pays.** Bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis**, like the rest of the display family. Impression arithmetic is taught once, in Targeted Display.

**How it's sold.** Almost always an add-on, and one of the easiest to open: *"How many customer emails and phone numbers do you have in your system?"* Owners answer immediately, and the number is usually bigger than they expect. The natural follow-up is the reactivation conversation, because every business has lapsed customers and every owner knows it.

**This is the same list as the email module — say so.** Selling both is not double-dipping, it's coverage. Email reaches the inbox; this reaches everywhere else. Clients who buy both get the list working twice.

**Bundles with:**
- **Email marketing** — same first-party list, two delivery paths. The cleanest bundle in the catalog and the easiest to explain.
- **Targeted display** — CRM folds in as one audience alongside retargeting and contextual.
- **Geofence and event targeting** — same buying basis, same cross-device machinery.
- **Website / landing pages** — the ad needs somewhere useful to land, and a "welcome back" page makes the lapsed-customer segment much stronger.

**Objections and responses:**
- *"I already email these people."* → "Different tactic, same list. Email lands in the inbox. This reaches them everywhere else. Most people don't open every email — this one they don't have to open."
- *"Isn't it strange to advertise to people who already know me?"* → "It's stranger to spend all your money on people who don't. Existing customers are several times more likely to buy than strangers."
- *"Will they know I gave you their information?"* → Be careful and accurate here: the list is used to match devices, not to display anyone's personal information, and nothing about a customer is exposed in the ad. Don't embellish beyond that.
- *"I don't keep good records."* → That's a legitimate no on this tactic, and a legitimate yes on helping them start collecting. Say the honest thing; it's a better long-term sale.

**Lines for the room:**
- *"The best audience you'll ever buy is the one you already own."*
- *"Email marketing lands in their inbox. This uses the same list to reach them everywhere else."*
- *"Nobody fires their vet. They just drift. These people aren't lost, they're lapsed."*
- *"You're spending most of your money on strangers while the list of people who already like you sits in a drawer."*

**Retention note.** Same click-column risk as all display. Additional watch item specific to this tactic: **list freshness.** A CRM audience decays — addresses go stale and the same people see the same campaign for months. Set the expectation at the first meeting that the client refreshes the list periodically, and use segment refreshes as a natural reason for an ongoing conversation rather than a set-and-forget campaign.

**Writer's notes:**
- **Statistics sources:** the 60–70% versus 5–20% probability comparison is from *Marketing Metrics* (Farris et al.), widely reproduced. The 5–25x acquisition-versus-retention cost range traces to Harvard Business Review. The roughly two-thirds-of-revenue-from-existing-customers figure appears across 2026 retention compilations. **All are long-standing industry research, not audited measurements of our product — attribute as "research shows."** Chosen because all three prove one idea: *the audience they already own outperforms strangers.*
- **Deliberately excluded:** the CPM figure, the click-through estimate, the ad-set design fee, the monthly minimum, and the contract term. Also excluded: any profit-lift figures, since those read as promised results.
- **Compliance flags, both real:**
  1. **The list must be the client's own first-party data.** The script says this out loud and Q9 tests it. A purchased or scraped list should never be run as a CRM audience, and reps need to know that before somebody hands them a spreadsheet.
  2. **Language on privacy.** Reps say the list is used to *match devices*, never that it identifies or displays anyone's information. Same discipline as the geofencing modules.
- **Match rate is deliberately taught without a number.** Rates vary by list quality and platform, and quoting a percentage would set an expectation we may not hit. The script teaches the concept and the disclosure habit instead.
- **Confirm before recording:** whether the platform also supports *suppression* — excluding existing customers from prospecting campaigns. That's a genuine benefit and a strong efficiency argument, but it isn't stated on the sales sheet so it was left out of the script.
- **Payoff vertical (veterinary clinic)** is new, and matches the pet-care example on the sales sheet. Updated list: diner, roofer, flooring co., hearing clinic, insurance agency, storage co., garden center, medical clinic, auto repair shop, pest control co., RV dealership, used-car lot, physical therapy clinic, wedding/event venue, **veterinary clinic**.
