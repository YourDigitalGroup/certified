# Email Marketing — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Online Content Marketing (Room #8 in the Digital House)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime target:** ~6.5 minutes + 10-question quiz
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.

## SECTION A — The Reusable Tactic Lesson Template
*(Use this exact shape for all ~25 tactic modules. Only the content changes — the beats never do.)*

Two questions before you write: **"What's the plain-English definition?"** and **"What are the 3 core mechanics?"** Everything else follows the beats.

**Voice convention (all modules):**
- **V1 — Guide:** the teaching spine. Carries every segment.
- **V2 — Scene:** short in-the-world lines only — a customer's search, a quoted reaction, a review snippet, and the quiz prompts. Its job is to signal a "mode switch" and keep the ear awake.
- *(Optional V3 later if you want a distinct quiz host — but two voices covers everything below.)*

**On-screen rule — the visual kit (all modules):** keep it clean and real. Three ingredients only:
1. **Animated text** — key words and short lines build/fade in as V1 speaks. This is the workhorse.
2. **Real photos** — actual screenshots of the product (a real email open on a phone, a real inbox list of subject lines, a real signup form) and real photography of people/businesses (a shop owner at a laptop, a storage facility, a salon front desk). No clip-art, no cartoon figures.
3. **Green highlight box** — a green callout box drawn over a real screenshot to point at a specific section (same style as the sales sheet: box the subject line, box the single call-to-action button, box the unsubscribe link), with a text label animating in beside it.

**No cheesy line illustrations, icon/emoji cards, or metaphor graphics** (no flying envelopes, glowing houses, mailbox drawings, little walking figures). Any analogy the Guide speaks stays *spoken only* — we don't draw it. Quiz cards being tappable is UI, not illustration, so that stays.

**Quiz blueprint (same every tactic):** Q1–3 = the 3 mechanics · Q4–6 = terms · Q7 = who it's good for + why · Q8 = why-it-matters scenario · Q9–10 = features/benefits (one may use the Segment-6 payoff).

**Answer placement rule (all modules):** vary which letter is correct — roughly even across A, B, C, and D, with no repeating pattern. If the correct answer sits at B nine times out of ten, trainees stop reading the options and start pattern-matching, and the quiz measures nothing. Also keep the wrong answers plausible; obviously silly distractors have the same effect.

**Payoff rules (Segment 6):** pick the easiest-to-picture business from Segment 3; keep outcomes plain and clearly *illustrative* — no invented numbers, no real names.

## SECTION B — Email Marketing: Full Script

> **Best-practices note.** Segment 4 gives plain guidance on send day, send time, subject-line length, email length, and image balance. Send timing is deliberately general — Tuesday through Thursday, mid-morning, industry-dependent — because the published data conflicts and because the goal is a rep who sounds informed, not one who opens a conversation about split-testing. If a client pushes for more precision, the script hands the rep an easy out: it depends on their business, and that's what the strategy side is for. Re-check the figures before recording; the image-ratio guidance in particular has softened in recent years.

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is *(0:00–0:40)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Real phone screenshot of a crowded inbox. A **green box** draws around one subject line that's short, specific, and clearly local. That email opens: one photo, a few lines, one obvious button. **Green box** on the button, label *one clear action.* Then a real screenshot of a spreadsheet of email addresses — green box on the row count, label *already theirs.*

**V2 — Scene:** *"Oh — I've been meaning to call these guys."*
**V1 — Guide:** That's the sound of email working. Not a hard sell. Just a business landing in somebody's inbox at the exact moment that person had a half-formed thought about needing them.
**V1 — Guide:** So, plainly: **email marketing** is sending useful, well-written messages to people who already gave the business their address — customers, past customers, folks who asked for a quote — on a regular schedule, with one clear thing to do in each one.
**V1 — Guide:** Here's the analogy. Every other room in the Digital House rents attention. Search rents it. Social rents it — those followers belong to the platform. Email is the one place where the **audience belongs to the business.** Nothing stands between them and the inbox.
**V1 — Guide:** And nearly every business you walk into is already sitting on a list — addresses from quotes, online orders, the sign-up sheet by the register — in a spreadsheet nobody's opened in two years. You're not asking them to build anything. You're asking them to use what they've got.

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters *(0:40–1:20)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of a shop owner at a laptop. Then a real comparison — a social post's modest reach beside an email delivered straight to an inbox, **green boxes** on each. Then a real spreadsheet of contacts, green box on the total. Stakes text builds: *they own this audience → repeat customers are the cheap ones → the list is already there.*

**V1 — Guide:** Three reasons a business needs this.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: they own the audience.** On social, a business posts and the platform decides how few of its own followers see it. Email has no middleman — if somebody's on the list, the message goes to them. And nobody can change those rules on a Tuesday.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: the cheapest sale is to somebody who already bought.** Every owner is chasing new customers. Meanwhile the person who paid them once, liked it, and forgot they exist is the easiest money in the building. Email is the only tactic pointed straight at that person.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: the list is already there, doing nothing.** Ask an owner how many addresses they've collected over the years. They'll say "a few hundred" and then look a little sheepish, because they know they've never used them. That's not a hard sell — that's pointing at money on the floor.

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For *(1:20–1:55)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Three real photos appear one at a time — a salon or auto-service bay, a business taking an online quote request, a storage facility with seasonal signage. Each business type's name and its one-line reason type in beside the photo.

**V1 — Guide:** So who do you picture pitching this to? Three easy ones.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses people buy from more than once.** Salons, restaurants, auto service, retail. The whole game is being remembered at the right moment, and the inbox is the cheapest reminder there is.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses already collecting addresses without realizing it.** Anybody taking online orders, quotes, or appointments has been building a list for years. Ask to see it — that moment is usually when the deal happens.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses with seasons or news.** Storage, tax prep, HVAC tune-ups, event venues, new inventory. There's a natural reason to reach out that isn't "please buy something."
**V1 — Guide:** The thread: a reason to stay in touch, and somebody worth staying in touch with. Flip side — a business selling one thing once, to strangers who'll never return, has less use for this.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics *(1:55–4:35)*
**ON-SCREEN:** (1) Two real contact lists side by side, **green box** on each → label *the list · theirs vs. acquired*. (2) Two real emails side by side — one written as a press release ("We are excited to announce…"), one written to the reader — **green box** on the second, sub-label *written to their customer*; then a real designed email graphic beside a generic stock photo, green box on the real one, sub-label *graphics included*. Then green box moves to the subject line, the single button, and the balance of text and image → label *the message*; best-practice lines build as short text beside it. (3) A repeating monthly send on a calendar, then a real report view → label *the rhythm*. Closes on text: *The list · The message · The rhythm.*

**V1 — Guide:** Three moving parts. Just three.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: the list.** Two kinds. Their **own list** — customers, past customers, quote requests. That's the gold; those people half-expect to hear from them. And **acquired lists** — curated contacts for reaching people who've never heard of them. Legitimate tool, done properly, but set expectations honestly: strangers respond differently than customers do.
**V1 — Guide:** And know the rules, because they're not optional. Every email needs a working unsubscribe, an honest subject line, and a real address for the business. That's the law, not a preference. Anybody promising a client they can blast a bought list of random addresses is going to get that client's email shut off.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: the message.** And before any rules about formatting — this is the part that's actually worth the money, so slow down here.
**V1 — Guide:** These emails get **written by a professional, and written to the reader.** Not to the business owner. To *their customer.* That distinction is the whole program, so let me make it concrete.
**V1 — Guide:** Most small-business email sounds like a press release. *"We are excited to announce that our company has recently expanded our facility to include additional climate-controlled storage options."* Nobody has ever read that sentence on purpose. It's about the business. Rewrite it aimed at the person holding the phone: *"Your garage doesn't have to look like that."* Same information. One of them gets opened.
**V1 — Guide:** So the rule is simple enough to say in a meeting: fewer "we" and "our," more "you" and "your." A promotional blast talks about itself. A good email talks to somebody.
**V1 — Guide:** And it isn't a blog post either. This isn't an article about the industry, and it isn't a sales sheet with a header. It's a short message from a business somebody already knows, about something that might actually be useful to them this month.
**V1 — Guide:** You know exactly what I'm describing, because you sell copy for a living. You know the spot that talks *at* a listener versus the one that sounds like somebody talking to them. Same craft. The trick in email is doing it in two layers at once — writing in the *advertiser's* voice, aimed at the *advertiser's customer.* That's a skill, and it's the one they're paying for.
**V1 — Guide:** **All the graphics come with it, too.** Designed to match the business, built at the right size so an email loads fast on a phone, using real photos of their place when we've got them instead of a stock photo of a smiling stranger in a headset. The owner isn't hiring a designer, sending us artwork, or approving a separate bill for it. It's in there.
**V1 — Guide:** So when an owner asks why they'd pay for this when they could type an email themselves — that's the answer. Anybody can send an email. Writing something a customer opens and acts on, in that business's voice, with graphics that look like the business, month after month without running dry — that's the expertise. That's what makes the program worth doing.
**V1 — Guide:** Now, on top of that voice, a few plain formatting practices. Knowing these will make you sound sharp, and none of it's complicated.
**V1 — Guide:** **Subject line short and specific** — roughly thirty to fifty characters, because a phone cuts it off around there. "Climate-controlled units now open" beats "A message from our team about exciting news."
**V1 — Guide:** **Send mid-week, in the morning.** Generally Tuesdays, Wednesdays, and Thursdays, generally mid-morning — but it's all industry-dependent, and that's a fine thing to say out loud. A restaurant and an accountant aren't catching people at the same moment. Weekends are usually weakest.
**V1 — Guide:** **Keep it short.** A photo, a paragraph or two, one button. Say enough to be worth opening, then send them to the website to finish the story. That's what the site is for.
**V1 — Guide:** **Don't build the whole email out of pictures.** More text than images — sixty-forty is the old rule of thumb and still a fine target. Spam filters distrust an email that's one giant image with no readable words. And plenty of inboxes block images until somebody clicks to load them, so if the offer only lives inside the picture, that reader sees a blank rectangle. Every image gets a written description behind it — helps those readers, and helps anybody on a screen reader.
**V1 — Guide:** **And build it for a phone,** because that's where most email gets opened. **Responsive design** just means it rearranges itself to fit the screen, with a button big enough for a thumb.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: the rhythm.** One email is not a campaign. Showing up consistently is what makes a business recognizable in a crowded inbox. And there's reporting after each send, so the client sees what landed instead of guessing. That's the part an owner doing it themselves never reaches — they send one email, get busy, and never send another.
**V1 — Guide:** That's it. The list, the message, the rhythm.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms *(4:35–5:15)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and its one-line definition animate in as text, one at a time. **Green box** on the real screenshot where relevant — the subject line and the gray preview text beside it, the unsubscribe link in the footer, the same email on phone and desktop.

**V1 — Guide:** Five terms so you sound like you know your stuff — because now you do.
**V1 — Guide:** **First-party list** — contacts the business collected itself. "First-party" just means it's theirs, as opposed to acquired from somewhere else.
**V1 — Guide:** **Subject line and preview text** — the subject is the headline; the preview text is that gray bit of copy the inbox shows right after it. Most people write the subject and forget the preview, which wastes a second chance to get somebody in.
**V1 — Guide:** **Open rate and click rate** — how many opened it, and how many then clicked. Two different problems: weak opens usually means the subject line, weak clicks usually means the offer or the button.
**V1 — Guide:** **Unsubscribe** — the link letting somebody leave. Legally required, and genuinely a good thing: somebody who can't escape marks it as spam instead, and that hurts everyone else's mail getting through.
**V1 — Guide:** **Responsive design** — the email adjusts to whatever screen it opens on. Most people read on a phone, so an email that isn't responsive is one most people won't read.

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff *(5:15–6:15)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature names animate in as a text list, green box ticking to each element on the real email screenshot. Then four benefit lines build as text: *An audience they own · Written and designed for them · Brings past customers back · Reporting they can read.* For the payoff: two real storage-company screenshots side by side — **before** (a spreadsheet of unused addresses) and **after** (a clean responsive email, one photo, short copy, one button) — plus a real photo of a storage facility. A small text tag reads *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** What's in it? Lead with the two that matter most.
**V1 — Guide:** **All the writing is included, and it's written by somebody who does this for a living** — in the business's voice, aimed at that business's customer, not at the owner and not as a sales pitch. **And all the graphics are included** — designed to match the business, sized right for a phone, real photos of their place where we have them.
**V1 — Guide:** Then everything around it: emails professionally built to work on any device. Somebody reaching out to the owner regularly to find out what's actually going on, so the content is real news instead of filler. Links back to their website and landing pages, so the email feeds the room where selling happens. Sending to their own database, plus curated list acquisition when the goal is new people. Full compliance with spam law and industry guidelines. And reporting after each send.
**V1 — Guide:** What does the owner get? Four things. **An audience they own** — no algorithm, no rented land. **Writing and design they'd never produce themselves**, landing straight in the inbox. **Past customers coming back**, which is the cheapest revenue they'll ever book. And **reporting they can actually read**, so they know what landed.
**V1 — Guide:** Under the hood, the simple truth: this is the only room in the Digital House where the business owns the audience outright. The website is property they own. The list is people they own the relationship with.
**V1 — Guide:** So picture a local storage company, eight locations, been around forever. Before: a spreadsheet with years of renter addresses — people who rented a unit, moved out, and never heard another word. The owner didn't think of it as marketing. He thought of it as old paperwork.
**V1 — Guide:** After: one email a month, written for the renter rather than about the company. Not "we have expanded our facility" — more like "the garage is about to get worse before it gets better." A real photo of the units, not a stock photo. Short, one button, built for a phone.
**V2 — Scene:** *"Got your email — perfect timing, my garage is a disaster."*
**V1 — Guide:** — and that became a normal way for units to get rented. Nothing about the business changed. He just started talking to people who'd already trusted him once. *(Illustrative — not a promise of specific numbers.)*

### SEGMENT 7 — Recap + Quiz Bridge *(6:15–6:45)*
**ON-SCREEN:** The three mechanics recap as animated text — 1: the list · 2: the message · 3: the rhythm — with the real email screenshot behind them and the green box tapping each area one last time. A *Quiz →* button slides up.

**V1 — Guide:** Three parts. One: the list — their own contacts are gold, acquired lists are a different tool, and the rules aren't optional. Two: the message — written by a professional in their voice, aimed at their customer, with the graphics included; then the plain stuff, short specific subject line, generally mid-week and mid-morning, brief, more words than pictures, built for a phone, one clear action. Three: the rhythm — show up consistently, and let the reporting show what landed.
**V1 — Guide:** And if one thing sticks: the writing and the graphics *are* the product. Anybody can send an email. Sounding like the business, to that business's customer, every month — that's what they're buying.
**V1 — Guide:** And the line to carry into a room: every other tactic rents attention. This is the one where they own the audience — and most of them are already sitting on it.
**V1 — Guide:** That's email marketing. Quick ten-question check. No pressure — you already know this.

## SECTION C — Quiz (10 Questions)
**ON-SCREEN:** One card per question, four tappable options; correct choice highlights green on select. **V2 — Scene** reads each question stem aloud.

**1.** What's the difference between a business's own list and an acquired list?
A) There's no real difference in how people respond
B) Acquired lists are illegal to use
C) Their own list is customers who already know them; an acquired list reaches new people, and strangers respond differently ✅
D) Their own list can't be emailed without permission from the platform

**2.** Who should the email be written to?
A) The business owner, since they're approving it
B) Nobody in particular — keep it general so it fits everyone
C) The industry, like a blog post about the category
D) The advertiser's customer, in the advertiser's voice — fewer "we" and "our," more "you" and "your" ✅

**3.** Why does consistency matter more than any single email?
A) Because the law requires a minimum number of sends
B) Because the first email never gets delivered
C) It doesn't — one good email is enough
D) Showing up regularly makes the business recognizable, and reporting after each send shows what landed ✅

**4.** "Preview text" is…
A) A draft version of the email before it's sent
B) The short line of copy the inbox shows after the subject line ✅
C) The first image in the email
D) A test send to the client

**5.** A business has a strong open rate but almost nobody clicks. Where's the problem most likely?
A) The subject line
B) The send day
C) The offer or the button inside the email ✅
D) The unsubscribe link

**6.** Why is the unsubscribe link a good thing, not just a legal requirement?
A) Because people who can't escape mark it as spam, which hurts future delivery for everyone ✅
B) Because it makes the list grow faster
C) Because it's required to include images
D) It isn't — it should be hidden as well as possible

**7.** Which business is an especially strong fit, and why?
A) A business selling one thing once to strangers who'll never return
B) A company with no customer contact information at all
C) A business that only sells through a single wholesale buyer
D) A salon, because people buy repeatedly and the whole game is being remembered at the right moment ✅

**8.** An owner mentions he has a few hundred customer email addresses in a spreadsheet he's never used. What's the opportunity?
A) Nothing — old addresses are worthless
B) He already owns an audience aimed at his cheapest-to-sell customers, and it's doing nothing ✅
C) He should sell the list to another business
D) He needs to start over and collect new addresses

**9.** A client asks what the best day to send is. What's the best answer?
A) There's one correct day that works for every business
B) Whenever the client happens to have time
C) Generally Tuesday, Wednesday, or Thursday — though it's industry-dependent ✅
D) Weekends, because inboxes are emptier

**10.** In the storage-company example, what actually changed?
A) He started emailing past renters he'd never contacted — short, mobile-friendly, written for the renter rather than about the company ✅
B) He built new storage buildings
C) He lowered his monthly rates
D) He bought a list of strangers

**Answer key:** 1-C · 2-D · 3-D · 4-B · 5-C · 6-A · 7-D · 8-B · 9-C · 10-A
