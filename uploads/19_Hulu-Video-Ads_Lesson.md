# Hulu Video Ads — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Targeted Digital Marketing (Room #26 in the Digital House)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime:** 1340 counted spoken words ≈ **8:56** at 150 wpm
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.
**Spine — the one sentence a trainee must repeat cold:** *You're not just buying streaming. You're buying the show.*

## SECTION A — The Reusable Tactic Lesson Template
*(Identical across all modules. Only the content changes — the beats never do.)*

**Voice convention:** **V1 — Guide** carries the teaching. **V2 — Scene** delivers only in-world lines and the quiz prompts.

**On-screen kit:** animated text · real photos and product screenshots · green callout boxes. **No line illustrations, icon cards, mascots, or drawn metaphors.** Spoken analogies stay spoken.

| # | Segment | Job |

## SECTION B — Hulu Video Ads: Full Script

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of two people watching a prestige drama on a living room TV. Then a real full-screen streaming ad break, **green box** on the frame, label *full screen, no skip.* Then three device shots — TV, phone, laptop — linked with a green bracket.

**V2 — Scene:** *"Don't start the next one without me."*
**V1 — Guide:** That's somebody two hours into a show they've been waiting all week for. Not a clip, not a scroll — an actual episode of an actual series they care about. And in the middle of it, one full-screen ad they can't skip past.
**V1 — Guide:** So, plainly: **Hulu video ads** are full-screen, non-skippable video ads inside Hulu's premium, long-form programming — the originals, the series, the movies, and the live sports that come with it. On the television, and on phones and computers too.
**V1 — Guide:** Now, we sell several streaming television products, so let's place this one cleanly. **Hulu is bought on its own.** It isn't bundled inside our broad streaming buy — that product covers other services. If a client wants to be on Hulu, this is the way.
**V1 — Guide:** And that's the real advantage: **you know exactly what your ad is sitting inside.** Not streaming in general — this programming, on this service.
**V1 — Guide:** It's bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis**, and one impression means the ad played full-screen during somebody's show. The line to carry: **you're not just buying streaming — you're buying the show.**

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters
**ON-SCREEN:** Three statistics build as large animated numbers. Then a long-form episode timeline with a **green box** on a mid-roll break, beside a short clip with no break. Then text: *the ads were always part of the deal here.*

**V1 — Guide:** So why should an advertiser do this? Three reasons.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: this is the most established ad audience in streaming.** Hulu has been running an ad-supported tier since 2012 — it was the first major streaming service to do it, by more than a decade. Everybody else added ads recently and had to talk their subscribers into it.
**V1 — Guide:** That history shows up in the numbers. Hulu with ads reaches somewhere around a fifth to a quarter of American broadband households — the highest ad-supported penetration of any streaming platform. And its ad-supported tiers make up the majority of its roughly fifty million American subscribers.
**V1 — Guide:** Why that matters practically: on Hulu, commercials were never a downgrade somebody grudgingly accepted. They've always been part of the deal. Viewers there are used to them.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: long-form content holds people differently.** There's a real difference between somebody watching a two-minute clip and somebody an hour into an episode. The person deep in a show isn't going anywhere — they're invested, they're comfortable, and the ad break is just part of watching. That's a better place for a local business than a feed somebody's scrolling past.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: full screen, no skip, and nothing to worry about.** The ad takes the whole screen. It can't be clicked past. And it runs inside professionally produced programming, so a client never has to wonder what their ad ended up next to. That's **brand safety**, and business owners in small towns care about it more than reps expect.
**V1 — Guide:** One honest note on budget. This is a premium buy and it costs more per thousand than our broad streaming product. That's what a named premium service costs. If a client's budget can't support it, the broad streaming buy is the better recommendation and you should make it.

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For
**ON-SCREEN:** Three real photos, one at a time — a jewelry counter, a professional office, a business with a distinctly premium presentation. Each type's name and reason type in beside it.

**V1 — Guide:** Three easy targets.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses where the surroundings reflect on them.** Jewelers, high-end retail, medical and dental practices, financial firms, attorneys. When a business is selling trust or taste, appearing inside a prestige drama does something for them that a random ad slot does not.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses selling something emotional or seasonal.** Jewelry, travel, weddings, holidays, big gifts. Long-form content is where people are relaxed and in a mood to feel something, and that's a better environment for that kind of message.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses that need to reach a household.** Full-screen television during an evening show reaches everybody in the room — and for anything decided jointly, that's the whole point.
**V1 — Guide:** And the honest flip side: if a client mainly needs efficient reach across a lot of streaming and doesn't much care which service, sell them the broad streaming buy instead and save them money. This one is for when the environment is part of the message. And as with all television, a thin budget here buys something nobody notices.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics
**ON-SCREEN:** (1) Real premium programming stills, then a full-screen ad frame, then TV/phone/laptop icons → *premium long-form, every screen*. (2) Targeting panels building as text — demographic, behavioral, geographic, topics and channels → *how it's aimed*. (3) Two columns: *broad streaming buy = reach across many services* / *Hulu = one named premium service*, **green box** switching between them → *when to sell which*. Closes on text: *Premium long-form · Aimed · Chosen deliberately.*

**V1 — Guide:** Three moving parts.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: premium long-form, on every screen.** The ads run inside Hulu's own programming — full episodes, original series, movies, and the live sports that come through the bundle. Full-screen, non-skippable, and professionally produced content throughout.
**V1 — Guide:** And it's genuinely multi-device: the connected television, the phone, the laptop. Most of the viewing is on the big screen, but the same campaign follows people to the other two.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: how it's aimed.** There's advanced **demographic targeting** — age, household characteristics, the things a client can describe. And **behavioral targeting**, based on what households have actually been doing rather than what a survey says about them.
**V1 — Guide:** Then geography gets set so the money stays inside the area they serve, and topics, placements, and channels get identified so the ads run against fitting programming.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: knowing which television product to sell.** This is the part that makes you good at your job, because we now have several and a client only needs one or two. These don't overlap — they're separate buys on separate inventory.
**V1 — Guide:** Sell the **broad streaming buy** when the client needs reach across a lot of streaming services and the budget has to stretch. It's cheaper per thousand.
**V1 — Guide:** Sell **Hulu** when the environment is part of the message — when the business is premium, when what they're selling is emotional, when the client cares about exactly what their ad sits beside. A named premium service costs more than a broad buy. Say that plainly and let them choose; a client who picks with their eyes open doesn't complain later.
**V1 — Guide:** Then the management: full campaign management, weekly refinement, reporting, and video production if they need it.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and definition animate in as text, with green boxes on a long-form episode timeline, a full-screen ad, and three device icons.

**V1 — Guide:** Four terms and you're fluent.
**V1 — Guide:** **Long-form content** — full episodes, series, and movies, as opposed to short clips. It matters because somebody an hour into a show is settled in and going nowhere.
**V1 — Guide:** **Behavioral targeting** — reaching households based on what they've actually been doing, rather than only on who they are on paper. You've met this one in the display room; same idea, on television.
**V1 — Guide:** **Brand safety** — the assurance an ad appears beside professionally produced programming, not beside something that could embarrass the business.
**V1 — Guide:** **Multi-device reach** — the same campaign running on the connected television, the phone, and the computer, rather than one screen only.

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature list animates in. Four benefit lines: *Premium surroundings by choice · Full screen, can't be skipped · An audience used to ads · Every screen, one campaign.* Payoff: a real jewelry counter photo beside a full-screen streaming ad during a prestige drama. Small tag: *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** What's in it? Full-screen non-skippable video ads inside Hulu's long-form programming. Advanced demographic and behavioral targeting. Geographic targeting. Topics, placements, and channel targeting identified. Multi-device delivery across television, mobile, and desktop. Full campaign management with weekly refinement. Regular reporting. And video production if they need it.
**V1 — Guide:** What does the owner get? **Premium surroundings by choice**, not by chance. An ad that's **full screen and can't be skipped.** An audience that's **used to commercials**, because on this platform they always have been. And **one campaign across every screen** they watch on.
**V1 — Guide:** So picture a family jeweler on a small-town square. Third generation, does beautiful custom work, and competes against a chain in the mall an hour away — plus everything online. Their whole business is trust and feeling, and neither of those fits in a banner ad.
**V1 — Guide:** After: a spot running inside evening programming — real shows, full screen, no skip — aimed at households in their region during the run-up to the holidays. Both people on the couch, relaxed, an hour into something they were enjoying.
**V2 — Scene:** *"We saw your ad during that show we watch, and I said — let's just go there instead of the mall."*
**V1 — Guide:** — and notice what did the work. Not a coupon. The company appeared somewhere that felt worth appearing, and that was the whole argument. *(Illustrative — not a promise of specific numbers.)*

### SEGMENT 7 — Recap + Quiz Bridge
**ON-SCREEN:** Recap text — 1: premium long-form · 2: aimed · 3: chosen deliberately. *Quiz →* button slides up.

**V1 — Guide:** Three parts. One: premium long-form programming, full screen, non-skippable, across television, mobile, and desktop. Two: aimed with demographic and behavioral targeting, plus geography and content placement. Three: chosen deliberately — the broad streaming buy for reach across many services, and this one when the environment is part of the message. Separate buys, not overlapping ones.
**V1 — Guide:** And two things to carry in. **You're not just buying streaming — you're buying the show.** And be clear with clients that Hulu is its own buy, not something tucked inside a broader package.
**V1 — Guide:** That's Hulu. Quick ten-question check. No pressure — you already know this.

## SECTION D — AE Field Notes *(not narrated)*

**How the advertiser pays.** Bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis**. One impression means the ad played full-screen during programming. Impression arithmetic is taught once, in Targeted Display.

**⚠️ Hulu is a separate buy. It is not inside the broad streaming package.** Our streaming TV product (Room 23) covers other services; Hulu is sold on its own. That means:
- If a client wants Hulu, this is the only way to get it.
- Selling both is additive, not duplicative — different inventory, no overlap to disclose.
- **Never tell a client Hulu is "included" in a streaming package.** It isn't, and that promise would be undeliverable.

If a client asks whether their existing streaming buy already covers Hulu, the answer is a clean no.

**Where this sits among the streaming TV products.**
- **Streaming TV (Room 23):** broad buy across a range of streaming services and networks. Cheapest per thousand. Zip-code geography. **Does not include Hulu.**
- **Social OTT/CTV (Room 17):** same streaming delivery, social-style creative instead of a commercial.
- **YouTube TV (Room 24):** the live channel lineup — local news and sports.
- **Amazon Prime Video (Room 25):** Prime Video content, targeted on actual purchase behavior.
- **Hulu (this, Room 26):** Hulu specifically — premium long-form, chosen for the environment.

**How it's sold.** This is an environment sale, not a reach sale. Open with: *"Where your ad shows up says something about you. Do you want to choose, or take what's available?"* For a premium business that lands immediately. For a price-driven business it won't, and that's your signal to pitch Room 23 instead.

**Bundles with:**
- **Streaming TV (Room 23)** — genuinely additive, since the inventory doesn't overlap. Position Room 23 as reach across many services and this as the named premium service.
- **Broadcast and cable television** — additive; the same spot following the audience that cancelled.
- **Website / landing pages** — the ad drives searches and direct visits rather than clicks, so the site has to hold up.
- **Reputation management** — a premium impression sends people to look the business up. A weak star rating wastes it.

**Objections and responses:**
- *"Isn't Hulu already in the streaming package you sold me?"* → No, and answer cleanly. Hulu is bought separately. Adding it is new inventory, not a duplicate.
- *"Why does it cost more than the streaming package?"* → A named premium service costs more than a broad buy across many services. If that isn't worth it for their business, recommend the broad buy.
- *"Everybody pays to skip the ads."* → On Hulu the ad-supported tier is the established, majority product and has been since 2012. Viewers there expect commercials.
- *"How would I know it worked?"* → Be straight: there's no click on a television. Agree upfront on site traffic, branded search, and customers mentioning it.
- *"Can I pick the show?"* → Topics, placements, and channels get identified as part of setup. Don't promise a specific title without checking.

**Lines for the room:**
- *"You're not just buying streaming. You're buying the show."*
- *"The broad buy gets you a lot of streaming. This gets you Hulu specifically. Which matters more for you?"*
- *"Where your ad shows up says something about you."*
- *"On Hulu, the commercials were never a downgrade. They've been part of the deal since 2012."*

**Retention note.** The main risk is **budget mismatch** — premium inventory on a thin budget produces nothing visible, and the client blames the tactic rather than the weight. Second risk is **measurement**: there's no click on a television, so agree at the first meeting on site traffic, branded search, and customers mentioning it.

**Writer's notes:**
- **Statistics sources:** Hulu with Ads reaching roughly a fifth to a quarter of US broadband households — the highest ad-supported penetration of any US platform — is from 2026 ad-tier penetration analysis (Antenna/Hub Research via industry reporting), which also credits Hulu's lead to having launched its ad tier in October 2012 as the first major SVOD to do so. The roughly 50 million US subscriber figure across all tiers, with ad-supported tiers forming the majority, comes from 2025–2026 industry estimates; **note that Disney stopped reporting standalone Hulu subscriber numbers, so all subscriber figures are now estimates rather than disclosed counts — the script says "roughly" for that reason.** Nielsen reports Disney's streaming services partly in aggregate, so avoid quoting a precise Hulu share of TV viewing. Attribute as "industry reporting shows" and re-verify before recording.
- **Deliberately excluded:** the CPM figure, the monthly minimum, the contract term, and the production quote. Completion-rate figures were excluded per the standing rule; the script makes the same point through "non-skippable."
- **The relative cost position is stated directionally** — more per thousand than the broad streaming buy — with no figures. Necessary, because a rep who doesn't know this is a premium product will sell it at a budget that fails.
- **Hulu is deliberately described as a standalone buy throughout.** Per direction, Hulu has been removed from the broad streaming product, so there is no overlap to manage. Reps should never describe Hulu as included in a streaming package.
- **Confirm before recording:** whether specific show or title-level placement can be requested, or only topic and channel categories. The script says topics, placements, and channels are "identified," and the objection list tells reps not to promise a named title. Tighten if the real capability is broader or narrower.
- **Payoff vertical (family jeweler)** is new. Updated list: diner, roofer, flooring co., hearing clinic, insurance agency, storage co., garden center, medical clinic, auto repair shop, pest control co., RV dealership, used-car lot, physical therapy clinic, wedding/event venue, veterinary clinic, private school, clothing boutique, brewery/taproom, home builder, paint store, coffee drive-thru, technical college, senior living community, farm equipment dealer, community credit union, furniture store, outdoor/sporting goods store, **family jeweler**.
