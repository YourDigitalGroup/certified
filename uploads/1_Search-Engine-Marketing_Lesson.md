# Search Engine Marketing (SEM) — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Targeted Digital Marketing (Room #12 in the Digital House)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime:** 1253 counted spoken words ≈ **8:21** at 150 wpm *(GBP reference = 967 words / 6:27)*
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.
**Spine — the one sentence a trainee must repeat cold:** *SEM buys the moment somebody decides.*

## SECTION B — SEM: Full Script

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is
**ON-SCREEN:** Real phone screenshot of a search for *"emergency pest control near me."* **Green box** on the top result's small *Sponsored* label. Then the unpaid listings below, boxed. Then a real ad with sitelinks under it.

**V2 — Scene:** *"There are wasps in the soffit. Right now."*
**V1 — Guide:** That person isn't researching. They want a phone number in ninety seconds. And look at the top of that page — those first results say "Sponsored." Businesses that paid to be standing there at the exact second somebody needed them.
**V1 — Guide:** So, plainly: **Search Engine Marketing** — SEM, or paid search — is buying an ad that only appears when somebody searches something specific. You pick the words. Nobody types them, you show nothing.
**V1 — Guide:** And here's the thing to say early, because it's the first question every advertiser asks. **With search ads, the advertiser pays per click.** Being shown costs nothing. Nothing is owed until a person reads that ad, decides this is the business they want, and taps it.
**V1 — Guide:** That's the opposite of display, where they buy impressions on a cost-per-thousand basis and pay for the ad to be *seen* whether anybody acts or not. Neither is better — different jobs. But get the two straight now, because a client who mixes them up is unhappy by month two.
**V1 — Guide:** Here's the analogy. Every other ad you've ever sold interrupts somebody — a spot interrupts a song. This is the opposite. It's the only advertising where the customer starts the conversation. You're not buying attention. **You're buying the moment somebody decides.**

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of somebody on a phone in a driveway, stressed. Then a real results page with **green boxes** on the top sponsored positions. Then the budget math builds as plain numbers. Then a text line: *what's one customer worth?*

**V1 — Guide:** So why should an advertiser spend money on this? Three reasons, and these are the ones to say out loud.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: this is the highest-intent moment that exists.** Somebody typing "emergency pest control" isn't a lead we hope to warm up — that's a customer deciding, right now, phone in hand.
**V1 — Guide:** And the evidence is in what people actually *do.* Close to half of all searches are somebody looking for a local business — that's where the town goes to decide. And when somebody searches for something nearby on a phone, a large majority visit a business within a day, and a meaningful share buy. Same day.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: it's the only fast lane they've got.** Earned rankings take months, and they should be working on that too. But a business that's new, or just moved, or lost a big account last week can't wait six months. Turn ads on and they're at the top tomorrow.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: they only pay for people who raised a hand.** Every other ad they buy costs the same whether or not a soul responds. Here, being shown is free. Pause on that in a meeting.
**V1 — Guide:** One honest caution that goes with that: they're paying for the *visit*, not the customer. What happens after the click is what decides whether this works.

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For
**ON-SCREEN:** Three real photos, one at a time — a service van pulling up to a house, a law office or specialty clinic, a business with a new-location banner. Each type's name and reason type in beside it.

**V1 — Guide:** Three easy targets.
**V1 — Guide:** **Urgent-need businesses.** Pest control, plumbing, locksmiths, towing, water damage. Nobody shops around when there are wasps in the wall — whoever's at the top gets the call.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses where one customer is worth a lot.** Attorneys, specialty medical, HVAC replacement, remodelers. One job worth thousands pays for a lot of visits.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses that need traffic now.** New locations, a business that moved, a season about to start.
**V1 — Guide:** Honest flip side: if nobody searches for what they sell, this is the wrong tactic. A brand-new product nobody's heard of has no search volume — you can't buy words nobody types. That's a display conversation.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics
**ON-SCREEN:** (1) A keyword list, then a real auction-style results page → *buy the moment*. (2) A real keyword, its matching ad, and the landing page it opens, boxed in sequence → *match the promise*. (3) A negative-keyword list, a map radius, an hours schedule → *control the waste*. Closes on text: *Buy the moment · Match the promise · Control the waste.*

**V1 — Guide:** Three moving parts. Just three.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: buy the moment.** It starts with keyword analysis — what people in that town actually type when they need this business. Those words are the product. You're not buying an audience, you're buying a phrase.
**V1 — Guide:** Then there's an auction, and here's the surprise: the highest bidder doesn't automatically win. Relevance gets weighed too — so a smaller business with a tighter ad can beat a bigger one throwing money at it. Good news for the people you sell to.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: match the promise.** Three things have to line up — the words somebody typed, the ad they see, and the page that opens when they click.
**V1 — Guide:** Somebody searches "emergency pest control." The ad says emergency pest control. The page opens on emergency pest control — not the homepage with a menu and a photo of the owner's truck. And remember: they paid the moment that person clicked. A bad landing page isn't a missed opportunity, it's a receipt for nothing.
**V1 — Guide:** When the three match, two things happen: more people convert, *and* the click costs less, because relevance is rewarded. Mismatch is the number one reason a client tells you they tried search ads and it didn't work.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: control the waste.** This is where the ongoing management lives, and it never stops.
**V1 — Guide:** **Negative keywords** block searches that will never become customers — a pest control company doesn't want "pest control jobs" or "how to kill wasps yourself." That's somebody else's click on their money, and every one of those is a real charge.
**V1 — Guide:** Then geography, so they're not buying a town they won't drive to. Scheduling, so ads run when somebody can answer the phone. And **conversion tracking** — code reporting which clicks became calls and forms. That's what turns guessing into managing, and why campaigns get refined weekly instead of set and forgotten.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and definition animate in as text. **Green box** on the real screenshot where visible — the search box, the Sponsored label, a negative list, the ad-to-page match.

**V1 — Guide:** Five terms and you're fluent.
**V1 — Guide:** **Keyword** — the words somebody types. What you're actually buying.
**V1 — Guide:** **Pay-per-click** — how search advertising is bought. Showing up costs nothing; the advertiser pays when somebody clicks. It's the phrase they'll hear, and now you can explain it in one sentence.
**V1 — Guide:** **Negative keyword** — a word you block so the ad doesn't show for it. The cheapest improvement in all of paid search.
**V1 — Guide:** **Quality Score** — the grade on how relevant the ad and page are to what was typed. Better relevance means better position and a lower cost. It's why matching matters so much.
**V1 — Guide:** **Landing page** — you've met this one. One page, one offer, one action. It's the difference between paying for a click and earning a customer.

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature list animates in. Then four benefit lines: *Reach people at the exact moment · Start immediately · Pay only for people who act · See what actually worked.* Payoff: two real screenshots — **before** (ad pointing at a cluttered homepage) and **after** (tight ad, matching landing page, click-to-call button) — plus a real photo of a pest control van. Small tag: *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** What's in it? A keyword audit and analysis. Audience segments and geographic targeting, so money stays where they can serve. A daily ad schedule. Campaigns built to compete for top placement. Conversion tracking, so leads get counted instead of guessed at. Weekly refinement — negatives added, weak keywords cut, budget moved to what's working. And reporting.
**V1 — Guide:** What does the owner get? They **reach people at the exact moment of need.** They **start immediately** instead of waiting months. They **pay only for people who took an action.** And they **see what actually worked** — which almost nothing else they buy can tell them.
**V1 — Guide:** So picture a local pest control company. Before: ads pointed at the homepage, no negative keywords. They paid real money every time a do-it-yourselfer clicked through, and anyone who landed there got the company history page and had to hunt for a phone number.
**V1 — Guide:** After: separate ads for wasps, termites, and rodents. Each one opening on a page about that exact problem, with a call button at the top. Do-it-yourself searches blocked. Ads shut off at ten at night when nobody's answering.
**V2 — Scene:** *"You were the first one that actually said wasps."*
**V1 — Guide:** — same budget, same company. The money just stopped landing in the wrong place. *(Illustrative — not a promise of specific numbers.)*

### SEGMENT 7 — Recap + Quiz Bridge
**ON-SCREEN:** Recap text — 1: buy the moment · 2: match the promise · 3: control the waste. *Quiz →* button slides up.

**V1 — Guide:** Three parts. One: buy the moment — you're bidding on words people type, and relevance beats budget. Two: match the promise — keyword, ad, and page all saying the same thing. Three: control the waste — negatives, geography, hours, tracking, refined weekly.
**V1 — Guide:** And two things to carry in. They pay **per click**, not to be seen — the opposite of display. And every other ad you sell interrupts somebody; this one shows up because they asked.
**V1 — Guide:** **SEM buys the moment somebody decides.** Quick ten-question check. No pressure — you already know this.

## SECTION C — Quiz (10 Questions)

**1.** In the ad auction, does the highest bidder always win the top spot?
A) Yes — position is purely about budget
B) No — relevance is weighed too, so a tighter, more relevant ad can beat a bigger spender ✅

D) Position is assigned randomly

**2.** A client's ad for "emergency plumber" opens on their homepage. What's wrong?
A) Nothing — the homepage shows everything they do
B) Homepages load faster than other pages
C) The promise breaks — they paid for the click, then made the person hunt, which loses customers and costs more ✅

**3.** What does adding negative keywords accomplish?
A) It lowers the quality of the campaign
B) It removes competitors' ads from the page
C) It hides the ad from existing customers
D) It stops the ad from showing on searches that will never become customers ✅

**4.** How does an advertiser pay for search ads, and how is that different from display?
A) Per impression, same as display
B) A flat monthly fee for placement
C) Per click — being shown costs nothing, whereas display is bought per thousand impressions ✅
D) Only when a sale closes

**5.** "Quality Score" is best described as…
A) A customer satisfaction rating
B) The number of clicks an ad receives
C) The search engine's grade on how relevant the ad and page are to what was typed ✅

**6.** A "keyword" is…
A) A password for the ad account
B) The words somebody types into the search box ✅

**7.** Which business is an especially strong fit, and why?
A) A brand-new invention nobody has heard of or searches for
B) A business that only sells to three long-term contract clients
C) A pest control company, because nobody shops around when there are wasps in the wall ✅

**8.** A business just moved and lost its search visibility. Why is SEM the right first move?
A) Because ads permanently improve earned rankings
B) Because earned rankings take months to rebuild, and ads put them at the top now ✅

**9.** What does conversion tracking make possible?
A) Blocking clicks from competitors
B) Guaranteeing a set number of leads
C) Seeing which clicks actually became calls and form fills, so the campaign can be managed rather than guessed at ✅

A) They raised their prices
B) They stopped advertising entirely

## SECTION D — AE Field Notes *(not narrated)*

**How it's sold.** Rarely a first sale — it's the accelerator you attach once something exists to send traffic to. The natural sequence is website or landing pages first, then SEM. Selling ads into a weak site is the fastest way to create an unhappy client.

**Bundles with:**
- **Targeted landing pages** — effectively required. The "match the promise" mechanic doesn't work without them, and this is the cleanest bundle in the catalog.
- **Website** — the destination has to be fast and obvious on a phone.
- **Google Business Profile** — the ad and the listing occupy the same page; both correct is a double presence.
- **Reputation management** — a strong star rating measurably improves ad conversion. Same clicks, more calls.
- **SEO** — the honest pairing: one is rented and instant, one is earned and slow. Sell both and say exactly that.

**Objections and responses:**
- *"I tried Google Ads and it didn't work."* → Ask two questions: did it point at your homepage, and were there negative keywords? Nearly every failed campaign fails on one of those. They didn't buy a bad tactic, they ran an unmanaged one.
- *"Why pay when I show up for free?"* → You don't show up for free at the top of the page, and free takes months. This is the fast lane while the earned work builds. Different jobs.
- *"It stops the day I stop paying."* → True, and say so plainly. This is rented attention, which is exactly why it belongs beside the things they own — the website and the earned rankings. Never argue this one; agreeing builds more credibility than dodging.
- *"I'll run it myself."* → The setup isn't the work. The weekly negatives, the schedule, the budget shifting — that's the work, and it's what nobody does after month two.

**Lines for the room:**
- *"Every other ad you buy interrupts somebody. This one shows up because they asked."*
- *"You pay nothing to be seen. You pay when someone chooses you."*
- *"If your ad lands on your homepage, you're paying for the click and then hiding the answer."*
- *"Relevance beats budget — you don't have to outspend the big guy, you have to be more specific than him."*

**Retention trap — pre-frame at the first meeting.** Clients judge SEM on clicks, then feel cheated when clicks don't feel like customers. Say it before they can: *"Clicks are the cost, not the result. What we'll look at together is calls and forms — that's what conversion tracking is for."* Setting the measure up front prevents the month-three argument.

**How the advertiser pays — say this early.** Search is bought **per click**. Being shown costs nothing; the advertiser owes only when somebody clicks. State the model, never the rate — and always contrast it with display, which is bought per thousand impressions and charges for the ad being seen. Reps who blur the two create unhappy clients by month two.

**The caution that goes with it:** the advertiser is paying for the *visit*, not the customer. What happens after the click decides whether it works, which is why landing pages aren't optional on this product.

**No budget arithmetic on this tactic.** Unlike impression products, search needs no math to be understood — everybody already knows what a click is. Walking through cost-per-click figures and assumed call rates adds risk (it implies a promised result) without adding clarity. Keep the model, skip the math.

**Evidence worth memorizing (two, not twenty):**
- **Close to half of all searches** are people looking for a local business or service.
- **A large majority of people who search for something nearby on a phone visit a business the same day,** and a meaningful share buy.

Both prove one idea, and it's the line: *the difference isn't the ad, it's the moment.* Answer "does this work?" with behavior, never with ad metrics.

**Writer's notes:**
- No figures from the sheet were used. The CPC and CTR figures are labeled estimates that don't guarantee performance, and the monthly minimum, contract length, and setup fee are all commercial terms — excluded per standing rules.
- "Top four placement" is described as competing for top placements rather than promised, since position is auction-determined and can't be guaranteed.
- **Statistics sources:** the "close to half of all searches are local" figure appears across multiple 2026 PPC benchmark compilations. The same-day local-search behavior figures originate from Google/Think with Google consumer research, widely reproduced. **Both are industry-cited research, not audited measurements — attribute as "research shows" and re-verify annually.**
- **Deliberately excluded, per standing direction:** all click-through rates, conversion-rate percentages, CPC figures, and ROAS ranges. Lessons state the *payment model* — per click for search — but never rates or ad-performance metrics. Evidence is given as consumer behavior instead.
- Payoff vertical (pest control) is not on the do-not-reuse list. Add it: diner, roofer, flooring co., hearing clinic, insurance agency, storage co., garden center, medical clinic, auto repair shop, **pest control co.**
