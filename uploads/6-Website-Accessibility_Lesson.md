# Website Accessibility — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Online Visibility (Room #3 in the Digital House)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime target:** ~6.25 minutes + 10-question quiz
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.

## SECTION A — The Reusable Tactic Lesson Template
*(Use this exact shape for all ~25 tactic modules. Only the content changes — the beats never do.)*

Two questions before you write: **"What's the plain-English definition?"** and **"What are the 3 core mechanics?"** Everything else follows the beats.

**Voice convention (all modules):**
- **V1 — Guide:** the teaching spine. Carries every segment.
- **V2 — Scene:** short in-the-world lines only — a customer's search, a quoted reaction, a review snippet, and the quiz prompts. Its job is to signal a "mode switch" and keep the ear awake.
- *(Optional V3 later if you want a distinct quiz host — but two voices covers everything below.)*

**On-screen rule — the visual kit (all modules):** keep it clean and real. Three ingredients only:
1. **Animated text** — key words and short lines build/fade in as V1 speaks. This is the workhorse.
2. **Real photos** — actual screenshots of the product (the real accessibility panel open on a real site, a real contrast adjustment) and real photography of people/businesses (an older customer on a tablet, a clinic waiting room). No clip-art, no cartoon figures.
3. **Green highlight box** — a green callout box drawn over a real screenshot to point at a specific section (same style as the sales sheet: box the panel icon, box the unlabeled image), with a text label animating in beside it.

**No cheesy line illustrations, icon/emoji cards, or metaphor graphics** (no wheelchair icons floating over a laptop, no ramp drawings, no locked-door metaphors). Any analogy the Guide speaks stays *spoken only* — we don't draw it. Quiz cards being tappable is UI, not illustration, so that stays.

**Quiz blueprint (same every tactic):** Q1–3 = the 3 mechanics · Q4–6 = terms · Q7 = who it's good for + why · Q8 = why-it-matters scenario · Q9–10 = features/benefits (one may use the Segment-6 payoff).

**Answer placement rule (all modules):** vary which letter is correct — roughly even across A, B, C, and D, with no repeating pattern. If the correct answer sits at B nine times out of ten, trainees stop reading the options and start pattern-matching, and the quiz measures nothing. Also keep the wrong answers plausible; obviously silly distractors have the same effect.

**Payoff rules (Segment 6):** pick the easiest-to-picture business from Segment 3; keep outcomes plain and clearly *illustrative* — no invented numbers, no real names.

## SECTION B — Website Accessibility: Full Script

> **⚠️ Compliance guardrail — don't edit these out for punchiness.** Reps are not attorneys, and no tool makes a business immune from suit. Three rules, all written into the script below: never promise legal immunity (say *reduces risk, shows good-faith effort*); never imply the panel alone equals compliance; never quantify the legal side — specific legal questions go to the client's attorney, and the script says so out loud.

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is *(0:00–0:45)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of an older man on a tablet, squinting, holding it at arm's length. Then a real website screenshot as he'd see it — light gray text on white, small type. A **green box** draws around the low-contrast text, label *can you read that?* Then the same site with the accessibility panel open: text two sizes up, contrast raised, suddenly legible. **Green box** on the panel icon, label *accessibility panel*.

**V2 — Scene:** *"Hang on — I've got to get my glasses."*
**V1 — Guide:** Everybody's heard somebody say that. And that person isn't a special case. That person's a customer — squinting at gray type the size of a grain of rice, trying to find a phone number, about fifteen seconds from calling somebody else.
**V1 — Guide:** Nobody did that on purpose. Somebody picked a font because it looked clean on their brand-new laptop. That's the whole story of most inaccessible websites.
**V1 — Guide:** So, plainly: **website accessibility** means building a site so people with disabilities can actually use it — people who can't see well or at all, can't use a mouse, are hard of hearing, or have a tremor.
**V1 — Guide:** What we sell has a visible piece and an invisible one. The visible piece is a little **panel** on the site — tap it and you can enlarge the text, raise the contrast, magnify, space out the lines. The invisible piece is the work underneath that lets the software people already use make sense of the page.
**V1 — Guide:** Think of a wheelchair ramp. Nobody argues about a ramp. Same idea on a website — except most owners have never once considered that their front door might be too narrow. That's your opening: you're not talking anybody out of a bad decision, you're showing them something nobody's shown them.

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters *(0:45–1:35)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photos in sequence — an older woman on a phone at a kitchen table, a man with his arm in a sling on a laptop one-handed, someone on a phone in bright glare. Then real screenshots with **green boxes**: an image with no description where a screen reader says only "image," a form field with no label. Stakes text builds: *more people than they think → it's the law → a locked door on a sale → better for everybody.*

**V1 — Guide:** Four reasons a business needs this — and lead with the first one, not the second.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: it's far more people than any owner thinks.** Roughly one in four American adults lives with some kind of disability. And it's bigger than that — an arm in a sling means one-handed, bright sun means gray text is invisible, and the biggest group of all is just people getting older. Eyes change, hands get less steady. Nobody calls that a disability, and it absolutely affects whether they can use a website. For your typical local advertiser, that's not charity — those are their best customers.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: it's the law — and here's where you're careful.** Courts have applied the Americans with Disabilities Act to websites, and businesses of every size have been sued. That risk is real. But **you are not an attorney.** Never promise this makes anybody lawsuit-proof, because no tool does that. What's true is plenty: it reduces risk and shows a good-faith effort. Specific legal question? *"That's a great question for your attorney."* Say it and move on.
**V1 — Guide:** And lead with reason one, because "you might get sued" makes you sound like you're selling insurance out of a trunk. "A quarter of your customers may be struggling to use your site" is the better conversation.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: every barrier is a locked door on a sale.** Can't read the menu, they don't order. Can't finish the form without a mouse, that's a lead they paid for and dropped on the floor.
**V1 — Guide:** **Four: it makes the site better for everybody.** Ever used a curb cut while pushing a stroller? Built for wheelchairs, used by everyone. Same here — bigger tap targets, higher contrast, captions you can read with the sound off. And the structure a screen reader needs is largely what search engines need too.

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For *(1:35–2:15)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Three real photos appear one at a time — a pharmacy counter or hearing clinic with older patients, a phone with an online ordering or booking screen, a multi-location business with visible signage. Each business type's name and its one-line reason type in beside the photo.

**V1 — Guide:** So who do you picture pitching this to? Three easy ones.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses whose customers are mostly older.** Pharmacies, hearing and vision clinics, senior services, medical practices, financial advisors. Barely a pitch — you're not describing a hypothetical person, you're describing the people in their waiting room.
**V1 — Guide:** **Anybody who transacts online** — ordering, booking, forms, payments. A barrier there isn't an inconvenience, it's a dead transaction. These also draw the most legal attention, because the website *is* how business gets done.
**V1 — Guide:** **Public-facing businesses held to a higher standard** — healthcare, hospitality, education, anybody doing government work, franchises and multi-location operations. Bigger and more visible means more exposure.
**V1 — Guide:** Notice the thread: the more a website is a way to actually *do business*, the more this matters.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics *(2:15–4:05)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Real screenshots throughout; a **green box** moves for each mechanic. (1) Barriers: box on small gray text, then an image a screen reader would announce only as "image," then a mouse-only menu → label *the barriers*. (2) Fixes: the real panel opens; box moves through the one-tap profiles, text size, contrast, magnifier, line spacing → label *the panel*; then a box on page structure being labeled automatically → sub-label *and the part you can't see*. (3) Box on a monitoring view, then a newly added page that broke something → label *the upkeep*. Closes on text: *What blocks people · What fixes it · Who keeps watching.*

**V1 — Guide:** Three moving parts. Just three.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: the barriers — what's actually stopping people.** Three big ones. **Text nobody can read** — too small, or too little contrast. Most common problem on the internet. **Pictures with no description** — somebody who can't see uses a **screen reader**, software that reads the page out loud. It reads whatever written description the site provides, and if nobody wrote one, it just says "image." Picture a restaurant whose whole menu is a photograph: a blind customer hears the word "image," and that's their entire experience of that restaurant. And **things that only work with a mouse** — plenty of people navigate by keyboard, tab tab tab enter. If a menu only opens on hover, they're stuck. *(Two more worth a mention: video with no captions, and buttons too small to hit.)*
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: the fixes — the panel, and the part you can't see.** A small button sits on the site. Tap it and a panel opens with controls: one-tap profiles for common needs, plus text size, contrast and color, a magnifier, line and letter spacing, a bigger cursor, and language translation. There's also a way for a visitor to request a specific accommodation.
**V1 — Guide:** The key idea to say out loud: **the visitor chooses.** The business isn't guessing what somebody needs — the person adjusts the site to themselves in about four seconds and gets on with it.
**V1 — Guide:** Underneath, the tool is also reading through the site and fixing what it can — labeling images, buttons, and form fields so that software can understand the page. Your client will never see it. It's most of the actual work.
**V1 — Guide:** And say this honestly, because it builds more trust than overselling: a panel by itself isn't the whole job. A badly built site is still badly built. The strongest position is all three together — a solid site, the panel and automated fixes, and somebody watching over time.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: the upkeep — this is never finished.** Somebody adds a page, uploads a dozen photos with no descriptions, redesigns a section and the contrast goes out the window. Any of that can break what was fine last month. Accessibility isn't a project you complete, it's a condition you maintain — like security. So there's ongoing monitoring and reporting. And that's also the honest answer on the legal side: what matters isn't one moment of perfection, it's a documented, continuing effort.
**V1 — Guide:** That's it. What blocks people, what fixes it, who keeps watching.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms *(4:05–4:50)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and its one-line definition animate in as text, one at a time. **Green box** on the real screenshot where relevant — the panel for accessibility, an image with its written description visible for alt text. ADA and WCAG are text-only.

**V1 — Guide:** Five terms so you sound like you know your stuff — because now you do. Two are acronyms an owner may say to you first, so know them cold.
**V1 — Guide:** **Accessibility** — building a site so people with disabilities can use it: readable, navigable without a mouse, understandable to the software they rely on.
**V1 — Guide:** **ADA** — the Americans with Disabilities Act. A civil rights law, not a web rule. Older than the modern internet, and courts have applied it to websites. That's where the legal exposure comes from.
**V1 — Guide:** **WCAG** — say it "W-CAG." The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines: the rulebook for how to actually do this. It has levels, and the middle one — double-A — is what most businesses and courts treat as the target. You don't need to know what's in it. You need to know it's the yardstick.
**V1 — Guide:** **Screen reader** — software that reads a web page out loud, used by people who can't see the screen.
**V1 — Guide:** **Alt text** — the written description attached to an image, so a screen reader can say what the picture is instead of just "image." Smallest, most skipped, highest-impact item on the list. *(One more you may hear: Section 508 — same idea, for federal agencies and their contractors.)*

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff *(4:50–5:55)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature names animate in as a text list, green box ticking to each control on the real panel screenshot. Then four benefit lines build as text: *More people can use it · Real risk reduced · Better for everybody · Shows what they stand for.* For the payoff: two real hearing-clinic screenshots side by side — **before** (small gray type, image-only service list, mouse-only menu) and **after** (panel open, text stepped up, high contrast, everything labeled) — with a real photo of an older patient at a clinic front desk. A small text tag reads *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** What's in it? The panel itself, with one-tap profiles and controls for text size, contrast, magnification, spacing, and cursor. Screen reader and keyboard compatibility. Language translation. A way to request an accommodation. Automated scanning and labeling working underneath. Ongoing monitoring and reporting. Alignment with the recognized standards — WCAG double-A and the ADA, plus Section 508 for government work. And the panel gets styled to match the business, so it doesn't look like a stranger bolted onto their site.
**V1 — Guide:** What does the owner get? Four things. **More people can actually use their site**, including a lot of their best customers. **Real risk reduced** — reduced and documented, not eliminated. **A better site for everybody.** And it **shows what they stand for**, which in a small town isn't nothing. This is the rare product an owner might feel genuinely good about buying.
**V1 — Guide:** Under the hood it's one idea: a page is either built so software can understand it, or it isn't. Screen readers need that. Keyboards need it. Search engines happen to need it too. Curb cut again — build the ramp, everybody uses it.
**V1 — Guide:** So picture a local hearing clinic. Every patient walking in has a hearing difficulty and plenty of them are eighty. Before: small gray type, a services list that was actually one big image so a screen reader said "image" and nothing else, a menu that only opened with a mouse. Nobody there meant any of that — they just never saw it, because nobody there needed to.
**V1 — Guide:** After: a patient bumps the text up two sizes and raises the contrast before reading a word. The services are real text. The menu works from a keyboard. And a patient's daughter doesn't have to read the website out loud to her mother anymore.
**V2 — Scene:** *"I could actually read your website myself this time."*
**V1 — Guide:** — that's the sentence to hang onto. Nothing about the clinic changed. They were always good at taking care of people. The website finally matched. *(Illustrative — not a promise of specific numbers.)*

### SEGMENT 7 — Recap + Quiz Bridge *(5:55–6:20)*
**ON-SCREEN:** The three mechanics recap as animated text — 1: the barriers · 2: the fixes · 3: the upkeep — with the real panel screenshot behind them and the green box tapping each area one last time. A *Quiz →* button slides up.

**V1 — Guide:** Three parts, one more time. One: the barriers — unreadable text, pictures with no description, things that only work with a mouse. Two: the fixes — a panel the visitor controls themselves, plus the labeling underneath. Three: the upkeep — because a site changes every month and can break what was working.
**V1 — Guide:** And two things to carry out of here. Lead with the people, not the lawsuit. And when a legal question comes at you, "that's one for your attorney" is the best answer you've got — that's not dodging, that's being straight with them.
**V1 — Guide:** That's website accessibility — the widest front door in the Digital House. Quick ten-question check. No pressure — you already know this.

## SECTION C — Quiz (10 Questions)
**ON-SCREEN:** One card per question, four tappable options; correct choice highlights green on select. **V2 — Scene** reads each question stem aloud.

**1.** A restaurant's entire menu is one photograph. What does a customer using a screen reader experience?
A) The screen reader reads the menu items aloud
B) The screen reader describes the food automatically
C) The site converts the photo to text for them
D) They hear the word "image" and get nothing else ✅

**2.** What's the point of the accessibility panel being adjustable by the visitor?
A) It lets the business guess what most people need
B) The person adjusts the site to their own needs in seconds, rather than the business guessing ✅
C) It removes the need for the site to work properly underneath
D) It only changes how the site looks to the business owner

**3.** Why is accessibility something that has to be maintained rather than finished?
A) Because the guidelines are rewritten every month
B) Because the tool expires and needs replacing
C) Because a site changes constantly — new pages, unlabeled photos, redesigned sections — and each change can break what was working ✅
D) It doesn't; once a site is fixed it stays fixed

**4.** What is "alt text"?
A) The written description attached to an image so a screen reader can say what it is ✅
B) A backup version of a web page
C) Text that appears when a page fails to load
D) An alternate language version of the site

**5.** "WCAG" is best described as…
A) A federal agency that inspects websites
B) A piece of software installed on a site
C) The guidelines that serve as the yardstick for accessibility, with double-A as the common target ✅
D) A certification a business buys once

**6.** The ADA is…
A) A web design standard written for the internet
B) A voluntary industry best practice
C) A rule that applies only to government agencies
D) A civil rights law that courts have applied to websites ✅

**7.** Which business is an especially strong fit, and why?
A) A wholesaler with three longtime customers and no public site
B) A hearing clinic, because nearly every patient it serves is in the group that benefits most ✅
C) A business with no website at all
D) A business whose customers are all under thirty

**8.** An owner asks you point-blank whether this will keep them from getting sued. What do you say?
A) That it reduces risk and shows a good-faith effort, but legal questions are for their attorney ✅
B) Yes — that's the main thing it does
C) That lawsuits over websites aren't a real concern
D) That you'll put the guarantee in writing

**9.** Besides helping people with disabilities, what else does accessibility work tend to do?
A) Nothing else — the benefit is limited to that group
B) It slows the site down as a tradeoff
C) It requires removing images and video from the site
D) It makes the site better for everyone — outdoors, one-handed, sound off — and the structure helps search engines too ✅

**10.** In the hearing-clinic example, what actually changed?
A) The clinic started treating different patients
B) The clinic removed its website
C) Patients could adjust the site themselves and read it without help, and the content became readable by screen readers and keyboards ✅
D) The clinic stopped taking older patients

**Answer key:** 1-D · 2-B · 3-C · 4-A · 5-C · 6-D · 7-B · 8-A · 9-D · 10-C
