# Mobile Audience Targeting — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Targeted Digital Marketing (Room #18 in the Digital House)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime:** 1387 counted spoken words ≈ **9:15** at 150 wpm
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.
**Spine — the one sentence a trainee must repeat cold:** *The phone is the only screen that goes where your customer goes.*

## SECTION A — The Reusable Tactic Lesson Template
*(Identical across all modules. Only the content changes — the beats never do.)*

**Voice convention:** **V1 — Guide** carries the teaching. **V2 — Scene** delivers only in-world lines and the quiz prompts.

**On-screen kit:** animated text · real photos and product screenshots · green callout boxes. **No line illustrations, icon cards, mascots, or drawn metaphors.** Spoken analogies stay spoken.

## SECTION B — Mobile Audience Targeting: Full Script

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of somebody standing in a big-box store aisle looking at their phone. Then a real mobile ad for a local paint store on that phone, **green box** on a *Get Directions* button. Then four labels build as text and all converge on that one phone with a **green bracket** around them, label *one campaign, four methods*: *interests · competitor locations · site visitors · store visitors.*

**V2 — Scene:** *"I'm standing in the paint aisle at the big box store, on my phone, comparing prices."*
**V1 — Guide:** Hold that picture — it's the most valuable thirty seconds in that person's whole buying process, and until recently there was nothing a local paint store could do about it.
**V1 — Guide:** So, plainly: **Mobile Audience Targeting** — you'll hear it called MAT — is **one advertising campaign, running only on phones, that uses several different targeting methods at the same time.**
**V1 — Guide:** Get that sentence right, because it's the thing reps describe badly. This is **not** four separate campaigns. It is **not** four options a client picks between. It's *one* campaign that knows several different ways to recognize the right person — and they all run together.
**V1 — Guide:** Here are the methods it uses. **Interest and behavior targeting** — reaching people based on what they've been browsing and shopping for. **Geofencing** — a handful of chosen physical locations, like a competitor's store. **Ad and site retargeting** — people who've already seen the ad or been to the website. And optionally, **store retargeting** — people who walked into the client's *own* store.
**V1 — Guide:** So how does this sit next to the products we've already covered? Targeted display is a campaign built around audience and content, running on every device. Geofencing is a campaign built around physical places, on every device. **This one takes several of those methods, points all of them at phones, and runs them as a single campaign.** Same toolbox, one device, one campaign.
**V1 — Guide:** And here's why the phone specifically: **it's the only screen that goes where your customer goes.** In their pocket in the competitor's parking lot, on the couch that night, at work the next morning.
**V1 — Guide:** Like the rest of the family, it's bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis.**

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters
**ON-SCREEN:** Three statistics build as large animated numbers. Then a real phone with a "near me" search on it, **green box** on the results. Then text: *the phone isn't a second screen anymore.*

**V1 — Guide:** So why should an advertiser do this? Three reasons.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: the phone is where the day happens.** American adults spend roughly four to five hours a day actively on their phones, and smartphones account for about seventy percent of all digital media time in this country. This isn't a secondary screen anymore — for most people it's the primary one.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: on a phone, looking turns into going.** Here's the number to memorize, because it's the whole argument for a local business. Nearly nine in ten "near me" searches happen on a phone — and roughly three-quarters of those lead to somebody visiting a business that same day. Same *day.*
**V1 — Guide:** Phone behavior isn't research behavior — it's about-to-do-something behavior. Somebody on a laptop is planning. Somebody on a phone is moving.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: it's the only device in the room when the decision gets made.** A desktop sits still. The phone is standing in a competitor's aisle, in the driveway, at the kid's game. Every one of those is a moment a business would pay to be part of.
**V1 — Guide:** One honest note on budget. Because one campaign is running several targeting methods at once, it needs enough weight behind it for each of them to actually reach anybody. Fund it thinly and every method whispers. If a client can't fund it properly, sell them a single-method campaign instead — geofencing on its own, or targeted display on its own. That's a real recommendation, and it makes you the person they trust.

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For
**ON-SCREEN:** Three real photos, one at a time — a paint or hardware store aisle, a quick-serve restaurant or coffee counter, a repeat-visit business like a car wash or nail salon. Each type's name and reason type in beside it.

**V1 — Guide:** Three easy targets.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses that lose customers inside a competitor's store.** Paint, hardware, auto parts, tires, pharmacy, furniture. The customer is standing somewhere else with a phone in their hand, and this is the only tactic that can talk to them right then.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses people decide on while they're out.** Restaurants, coffee, car washes, quick service, convenience. Nobody plans lunch at a desk on Tuesday morning. They decide at eleven forty-five, in a truck, on a phone.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses built on repeat visits.** Where store retargeting shines — somebody who already walked in once is the cheapest business in the building.
**V1 — Guide:** Honest flip side: a business whose customers decide slowly at a desk may do better elsewhere. And a client who can't fund it properly should buy one method instead.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics
**ON-SCREEN:** (1) Four labeled method paths building as text, all converging on a single phone inside one bracketed campaign box → *one campaign, several methods*. (2) One person's path drawn across three moments — competitor's store, then browsing at home, then the client's website — with a **green box** at each → *the signals stack*. (3) A geofence selection view, a pixel, and a report showing store visits → *manage and measure*. Closes on text: *One campaign, several methods · Stacked · Measured.*

**V1 — Guide:** Three moving parts.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: several methods, one campaign, one device.** Let's be precise about each method, because a client will ask what they're actually getting.
**V1 — Guide:** **Interest and behavior targeting** reaches people by what they've been browsing and shopping for — including **purchase intent**, signals that somebody's actively in the market rather than idly curious. **Geofencing** covers a limited number of chosen locations. **Ad and site retargeting** reaches people who've already seen the ad or visited the website. And **store retargeting** — optional, and the one nobody expects — reaches people who walked into the client's *own* store.
**V1 — Guide:** Sit with that last one. Every person who walks through a client's front door becomes an audience they can talk to next week. Most owners have never once considered that possible.
**V1 — Guide:** And again — those aren't four line items on a proposal. They're four ways the same campaign finds people.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: the methods stack — and this is why one combined campaign beats a single method.** Any one of them is useful on its own. Running together, they follow a real person through a real decision.
**V1 — Guide:** Saturday, somebody's in the big-box paint aisle — geofencing reaches them. Sunday night they're browsing paint colors at home — interest targeting. Monday they click through to the client's site — now site retargeting has them. Tuesday they walk in — and store retargeting keeps them for the next project.
**V1 — Guide:** That's one customer, reached four different ways, over four days — by one campaign. No single method does that, and it's the whole argument for the combined product.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: manage it and measure it.** Geofence locations get chosen deliberately with the client — a handful, picked for a reason, not a scattershot list. Audience and geographic targeting get set, and a conversion pixel goes on the site.
**V1 — Guide:** Then ongoing refinement, management, and reporting. Plus an option to measure **offline visits** — whether people who saw the ad turned up in person. On a mobile campaign that's the most honest measure there is, because a phone is what walks through the door.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and definition animate in as text, with green boxes on the four paths and on a store-visit report.

**V1 — Guide:** Four terms and you're fluent.
**V1 — Guide:** **Mobile Audience Targeting** — MAT. Several targeting approaches combined and delivered specifically to phones.
**V1 — Guide:** **Purchase intent** — signals that somebody is actively shopping for something rather than just interested in it. Reading about trucks is interest. Comparing truck prices for two weeks is intent.
**V1 — Guide:** **Ad retargeting** — reaching somebody again *because they were served the ad*, even if they never clicked it. Different from site retargeting, which requires them to have visited the website.
**V1 — Guide:** **Store retargeting** — reaching people who physically walked into the client's own store. Their front door becomes an audience.

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature list animates in. Four benefit lines: *Reach them where the day happens · Talk to them in a competitor's aisle · Their own visitors become an audience · Measure who walked in.* Payoff: a real big-box paint aisle photo beside a real mobile ad for a local paint store — plus a real photo of an independent paint store counter. Small tag: *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** What's in it? Interest, behavior, and purchase-intent targeting. Geofencing of chosen locations. Ad and site retargeting. Optional store retargeting. Audience and geographic targeting set with the client. Conversion pixel tracking. Ongoing refinement and management. Optional offline visit measurement. And reporting.
**V1 — Guide:** What does the owner get? They **reach people where the day actually happens** — on the phone. They can **talk to somebody standing in a competitor's aisle.** Their **own visitors become an audience** they can reach again. And they can **measure who walked in**, not just who clicked.
**V1 — Guide:** So picture an independent paint store. Forty years in business, better product knowledge than anybody, losing customers every weekend to the big-box store nine minutes away — not on price, on habit.
**V1 — Guide:** After: a fence on the big-box store, so anybody in that paint aisle started seeing the independent's ad. Interest targeting for people researching interior paint. And everybody who'd ever walked into the independent's own store kept hearing from them between projects.
**V2 — Scene:** *"I was literally standing there holding a can, and your ad came up saying you'd match it. So I put it back."*
**V1 — Guide:** — and that's about as close as advertising gets to standing in the aisle with them. *(Illustrative — not a promise of specific numbers.)*

### SEGMENT 7 — Recap + Quiz Bridge
**ON-SCREEN:** Recap text — 1: four doors · 2: stacked signals · 3: measured visits. *Quiz →* button slides up.

**V1 — Guide:** Three parts. One: one campaign, phones only, using several targeting methods together — interests and intent, geofenced locations, ad and site retargeting, and optionally store retargeting. Two: those methods stack, so one customer gets reached several ways across a real decision. Three: manage and measure, including whether people actually walked in.
**V1 — Guide:** And the two lines to carry in. **The phone is the only screen that goes where your customer goes.** And the one that gets an owner's attention: **their own front door can become an audience.**
**V1 — Guide:** That's Mobile Audience Targeting. Quick ten-question check. No pressure — you already know this.

## SECTION D — AE Field Notes *(not narrated)*

**How the advertiser pays.** Bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis**. Offline visit measurement is an optional add-on. Impression arithmetic is taught once, in Targeted Display.

**What this product actually is — get this right in a proposal.** MAT is **one mobile campaign that runs several targeting methods simultaneously.** It is not a menu, and it is not four campaigns. The methods included are interest/behavior/intent targeting, geofencing of a limited number of locations, and ad and site retargeting — with store retargeting available as an option. Describing it as "four tactics" invites a client to ask which ones they can drop, or to assume they're buying four products' worth of reach. Describe it as one campaign with several ways of finding the customer.

**Budget reality — be honest early.** Because several methods run at once, the campaign needs enough weight for each to reach anybody, which is why the entry point sits above the single-method products. **A client who can't fund it properly should buy one method instead** — geofencing alone, or targeted display alone, at a workable budget. That's the better sale and the better relationship.

**How it's sold.** The strongest opener in the catalog for retail competing against a big box: *"When your customer is standing in their paint aisle with a phone in their hand — right now — what can you do about it?"* Owners have no answer to that question, and this is the answer.

**Store retargeting is the part nobody expects.** Lead with it for any repeat-visit business. *"Everybody who walks through your front door becomes an audience you can reach next month."* Most owners have never considered it possible, and it reframes their own traffic as an asset.

**Bundles with:**
- **Geofence and event targeting** — same family; MAT packages geofencing in, so be clear about what's already included before selling it twice.
- **Targeted display** — same, and worth checking for overlap so a client isn't buying the same thing under two names.
- **CRM targeting** — the pairing that covers everything: reach known customers by list, reach mobile behavior by device.
- **Website / landing pages** — the pixel needs a site, and a mobile-fast landing page matters more here than anywhere.

**Objections and responses:**
- *"Isn't this the same as the geofencing you sold me?"* → Answer carefully and honestly: geofencing is one of the four pieces inside this. This adds interest and intent targeting, retargeting, and store retargeting. **Do not sell both without explaining the overlap.**
- *"My customers are older, they don't use phones."* → Check the age, then be straight. Older customers use phones heavily, but if the client's audience is genuinely offline, say so and sell something else.
- *"How do you know somebody walked into my store?"* → Careful and accurate: a device entered the area, and nothing about a person's identity is used or displayed. Same discipline as all geofencing.
- *"Can you fence the whole shopping center?"* → You can, and it's usually a bad idea — the fence catches everybody driving past. Tight fences, chosen on purpose.

**Lines for the room:**
- *"The phone is the only screen that goes where your customer goes."*
- *"When your customer is standing in their aisle with a phone in their hand, what can you do about it? This."*
- *"Everybody who walks through your front door becomes an audience."*
- *"Somebody on a laptop is planning. Somebody on a phone is moving."*

**Retention note.** Two risks. The usual click-column problem — offline visit measurement is the antidote, so sell it and make store visits the agreed measure. And **overlap confusion**: because MAT contains geofencing and display, a client who also buys those separately may feel double-charged. Map out exactly what's in which product at the first meeting; that conversation prevents a cancellation six months later.

**Writer's notes:**
- **Statistics sources:** US adults averaging roughly four to five hours a day on smartphones is from eMarketer and Statista via 2026 screen-time compilations. Smartphones accounting for about 70% of US digital media time appears across 2026 mobile-usage compilations. The "near me" figures — nearly 88% of such searches on mobile with roughly 76% leading to a same-day store visit — originate from **Google** consumer data and are widely reproduced. **The Google-sourced near-me figures are the strongest and most persuasive in this module; cite the source if a rep is challenged.** Attribute as "research shows" and re-verify annually. All three prove one idea: *the phone is where the day happens, and phone behavior leads to action.*
- **Deliberately excluded:** the CPM figure, the offline-visit add-on rate, the click-through estimate, the monthly minimum, and the contract term. The sheet's geofence allowance of a specific number of locations was also excluded as a package deliverable count — the script says "a limited number of chosen locations." **If you'd rather reps state the exact count, add it to Part 1 and it goes in.**
- **⚠️ Product overlap — worth a policy decision.** This package contains targeted display, geofencing, and retargeting, all of which are sold as standalone products elsewhere in the catalog (Rooms 13, 14a, 13's retargeting mechanic). A rep could easily sell a client both MAT and standalone geofencing without realizing they overlap. The script and Field Notes both flag it, but **a one-page internal comparison showing what lives in which product would prevent a lot of confusion** and is worth building before this module goes out.

Updated list: diner, roofer, flooring co., hearing clinic, insurance agency, storage co., garden center, medical clinic, auto repair shop, pest control co., RV dealership, used-car lot, physical therapy clinic, wedding/event venue, veterinary clinic, private school, clothing boutique, brewery/taproom, home builder, **paint store**.
