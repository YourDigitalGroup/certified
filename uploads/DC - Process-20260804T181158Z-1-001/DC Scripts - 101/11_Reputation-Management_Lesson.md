# Reputation Management — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Online Content Marketing (Room #11 in the Digital House)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime target:** ~8.75 minutes + 10-question quiz *(see runtime note in Section B)*
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.

## SECTION A — The Reusable Tactic Lesson Template
*(Use this exact shape for all ~25 tactic modules. Only the content changes — the beats never do.)*

Two questions before you write: **"What's the plain-English definition?"** and **"What are the 3 core mechanics?"** Everything else follows the beats.

**Voice convention (all modules):**
- **V1 — Guide:** the teaching spine. Carries every segment.
- **V2 — Scene:** short in-the-world lines only — a customer's search, a quoted reaction, a review snippet, and the quiz prompts. Its job is to signal a "mode switch" and keep the ear awake.
- *(Optional V3 later if you want a distinct quiz host — but two voices covers everything below.)*

**On-screen rule — the visual kit (all modules):** keep it clean and real. Three ingredients only:
1. **Animated text** — key words and short lines build/fade in as V1 speaks. This is the workhorse.
2. **Real photos** — actual screenshots of the product (real star ratings side by side in a search result, a real review thread with an owner's reply, a real QR code card on a counter, a real website review widget) and real photography of people/businesses (a service counter, a shop owner on a tablet, a waiting room). No clip-art, no cartoon figures.
3. **Green highlight box** — a green callout box drawn over a real screenshot to point at a specific section (same style as the sales sheet: box the star rating, box the review count, box the owner's reply), with a text label animating in beside it.

**No cheesy line illustrations, icon/emoji cards, or metaphor graphics** (no glowing houses, giant floating stars, thumbs-up drawings, little walking figures). Any analogy the Guide speaks stays *spoken only* — we don't draw it. Quiz cards being tappable is UI, not illustration, so that stays.

**Quiz blueprint (same every tactic):** Q1–3 = the 3 mechanics · Q4–6 = terms · Q7 = who it's good for + why · Q8 = why-it-matters scenario · Q9–10 = features/benefits (one may use the Segment-6 payoff).

**Answer placement rule (all modules):** vary which letter is correct — roughly even across A, B, C, and D, with no repeating pattern. If the correct answer sits at B nine times out of ten, trainees stop reading the options and start pattern-matching, and the quiz measures nothing. Also keep the wrong answers plausible; obviously silly distractors have the same effect.

**Payoff rules (Segment 6):** pick the easiest-to-picture business from Segment 3; keep outcomes plain and clearly *illustrative* — no invented numbers, no real names.

## SECTION B — Reputation Management: Full Script

> **Runtime note.** About 8:40, kept as a single module. Nothing was removed from the argument — the reduction from roughly 10:45 came from consolidating duplicated points and letting on-screen text carry numbers the voice-over was narrating twice. Specifically: the "thin profile looks unproven" beat was merged into the statistics run (the 47%-under-20-reviews figure *is* that argument); the "nobody trusts a flawless score" idea was stated three separate times and now appears twice, once as a statistic and once in the terms; the six statistics are delivered as one rapid-fire block rather than a beat each; and the confidence-to-sales mechanism dropped its weakest third effect. All six consumer statistics, the year-over-year trend, the revenue research, the ad-multiplier argument, the national benchmark, the insulation math, the ten-second prospecting method, and the full "owner hears first" mechanic are intact.
>
>
> **Statistics note — verify before recording.** The consumer figures in Segment 2 beat one (92% factor in star rating · 68% require 4+ stars · 31% require 4.5+ · 47% won't use a business with under 20 reviews · 74% only care about reviews from the last 90 days · ~10% demand a perfect 5.0) all come from BrightLocal's Local Consumer Review Survey 2026, a representative panel of roughly 1,000 US adults. The year-over-year jumps (55%→68% and 17%→31%) are from the same survey series. Revenue figure is Harvard Business School research using Yelp data. **These are single-source survey figures, not audited measurements — attribute them as "consumer research shows" rather than as laws of nature, and re-check them annually since this survey moves noticeably year to year.**
>
> One figure was requested and deliberately *not* included: a statistic on consumers who always choose the highest-rated business without reading any reviews. No credible source for that claim could be found, and the available data trends the other way (review-reading is increasing). The script makes the same point defensibly instead — 92% use the rating while only ~41% always read the text, so for most people the score and count *are* the review.
>
> **Language note for Mechanic 2 — the one thing to get exactly right.** The value of the feedback step is *sequence*, not suppression: the owner is notified first, and the customer remains free to post publicly. Written that way, it's both accurate and the strongest thing in the program. Written the other way — "we stop bad reviews from going up," "negatives get intercepted," "we screen them out" — it becomes a promise the product doesn't make and a claim that creates exposure for the advertiser who bought it. Same feature, and the accurate version actually sells better because it's specific. Reps should be able to say "you find out first" fluently and should never say "it doesn't get posted."
>
> **Benchmark note for the writer.** The script cites **4.4 as the national average** star rating for a local business on Google — sourced from BrightLocal's study of 93,000+ local businesses across 26 industries, and corroborated by SOCi at 4.42. It is stated as the *yardstick*, never as our goal. Being at 4.4 means being average. Two caveats worth keeping in the script: averages vary by industry (home services run higher, restaurants and hotels lower), and the number that actually matters is the top three competitors on the local map. Re-check the figure before recording; it drifts upward slowly year over year.
>
> Also referenced: Northwestern's Spiegel Research Center finds purchase likelihood peaks around 4.2–4.5 and *falls* toward a perfect 5.0, because shoppers read near-perfect scores as fake. That's why the goal is "comfortably above average with real volume," not "as close to 5.0 as possible."

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is *(0:00–0:40)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Real phone screenshot of a "near me" search showing three businesses. **Green boxes** on the star ratings and review counts: one at 4.6 with a few hundred reviews, one at 4.8 with six reviews, one at 3.9 with forty. Then a real review thread where an owner has replied to a complaint — green box on the reply. Then a real counter-top QR code card, green box on it.

**V2 — Scene:** *"Okay, this one's four-point-six with three hundred reviews. Let's go there."*
**V1 — Guide:** Watch what just happened. That person didn't read a single review. Two numbers — the score and how many — and the decision was made in about a second and a half.
**V1 — Guide:** So, plainly: **reputation management** is two jobs done together. Steadily getting more customers to leave reviews, and watching what's said everywhere so nothing sits there festering.
**V1 — Guide:** Word of mouth used to happen over a fence and then evaporate. Now it's written down, permanent, and sorted by star rating. Same force that always drove local business — it's just visible now, which means it can be managed.
**V1 — Guide:** And this is the easiest conversation in the catalog to start. Pull up the client and their two closest competitors on your phone, in the meeting. You won't have to say much.

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters *(0:40–2:25)*
**ON-SCREEN:** The six statistics build one at a time as large animated numbers with short labels, then the year-over-year jumps animate beside two of them (55%→68%, 17%→31%). Then real search results with **green boxes** on star ratings in the local pack. Then the insulation arithmetic builds as plain numbers.

**V1 — Guide:** So why does a business need this? Four things — and the first one is just numbers, so let them land.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: for a lot of people, the rating *is* the decision.** Ninety-two percent of consumers say a star rating factors into which local business they pick. Sixty-eight percent won't consider a business under four stars. Thirty-one percent won't go below four-and-a-half. Forty-seven percent won't use a business with fewer than twenty reviews — so the count alone can disqualify you. Seventy-four percent only care about reviews from the last three months, meaning old reviews expire. And only about ten percent want a perfect five — almost nobody wants flawless, they want believable.
**V1 — Guide:** Two things about those numbers. First, **the bar is moving fast** — that sixty-eight percent was fifty-five a year ago, and the thirty-one percent nearly doubled in twelve months. Consumers get pickier every year, so a business standing still is quietly sliding below the line. Second, most people don't actually read the reviews — only about four in ten always do. So the score and the count *are* the review. Nobody's reading the thoughtful paragraph the owner is proud of.
**V1 — Guide:** Add it up: a business can be the best in town at the actual work and get filtered out before a human being ever considers them. That's not losing a sale. That's not being on the list.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: better reviews make more money, and here's the mechanism.** Reviews do one job — they lower risk. You're asking a stranger to hand over their car, their teeth, their kid, or a deposit, on faith. Reviews are the only evidence that shrinks that gamble. So when the score goes up, more people say yes, and they haggle less — because a business everybody vouches for doesn't have to be the cheapest one.
**V1 — Guide:** Harvard research using Yelp data found a one-star improvement lines up with roughly five to nine percent more revenue. And here's the argument for any client already buying ads from you: a campaign pointing traffic at a three-point-nine with twelve reviews is burning money. That same budget pointed at a four-point-seven with two hundred recent reviews converts completely differently. **The score is a multiplier on everything else they buy.**
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: Google reads them too.** Review signals — score, count, recency, whether the owner replies — help decide who shows up in that little map at the top of a local search. So it's not only about persuading someone who found them. It's about **whether they get shown at all.** Two shops equally close and equally relevant: better reviews gets served first.
**V1 — Guide:** **Four: it's a math problem, which is good news, because math is solvable.** And start with the number to memorize: the national average for a local business on Google is about **four-point-four**, from a study of ninety thousand-plus businesses.
**V1 — Guide:** Hear what that means. Four-point-four is not a goal — **four-point-four is average.** Middle of the pack. A business below it is below average, sitting on a screen beside competitors who aren't. Though the number that matters most isn't the national one, it's the top three on the map in *their* town.
**V1 — Guide:** Now the arithmetic — do this out loud with a client. A shop with ten reviews at four-point-five takes one one-star. They're at four-point-two — one bad night cost three tenths, and a dozen good ones to climb back. Same shop with a hundred reviews at four-point-five takes that same one-star. Four-point-four-seven. Nobody notices. Nothing to recover from.
**V1 — Guide:** That's the strategy in one word: **insulation.** Comfortably above that national average, with real volume behind it — seventy-five to a hundred reviews or better. At that point a bad day stops being an emergency. And notice what protected them. Not deleting anything. *Volume.*

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For *(2:25–3:10)*
**ON-SCREEN:** A real phone screen looking up a business — **green boxes** on the score and the review count, then on the top three map competitors. Then three real photos one at a time — an auto service counter, a dental or legal office, a busy restaurant or salon. Each business type's name and its one-line reason type in beside the photo.

**V1 — Guide:** Here's where that four-point-four pays off for *you* — because it's the easiest prospecting tool you'll ever have.
**V1 — Guide:** You can qualify a business in ten seconds from your phone, in their parking lot before you walk in. Two numbers: score and count. Under four-point-four? Below the national average — and now that's a fact you can state, not an opinion you have to argue. Good score but a handful of reviews? Fragile. Then glance at the top three competitors on the map, and you've got *their* gap, in *their* market, in numbers they can verify while you sit there. You can build a week of calls from a coffee shop.
**V1 — Guide:** Three easy targets.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses below that average, or with one bad review sitting out front.** The math is most fixable and the pain is already real.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses where the whole purchase is trust.** Auto repair, dentists, attorneys, home services, childcare. A stranger is deciding whether to hand these people their car, their teeth, or their kid. Reviews are the only evidence available.
**V1 — Guide:** **Busy businesses whose happy customers never say anything.** Restaurants, salons, retail. Hundreds of pleased people a week and almost none of them writes a word. That's not a reputation problem, it's a *collection* problem — the easiest kind to fix.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics *(3:10–6:35)*
**ON-SCREEN:** (1) A real monthly request email, a real custom QR code, then a real point-of-purchase card on a counter → label *ask everybody, always*. (2) A timeline builds as text — *customer unhappy → owner emailed same day → owner calls → customer decides what to post* — with a **green box** on the gap between steps two and four, sub-label *the window*; then a real alert email in an owner's inbox → label *owner hears first*. (3) Real review pages across several platforms, a real website widget, a real owner reply → label *watch everywhere, answer*. Closes on text: *Ask · Hear it first · Answer.*

**V1 — Guide:** Three moving parts. Just three.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: ask everybody, and keep asking.** Volume is the strategy, so the engine is built around asking constantly.
**V1 — Guide:** Every month we get the client's recent customer list and send a review request to all of them. Not once at signup — every month, forever, because there's a fresh batch of customers every month. And remember that seventy-four percent who only care about recent reviews? That's why it can't be a one-time push. Reviews expire, so the asking can't stop.
**V1 — Guide:** Then a **custom QR code** for the business, so somebody with a phone in hand is two taps from a review page. And optionally **point-of-purchase QR codes in the building** — a card at the register, a tent card on the table, a sticker on the service window. That's the highest-percentage moment there is: they're standing right there, just had a good experience, phone already out. Owners worry their customers will be annoyed to be asked. They aren't. Most people are glad to — nobody ever asked them before.
**V1 — Guide:** And we ask **everybody**, not just the ones we expect to say something nice. That's not us being noble, it's the math — you can't reach a hundred reviews by being choosy. So if a client asks whether we can keep the bad ones off his page, you've got a better answer than yes or no: *we don't hide bad reviews, we out-number them.*
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: the owner hears about it first.** Slow down here — this is the most valuable thing we do, and most reps breeze right past it.
**V1 — Guide:** Think about how an owner normally finds out he had an unhappy customer. He doesn't. He finds out in three weeks, scrolling his phone at nine at night, and there it is — one star, in public, in front of every future customer, permanently, from somebody long gone. Nothing to do. Already carved in stone. That's the real problem — not that unhappy customers exist, every business has those. It's that the owner is always the *last* to know.
**V1 — Guide:** Our program changes the order of events. When we ask a customer for feedback, they tell us how it went right there, before anything gets published anywhere. So the moment somebody says the wait was forty minutes or the part was wrong, **that lands in the owner's inbox the same day** — not in three weeks, not in public, while that customer is still reachable and still holding a phone.
**V1 — Guide:** And be precise, because the precision is what makes it sellable. We're not stopping anybody from posting — that customer is completely free to put it up publicly, and some will. What changed is **who finds out first**: the owner instead of the internet. That's the whole trick, and it's worth a fortune.
**V1 — Guide:** Because it buys him a **window** — a few hours between "this customer is unhappy" and "this customer has decided what to tell the world." That window is where every bit of service recovery in business history happens. He calls. He apologizes. He redoes the work, refunds the part, comps the meal.
**V1 — Guide:** And here's what anybody who's run a business already knows is true: most one-star reviews aren't written by people who hate a business. They're written by people who had a fixable problem and no way to say so. Somebody who gets a real phone call from the owner that afternoon usually doesn't have much left to be angry about. Some still post — fine, we're insulated by volume. The fixable ones get fixed.
**V1 — Guide:** So here's the line for the room, and write this one down: *"Right now you find out you had an unhappy customer by reading it on Google three weeks later, in public, permanently. With this, you find out that afternoon, in an email, while you can still pick up the phone."* Watch his face. That's the sale.
**V1 — Guide:** And that feedback is worth more than any single review saved — one complaint about the wait is a bad day, three is a staffing problem he didn't know he had. It's the only honest feedback most owners ever get, because unhappy customers rarely complain to your face. They just quietly never come back.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: watch everywhere, and answer.** Google matters most but isn't the whole picture, and this is where reps under-sell the program.
**V1 — Guide:** Depending on the business, reviews also live on Facebook, on Yelp, and on the site that matters *specifically to their industry* — doctors have theirs, restaurants and hotels have theirs, contractors and lawyers have theirs. We monitor all of them, so a scathing review on some industry site the owner has never logged into doesn't sit there for two years quietly costing him business.
**V1 — Guide:** Then we put those reviews to work. A **widget** on their website pulls in current reviews, so the good ones are selling on their own site instead of only living on Google.
**V1 — Guide:** And we reply — briefly and like a human to the good ones, and to the bad ones, which matters most. A complaint with a calm, decent owner reply underneath it doesn't read like a warning anymore. The next customer isn't reading the complaint. They're reading the response.
**V1 — Guide:** That's it. Ask, hear it first, answer.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms *(6:35–7:15)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and its one-line definition animate in as text, one at a time. **Green box** on the real screenshots where relevant — the average score, the review count and dates, the owner reply, several platform logos.

**V1 — Guide:** Five terms so you sound like you know your stuff — because now you do.
**V1 — Guide:** **Star rating** — the average score, and the most looked-at number about any business online. Two reference points: the national average is about four-point-four, and the trust sweet spot sits a little *under* perfect.
**V1 — Guide:** **Review volume and velocity** — volume is how many, velocity is how recently and steadily they arrive. A hundred reviews that all stopped four years ago is a business that looks like it *used* to be good.
**V1 — Guide:** **Solicitation** — a fancy word for asking. Emails, texts, QR codes, cards on the counter. The engine of the program.
**V1 — Guide:** **Review response** — the owner's public reply under a review. Customers read it, Google notices it, almost nobody does it.
**V1 — Guide:** **Review platforms** — everywhere reviews live. Google first, then Facebook, then whatever site rules their industry. Ask a client which one their customers actually use; sometimes it isn't the one you'd guess.

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff *(7:15–8:15)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature names animate in as a text list, green box ticking to each on the real screenshots. Then four benefit lines build as text: *Get chosen instead of skipped · Get shown higher · Hear it first, not last · Protected by the math.* For the payoff: two real auto-shop screenshots side by side — **before** (3.8 stars, a dozen reviews, an angry one on top with no reply) and **after** (4.5, well over a hundred, recent, owner replies visible) — plus a real photo of a service counter. A small text tag reads *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** So what's in it? Monthly review requests by email and text to the customer list the client gives us each month — that regular collection is what keeps the engine fed. A custom QR code. Optional point-of-purchase cards and signs for the counter or the table. Monitoring across the platforms that matter for their industry, not just Google. Review management — reading, tracking, responding. A widget for their website. And reporting so they can watch the score and the count move.
**V1 — Guide:** What does the owner get? Four things. They **get chosen instead of skipped**, because two-thirds of people won't consider a business under four stars. They **get shown higher**, since review signals feed the map results. They **hear about problems first** — same day, in private, instead of on Google in three weeks. And they end up **protected by the math**, which is the one to close on.
**V1 — Guide:** Under the hood it's simple: ask enough people often enough and a good business's real score floats up to where it belongs. We're not manufacturing a reputation. We're collecting the one they already earned, from people who liked them and never got asked.
**V1 — Guide:** So picture a family auto repair shop. Great mechanics, thirty years in town. Before: three-point-eight stars off twelve reviews, and the newest was a furious customer from two years ago about a misdiagnosed transmission, no reply underneath. Everybody searching for a mechanic read that first.
**V1 — Guide:** After: QR cards at the counter and on the invoice. A monthly request to everybody they served. The owner replying — including going back and answering that old angry one, calmly. And when a customer used the private feedback door about a long wait, the owner called him that afternoon.
**V2 — Scene:** *"You've got way more reviews than the other place — and they're recent."*
**V1 — Guide:** — and that's the whole thing. The score stopped losing them customers and started winning them. The shop didn't change. The scoreboard finally matched the shop. *(Illustrative — not a promise of specific numbers.)*

### SEGMENT 7 — Recap + Quiz Bridge *(8:15–8:40)*
**ON-SCREEN:** The three mechanics recap as animated text — 1: ask everybody · 2: hear it first · 3: watch everywhere and answer — with the real screenshots behind them and the green box tapping each area one last time. A *Quiz →* button slides up.

**V1 — Guide:** Three parts. One: ask everybody, every month — email, text, QR codes at the counter — because reviews expire and the asking can't stop. Two: the owner hears about it first, the same day, which buys him the window to actually fix something. Three: watch everywhere, put the reviews on their website, and answer them — especially the bad ones.
**V1 — Guide:** And two things to carry into a room. The number: four-point-four is the national average, so four-point-four is *average* — below it is below average, and that's a fact, not an opinion. And the pitch: this is a math problem. Get them above that average with seventy-five to a hundred reviews behind it, and one bad day stops being an emergency. Volume is the armor.
**V1 — Guide:** That's reputation management. Quick ten-question check to lock it in. No pressure — you already know this.

## SECTION C — Quiz (10 Questions)
**ON-SCREEN:** One card per question, four tappable options; correct choice highlights green on select. **V2 — Scene** reads each question stem aloud.

**1.** A shop with 10 reviews at 4.5 stars gets one 1-star review, dropping to about 4.2. A shop with 100 reviews at 4.5 gets the same 1-star. What happens?
A) It drops just as much, since one review is one review
B) It barely moves — around 4.47 — which is why volume is the strategy ✅
C) The review is automatically removed
D) Its rating goes up

**2.** Why does the program collect the client's customer list every month rather than just once at signup?
A) Because review links expire after 30 days
B) Because Google requires a monthly upload
C) To replace the need for QR codes
D) There's a fresh batch of customers every month, and volume only builds if the asking never stops ✅

**3.** What does the feedback step actually change for a business owner?
A) It prevents unhappy customers from posting reviews
B) It removes negative reviews already published
C) It changes who finds out first — the owner the same day, instead of reading it on Google weeks later ✅
D) It replaces the need to respond to reviews

**4.** "Review velocity" refers to…
A) How fast the business replies to reviews
B) How recently and steadily new reviews are arriving ✅
C) How quickly a review is posted after a purchase
D) The total number of reviews

**5.** Why does a business with 6 five-star reviews often look worse to a customer than one with 200 reviews at 4.6?
A) Because five stars is a bad score
B) Because older reviews count more
C) Because six reviews looks unproven — volume reads as evidence ✅
D) Because Google hides businesses with few reviews

**6.** Besides Google, where else should a business's reviews be monitored?
A) Nowhere — Google is the only one that counts
B) Only on the business's own website
C) Only on Facebook
D) Facebook, Yelp, and whichever site matters most in that specific industry ✅

**7.** You look up a prospect and see 4.1 stars with 22 reviews. What does that tell you?
A) They're above the national average and don't need help
B) The score is fine; only the review count matters
C) They're below the national average of about 4.4 *and* thin on volume — a strong prospect on both counts ✅
D) Nothing useful without seeing the individual reviews

**8.** Two shops are equally close and equally relevant to a search. One has more reviews, a better score, and recent activity. What most likely happens?
A) Google shows them in alphabetical order
B) The one with the better review profile gets shown first ✅
C) Reviews have no effect on which businesses appear
D) Google shows whichever spends more on ads

**9.** Why is replying to a negative review worth doing?
A) It gets the review deleted
B) It hides the review from search results
C) The next customer reads the response, and a calm reply makes the business look like it cares ✅
D) It's required by Google

**10.** In the auto-shop example, what actually changed?
A) They hired all new mechanics
B) They got the old negative review removed
C) They lowered their labor rate
D) They started asking everyone, added QR cards, replied to reviews, and let volume raise the score ✅

**Answer key:** 1-B · 2-D · 3-C · 4-B · 5-C · 6-D · 7-C · 8-B · 9-C · 10-D
