# Event Targeting Ads — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Targeted Digital Marketing (Room #14b in the Digital House — the companion to Geofence Targeting)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime:** 1353 counted spoken words ≈ **9:01** at 150 wpm
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.
**Spine — the one sentence a trainee must repeat cold:** *The event does your targeting. The thirty days after does your selling.*

## SECTION B — Event Targeting: Full Script

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of a crowded expo hall or fairground. Then a real map with a **green boundary** around the venue, and a clock graphic showing a start and end time. Then a real display ad on a phone.

**V2 — Scene:** *"There have to be a thousand people in here."*
**V1 — Guide:** Saturday morning at the fairgrounds. A bridal expo, a home show, a farm show, a youth tournament — pick your event. And every single person who walked through that door told you something by showing up.
**V1 — Guide:** Nobody wanders into a bridal expo by accident. Nobody buys a ticket to a home and garden show unless they own a home and have something in mind. The event already sorted the crowd for you.
**V1 — Guide:** So, plainly: **event targeting** is drawing a boundary around a venue *for a specific window of time* and advertising to the phones inside it — then continuing to reach those people for up to about thirty days after they go home.
**V1 — Guide:** If that sounds like geofencing, good — it's the same family, bought the same way, on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis.** The difference is that a standing geofence is about a *place*, and this is about a *moment.* A competitor's parking lot is there every day. The expo is there Saturday, and then it's gone.
**V1 — Guide:** Which is exactly why it works. **The event does your targeting for you.** You're not guessing who might be interested — the ticket already proved it.

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of an expo crowd. Then three statistics build as animated numbers. Then a simple timeline: *event day → weeks of deciding → purchase*, with a **green box** over the weeks in the middle.

**V1 — Guide:** So why should an advertiser do this? Three reasons.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: the crowd is pre-qualified, and it's a crowd of strangers.** At a trade show or expo, roughly eight in ten attendees have the authority to actually buy — these aren't browsers, they're buyers. And about two-thirds of them are people the exhibiting businesses have never reached before. New prospects, already sorted by interest, standing in one building.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: the decision doesn't happen at the event — and that's the whole argument for this product.** Roughly six in ten attendees make their purchase decision within three months *after* attending. Nobody books a wedding venue at the expo. Nobody signs a siding contract at the home show. They collect brochures, go home, and decide over the next few weeks.
**V1 — Guide:** So think about what that means. Every business at that event spends money to be in the room on Saturday, and then goes completely silent for the exact weeks when the decision is actually being made. This is the tactic that doesn't go silent.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: a client doesn't have to be at the event to advertise to it.** This is the part owners find surprising and reps forget to say. No booth. No table. No sponsorship. If there's a farm show in town this weekend, a client can advertise to everybody in it without buying anything from the organizer.
**V1 — Guide:** One honest note to keep you clean: showing up in somebody's feed is not the same as shaking their hand at a booth. This is presence and familiarity, not a lead. Say it that way and you'll never oversell it.

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For
**ON-SCREEN:** Three real photos, one at a time — a bridal or home expo floor, a youth tournament field with out-of-town families, a convention or farm show hall. Each type's name and reason type in beside it.

**V1 — Guide:** Three easy targets.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses whose customers gather at a themed event.** Wedding venues, photographers, and florists at a bridal expo. Contractors, window companies, and landscapers at a home show. Implement dealers at a farm show. The event's whole theme is the qualifier.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses that benefit from visitors in town.** Hotels, restaurants, retail, attractions. A regional tournament or a concert fills the town with people who need dinner and a place to stay, and they're all looking at their phones.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses selling to a specific trade or profession.** A conference or association meeting puts an entire industry in one building for two days. For anyone selling business-to-business in a small market, that's a year's worth of prospecting in a weekend.
**V1 — Guide:** And the honest flip side: if there aren't meaningful events where a client's customers gather, this isn't the tactic — a standing geofence on a competitor or a point of interest will serve them better. Also, event campaigns are built around a calendar, so a client who wants something running *this week* with no event in sight needs a different answer.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics
**ON-SCREEN:** (1) A calendar of local events with three circled, then a map boundary around one venue → *pick the event*. (2) A timeline: event window → weeks after, with a phone, laptop, and tablet linked by a **green bracket** → *the window and the tail*. (3) Two ads side by side — one generic, one referencing the event context — green box on the second → *match the moment*. Closes on text: *Pick the event · Run the window, keep the tail · Match the moment.*

**V1 — Guide:** Three moving parts.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: pick the event.** We choose the event and the venue together with the client, and the choosing is the strategy. The best events are the ones where attending *proves* something — a bridal expo proves a wedding is coming, a farm show proves somebody farms.
**V1 — Guide:** A general-admission concert is a weaker fit, because all you've learned is that somebody likes music. Same technology, much fuzzier audience. So aim for events with a theme.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: run the window, then keep the tail.** The boundary is live for a set **event window** — the hours the event is actually happening. People inside it during that window become the audience.
**V1 — Guide:** Then they go home, and this is where the money is made: they stay reachable for **up to about thirty days**, and **across all their devices.** That's **cross-device matching** — the fence caught a phone in a crowded hall, but that person does their real deciding at home on a laptop. Linking those devices to the same household means the ad follows the person, not the phone.
**V1 — Guide:** Line that up with the fact that most of those decisions get made in the weeks after the event, and you can see the shape of it. The event gets you the audience. The tail is when you actually sell to them.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: match the moment.** Because we know what event somebody attended, the ad can speak to it. A wedding venue's ad in the weeks after a bridal expo can talk about booking a date, not about being a nice building. That relevance is most of what makes this outperform a generic banner.
**V1 — Guide:** Then the usual housekeeping: a daily schedule, conversion tracking, and weekly refinement while the tail runs.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and definition animate in as text, with green boxes on the venue boundary, the window clock, and the linked devices.

**V1 — Guide:** Four terms and you're fluent.
**V1 — Guide:** **Event targeting** — advertising to the people inside a specific venue during a specific event. Same technology as a geofence, aimed at a moment instead of a place.
**V1 — Guide:** **Event window** — the block of time the boundary is live. Outside those hours, nobody's being added.
**V1 — Guide:** **Post-event retargeting** — continuing to serve ads to attendees after they've gone home, for up to about thirty days. The tail, and the part that does the work.
**V1 — Guide:** **Cross-device matching** — connecting the phone that was at the event to the other screens in that household, so the ad reaches the person on the laptop and tablet too. Device-to-household, not identifying individuals.

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature list animates in. Four benefit lines: *A pre-qualified crowd · No booth required · Reach them while they decide · Every screen, not just the phone.* Payoff: a real photo of an expo floor beside a real ad on a laptop — plus a real photo of an event venue. Small tag: *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** What's in it? The event and venue chosen with the client. Ads served to attendees during the event window. Post-event retargeting for up to about thirty days. Cross-device delivery across all their screens. A daily ad schedule. Conversion tracking. Weekly refinement while the campaign runs. And reporting.
**V1 — Guide:** What does the owner get? **A pre-qualified crowd** the event sorted for them. **No booth required** — no table, no sponsorship, no fee to the organizer. They **reach those people during the weeks the decision actually happens.** And they reach them on **every screen**, not just the phone that was in the building.
**V1 — Guide:** So picture a wedding and event venue — a restored barn outside of town, gorgeous, and almost nobody knows it exists. Before: the owner paid for a booth at the regional bridal expo every year, handed out a few hundred cards, and hoped.
**V1 — Guide:** After: same booth, but now everybody who walked that expo floor kept seeing the venue for the next month — on their phone, and on the laptop where they were actually building a spreadsheet of venues and comparing dates.
**V2 — Scene:** *"I picked up your card at the show, and then you kept turning up while we were deciding."*
**V1 — Guide:** — and that's the difference. The card went in a tote bag with thirty others. The ad was still there in week three. *(Illustrative — not a promise of specific numbers.)*

### SEGMENT 7 — Recap + Quiz Bridge
**ON-SCREEN:** Recap text — 1: pick the event · 2: run the window, keep the tail · 3: match the moment. *Quiz →* button slides up.

**V1 — Guide:** Three parts. One: pick the event — the best ones are where attending proves something. Two: run the window, then keep the tail — up to about thirty days, across every device in the household. Three: match the moment, because you know what they went to.
**V1 — Guide:** And the line to carry in: **the event does your targeting, and the thirty days after does your selling.** Also remember — no booth required, and it's bought on a cost-per-thousand impression basis like the rest of display.
**V1 — Guide:** That's event targeting. Quick ten-question check. No pressure — you already know this.

## SECTION D — AE Field Notes *(not narrated)*

**How the advertiser pays.** Bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis**, like the rest of the display family. The impression arithmetic is taught once, in the Targeted Display module; here it's one line.

**How it's sold — and this is the best part.** Event targeting is the easiest *proactive* pitch in the catalog, because you don't wait for the client to have a need — you look at the calendar. Build a list of every meaningful event in the market for the next twelve months: fairs, expos, home and garden shows, farm shows, bridal shows, tournaments, concerts, conventions, festivals. That list is a year of call reasons, and every one comes with a natural deadline, which is the thing most digital tactics lack. Sell it the way you already sell around a local event.

**Bundles with:**
- **Geofence targeting** — same machinery, and the natural pairing: fence the competitor year-round, fence the expo in March.
- **Targeted display** — event audiences fold into a broader display program.
- **SEM** — attendees who go home and search need to find the client at the top.
- **Website / landing pages** — an event-specific landing page makes the "match the moment" mechanic much stronger.

**Objections and responses:**
- *"I already have a booth there."* → "Good — this makes the booth work harder. The card goes in a tote bag with thirty others. The ad is still there in week three."
- *"I can't afford a booth."* → "You don't need one. No table, no sponsorship, no fee to the organizer, and you still reach everybody in the building."
- *"The event's only one day."* → "The event is one day. The campaign is a month. The decision happens in that month."
- *"How is this different from the geofencing you sold me?"* → "Same technology. That one's about a place that's there every day. This one's about a moment that isn't."

**Lines for the room:**
- *"The event does your targeting. The thirty days after does your selling."*
- *"Nobody wanders into a bridal expo by accident."*
- *"You don't need a booth to advertise to everybody in the building."*
- *"Every business at that show goes silent the day it ends — right when people start deciding."*

**Retention note.** Two risks. First, the usual click-column problem — pre-frame that this is an impression product. Second, and specific to this tactic: because campaigns are event-shaped, they naturally end, and a client can drift away without ever formally cancelling. Fix that at the first meeting by selling from the calendar rather than one event at a time — book the year's events up front so the program has continuity instead of stopping and starting.

**Writer's notes:**
- **Statistics sources:** ~81% of attendees having buying authority and ~67% representing new prospects are CEIR figures, widely reproduced across 2026 trade show statistics compilations. ~59% making a purchase decision within three months of attending appears across the same compilations. **All are trade show and expo research — B2B-weighted — and they're survey findings, not audited measurements.** The script is careful to say "at a trade show or expo," which is accurate. Reps should apply the logic, not the exact figures, to consumer events like fairs and tournaments. Attribute as "research shows" and re-verify annually.
- **Deliberately excluded:** the CPM figure, the click-through estimate, the ad-set design fee, and the per-event minimum and contract terms.
- **The thirty-day window is kept** as a product capability, not a billing term. Confirm the current window before recording.
- **Compliance note:** as with all geofencing, reps say "a device was at the event," never "we know who attended." Cross-device matching is household-level linking, not identification. Keep that language consistent.
- **Terms count is four, not five,** by choice — a fifth would have been filler, and this module leans on Geofence Targeting for the shared fundamentals rather than re-teaching them.
- **Payoff vertical (wedding and event venue)** is new. Updated list: diner, roofer, flooring co., hearing clinic, insurance agency, storage co., garden center, medical clinic, auto repair shop, pest control co., RV dealership, used-car lot, physical therapy clinic, **wedding/event venue**.
