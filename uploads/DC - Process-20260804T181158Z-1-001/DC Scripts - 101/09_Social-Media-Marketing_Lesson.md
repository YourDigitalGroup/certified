# Social Media Marketing — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Online Content Marketing (Room #9 in the Digital House)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime target:** ~9.5 minutes + 10-question quiz *(see runtime note in Section B)*
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.

## SECTION A — The Reusable Tactic Lesson Template
*(Use this exact shape for all ~25 tactic modules. Only the content changes — the beats never do.)*

Two questions before you write: **"What's the plain-English definition?"** and **"What are the 3 core mechanics?"** Everything else follows the beats.

**Voice convention (all modules):**
- **V1 — Guide:** the teaching spine. Carries every segment.
- **V2 — Scene:** short in-the-world lines only — a customer's search, a quoted reaction, a review snippet, and the quiz prompts. Its job is to signal a "mode switch" and keep the ear awake.
- *(Optional V3 later if you want a distinct quiz host — but two voices covers everything below.)*

**On-screen rule — the visual kit (all modules):** keep it clean and real. Three ingredients only:
1. **Animated text** — key words and short lines build/fade in as V1 speaks. This is the workhorse.
2. **Real photos** — actual screenshots of the product (real business posts, a real boosted post with the "Sponsored" label, a real content calendar view) and real photography of people/businesses (a garden center in spring, a salon, a shop owner taking a photo on a phone). No clip-art, no cartoon figures.
3. **Green highlight box** — a green callout box drawn over a real screenshot to point at a specific section (same style as the sales sheet: box the Sponsored label, box the comment thread, box the Learn More button), with a text label animating in beside it.

**No cheesy line illustrations, icon/emoji cards, or metaphor graphics** (no glowing houses, megaphone drawings, floating thumbs-up, little walking figures). Any analogy the Guide speaks stays *spoken only* — we don't draw it. Quiz cards being tappable is UI, not illustration, so that stays.

**Quiz blueprint (same every tactic):** Q1–3 = the 3 mechanics · Q4–6 = terms · Q7 = who it's good for + why · Q8 = why-it-matters scenario · Q9–10 = features/benefits (one may use the Segment-6 payoff).

**Answer placement rule (all modules):** vary which letter is correct — roughly even across A, B, C, and D, with no repeating pattern. If the correct answer sits at B nine times out of ten, trainees stop reading the options and start pattern-matching, and the quiz measures nothing. Also keep the wrong answers plausible; obviously silly distractors have the same effect.

**Payoff rules (Segment 6):** pick the easiest-to-picture business from Segment 3; keep outcomes plain and clearly *illustrative* — no invented numbers, no real names.

## SECTION B — Social Media Marketing: Full Script

> **Runtime note:** about 8:05, and the length is almost entirely Mechanic 1, where the channels get explained. That's the part reps most need and most lack — an AE who can't tell an owner why their business belongs on one platform and not another can't sell this. If you need it shorter, the trim seam is the channel rundown: keep Facebook and the "visual pair," and compress the remaining channels into one sentence. That saves roughly a minute.
>
> **Package-specifics note — check the tier before recording.** This script names three concrete numbers: two channels, eight posts a month, two of them boosted. Two channels holds across every tier on the sheet. Eight posts a month matches the sheet's cadence across tiers. **Two boosts is the entry tier only** — the sheet shows the step-up tiers boosting twice that. So as written, this module teaches the entry package. Either keep it and have reps say "at the starting level, and it scales from there," or record the boost count as a range. Also note the tradeoff you're accepting: naming counts makes the pitch concrete, and it means the module needs re-recording whenever packaging changes. Every other module in this catalog deliberately avoids counts for that reason.
>
> **Scope note:** this module is the *management* side — the strategist, the calendar, the content, the posting, the boosting. Paid social advertising gets its own module. Mechanic 3 and Segment 7 both say plainly that management and ads together are the one-two punch, and point forward to that lesson. Keep those references so the two modules sell each other.
>
> **Demographics note:** the channel sheet is full of percentages. This script deliberately teaches the *shape* of each audience — older, younger, professional, visual — rather than the numbers. Two reasons: a rep quoting a stat from memory in a room will get it wrong, and "Facebook is where the grown-ups with money are" is what actually helps them pick a channel. Keep the sheet as the leave-behind and let it carry the figures.

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is *(0:00–0:45)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Real phone screenshot of a small business's social feed — a post with a decent photo, a few likes, and one comment nobody answered. A **green box** draws around the unanswered comment, label *asked two days ago.* Then the same account's page showing the last post was months ago — **green box** on the date. Then a real screenshot of a boosted post with the *Sponsored* label, green box on that label.

**V2 — Scene:** *"Do you all still do this? Hello?"*
**V1 — Guide:** Two days. That comment's been sitting there two days. And it's not a troll or a complaint — it's a person with money in their pocket asking a question, in public, where everybody can see it go unanswered.
**V1 — Guide:** Scroll up a little and it gets worse. The last post was in March. It's August. To anybody who wanders by, this business looks closed.
**V1 — Guide:** So, plainly: **social media marketing** is showing up regularly on the platforms where a business's customers already spend their time — posting things worth seeing, actually talking to people who respond, and putting a little money behind the posts that deserve a bigger audience.
**V1 — Guide:** Here's the analogy. Social media is the coffee shop where the whole town hangs out. Being *in* the room isn't the goal — anybody can make an account in four minutes. The goal is being the one people are glad to see, showing up often enough that they remember you exist.
**V1 — Guide:** And nearly every owner you talk to already has an account. They set it up. They meant to keep it going. Then they got busy running a business, which is what they should be doing. You're not selling them a new idea. You're selling them the follow-through.

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters *(0:45–1:50)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of someone scrolling on a phone on a couch. Then two real business accounts side by side — one posting regularly with replies visible, one dormant with an old post and no replies. **Green boxes** flag the difference. Then a real screenshot showing a post with modest reach versus the same post boosted, green box on the Sponsored label. Stakes text builds: *nobody's searching for them today → dormant looks closed → posting isn't the same as being seen.*

**V1 — Guide:** So why does a business need this? Three reasons.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: search only catches people already looking.** Everything in the search rooms of the Digital House depends on somebody deciding they need something and typing it in. That's great — but on any given Tuesday, almost nobody in town is searching for a garden center or a salon. Social gets in front of people *before* they're looking, so when the time comes, the business is already familiar. That's the whole difference: search catches demand, social creates it.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: a dormant account is worse than no account.** This is the part owners genuinely don't know. If the last post was in March, a customer doesn't think "they're busy." They think "are these people still open?" A neglected page actively costs them — same as wrong hours on their listing. It's a lit sign in a dark window.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three — and this is the one that surprises everybody: posting is not the same as being seen.** An owner posts something, three people like it, and they conclude social media doesn't work.
**V1 — Guide:** Here's what's actually happening. These platforms don't show a business's post to everybody who follows it. Not close. They show it to a slice, see if that slice reacts, and decide from there. Which means a genuinely good post can quietly die in front of almost nobody. That's not the owner's fault and it isn't bad luck — it's how the platforms make money. Which is exactly why the paid piece matters, and we'll get there.
**V1 — Guide:** So the stakes: they're invisible to people who aren't looking yet, they look closed to the ones who wander by, and even when they do post, hardly anyone sees it.
**V1 — Guide:** One more thing, and it matters because you'll be selling websites to these same people. Social is necessary — every small business needs it and needs a plan for it. It is not *sufficient*. Social is where somebody notices them and remembers them. The website is where somebody checks them out and buys. Different jobs. An owner who thinks social is the whole plan is reaching a sliver of their own followers and has nowhere to send anybody. It's both, not either — and that's the same conversation from the website lesson, pointed the other direction.

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For *(1:50–2:35)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Three real photos appear one at a time — a plated dish or a salon chair mid-transformation, a coffee shop counter with regulars, a garden center in spring with seasonal signage. Each business type's name and its one-line reason type in beside the photo.

**V1 — Guide:** So who do you picture pitching this to? Three easy ones.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses people like to look at.** Restaurants, salons, boutiques, gyms, med spas, remodelers. Their work *is* the content — a plate of food, a haircut before and after, a finished kitchen. They're sitting on material and don't realize it.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses that live on regulars.** Coffee shops, local retail, vets, nonprofits, churches. Nobody needs them today, but everybody needs them eventually. Social is how they stay in somebody's head during the gap.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses with something to announce constantly.** Garden centers, car dealers, event venues, anybody seasonal. New inventory, a weekend special, an event — the calendar practically writes itself.
**V1 — Guide:** Notice the thread: something to *show*, or something to *say*, on a regular basis. And here's the honest flip side — a business with neither is a grind. A concrete supplier selling to three contractors doesn't need this, and telling them so is how you stay somebody they trust.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics *(2:35–6:15)*
**ON-SCREEN:** (1) Real logos/screenshots of each platform appear as it's named, with a one-line audience descriptor typing in beside it → label *pick the right rooms*. (2) A real content calendar view with next month's slots filled → sub-label *organization*; then two real posts side by side — one all-caps promotional, one genuinely useful — **green box** on the useful one, sub-label *proactive writing*; then a real comment thread with an owner replying → label *show up worth seeing*. (3) A real post shown normally, then the same post with the *Sponsored* label and a wider audience → label *put money behind the winners*. Closes on text: *Right rooms · Worth seeing · Money behind it.*

**V1 — Guide:** How does this actually work? Three moving parts. Just three.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: pick the right rooms.** Every platform has a different crowd in it, and the single most common mistake is trying to be on all of them. Being decent in two beats being invisible in six. So here's the quick tour, and you only need the gist of each.
**V1 — Guide:** **Facebook** is the town square, and for most local businesses it's the starting point — not a debate. It's got the widest spread of ages, and critically, it's the one where people in their fifties, sixties, and beyond actually are. That's the crowd with houses, cars, and money to spend. The mood is casual and social — neighbors, groups, events, "does anybody know a good plumber."
**V1 — Guide:** **Instagram** is the picture window. Same company as Facebook, so they're usually run together — that's often the natural second channel. Younger-leaning, creative, visual. If a business is good-looking, this is where it shines.
**V1 — Guide:** **Pinterest** is the planning board. People go there to plan something — a wedding, a remodel, a garden — often well before they buy. Skews strongly female, very aspirational. Terrific for anything about a project.
**V1 — Guide:** **LinkedIn** is the office. Formal, career-focused, and by a wide margin the highest-income crowd. This is where a business goes when it sells to other businesses, not to consumers. Most of your local retail clients don't belong here — a commercial insurance broker absolutely does.
**V1 — Guide:** **YouTube** is the living room television. Enormous, all ages, built for video storytelling. If a client has any video at all — an old commercial, or even just the audio from a radio spot — they're closer than they think.
**V1 — Guide:** **TikTok** is the talent show. Young, playful, fast, short video. Real reach if a business has a personality willing to be on camera. Genuinely wrong for a client who won't.
**V1 — Guide:** And two quick ones: **X** is the news ticker — opinionated, immediate, more male, mostly for commentary and current events. **Snapchat** is the group text — the youngest crowd, private and in-the-moment. Neither is where most local businesses should start.
**V1 — Guide:** Here's how to actually use all that. Don't ask "which platforms should we be on?" Ask **"who's your best customer?"** Fifty-eight-year-old homeowner? Facebook. Twenty-eight-year-old spending on herself? Instagram. Another business? LinkedIn. Everything follows from that one question, and you now know enough to answer it out loud.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: show up worth seeing.** This is the part that's actual work, it's the reason nobody's client is doing it well themselves, and honestly it's the heart of what we sell. Two halves to it: **organization**, and **proactive writing by somebody who knows what they're doing.**
**V1 — Guide:** **First, organization.** A **content calendar**, mapped out ahead of time and sent to the owner for approval every month before a single thing runs. That means nobody's scrambling on a Tuesday wondering what to post, and nobody's surprised by what showed up. It also means the content arrives on *time* — the garden center's spring content is written in February, not the week after everybody already bought their baskets. That's what planning ahead actually buys you.
**V1 — Guide:** **Second — and this is the real product — proactive writing and expert content creation.** Here's the thing to understand: the **voice** is the power of this program. Anybody can post. Posting is free and takes four minutes. What almost nobody can do is write something worth reading, in somebody else's voice, week after week, without running out of ideas.
**V1 — Guide:** And you already know exactly what I mean, because you've worked around copy your whole career. You know the difference between a spot that's just "SALE, SALE, SALE, come on down" and one with a hook that makes somebody actually stop. Radio spot, TV commercial, eight words on a billboard — same craft. Most small-business social media is a bullhorn: every post is an ad, and people scroll right past, because nobody follows a business to be sold to eight times a month.
**V1 — Guide:** So a strategist writes **thoughtfully**, not just promotionally. That means content that's genuinely useful or interesting on its own: here's what to plant this week. Here's how to tell if your roof actually has hail damage. Here's the crew, here's the shop dog, here's the thing we get asked constantly. Content that earns attention *before* it asks for anything. And then, sometimes, yes — ask for the sale. That one lands much harder when it isn't the eighth ask in a row.
**V1 — Guide:** **Proactive** is the other word that matters. A strategist isn't waiting for the owner to send a photo and a caption idea. They're thinking a month out — what's the season, what's coming up in that town, what are customers about to start wondering about. They come to the owner *with* the plan rather than asking what they'd like to post. That's the difference between a service and a chore.
**V1 — Guide:** And that's the honest answer when an owner says their niece can do this for free. Sure — she can post. She probably can't write in the business's voice, plan a season ahead, and know what's worth saying. That's a skill, and it's the one they're actually paying for.
**V1 — Guide:** And then the part almost everybody skips: **answering people.** Comments, questions, messages. That two-day-old comment from the top of this lesson? That's the whole game. A business that replies looks like a business that's open, run by humans, and paying attention.
**V1 — Guide:** One more thing that makes this easier to sell: a good post isn't single-use. The same photo and caption can go on their search listing as a post, feed the website, and turn up in the email. One piece of work, several rooms of the house.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: put money behind the winners.** Remember how the platforms only show a post to a slice of the audience? **Boosting** is how you buy your way past that. You take a post that's already working — the one people liked and shared and commented on — and put a little money behind it so it reaches well beyond the existing followers.
**V1 — Guide:** The order matters, and this is the part that sounds smart in a meeting: you don't guess. You post, you watch what people actually respond to, and *then* you spend money on the proven one. It's the difference between betting on a horse and betting after the race started.
**V1 — Guide:** That's exactly how the program is built. Eight posts go out in a month, and two of them get boosted — the two that earned it. Not the two we liked best when we wrote them. The two the town actually responded to.
**V1 — Guide:** And here's the important connection. Boosting a good post is one thing. Running real paid social advertising — built targeting, chosen audiences, an actual campaign — is another, and it's a separate conversation with its own lesson coming. But understand this now: **management and advertising together are the one-two punch.** Management is the jab — steady, constant, keeps the business present and gives the ads something credible to land on. Advertising is the right hand — it reaches the people who've never heard of them.
**V1 — Guide:** And that's why the pairing works so well. Run ads for a business whose page is dead and you've spent money sending curious people to a ghost town. Post faithfully and never advertise, and you're mostly talking to people who already know you. Together, each one makes the other work.
**V1 — Guide:** That's it. Right rooms, worth seeing, money behind it.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms *(6:15–7:05)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and its one-line definition animate in as text, one at a time. **Green box** on the real screenshot where relevant — the Sponsored label for boost, the likes and comments for engagement, the audience-size line for reach.

**V1 — Guide:** Five terms so you sound like you know your stuff — because now you do.
**V1 — Guide:** **Organic post** — anything a business posts without paying. Free to publish, and shown to only a fraction of the followers. "Organic" just means unpaid, exactly like it does in the search room.
**V1 — Guide:** **Boost** — money put behind an existing post so more people see it. That's the "Sponsored" label you notice on posts in your own feed.
**V1 — Guide:** **Engagement** — anything a person actually does with a post. Likes, comments, shares, saves, clicks. It's the difference between somebody scrolling past and somebody stopping.
**V1 — Guide:** **Reach** — how many actual people saw the post. Worth knowing because owners assume a thousand followers means a thousand people see everything. Not remotely how it works.
**V1 — Guide:** **Algorithm** — the platform's system for deciding who sees what. Say it plainly: it looks at whether early viewers engage, and shows the post more widely if they do. That's why engagement matters, and why boosting exists.

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff *(7:05–8:55)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature names animate in as a text list, green box ticking to each on the real screenshots. Then four benefit lines build as text: *Stay top of mind · Sound like themselves, only better · Talk with customers · Reach past their own followers.* For the payoff: two real garden-center account screenshots side by side — **before** (a dormant page, last post months old, an unanswered comment) and **after** (regular seasonal posts, replies visible, one post boosted) — plus a real photo of a garden center in spring. A small text tag reads *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** So what's in it? Get specific here — owners like knowing exactly what they're getting, and this is easy to say plainly.
**V1 — Guide:** **A dedicated strategist** — one real person who learns their business, their voice, and their calendar, not a rotating queue and not a ticket number. This is the piece to spend the most time on, because it's the piece competitors can't copy. Software can schedule posts. It can't know that the shop closes early on the first day of deer season.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two channels included.** Facebook as the foundation, plus a second channel picked to match their customer — usually Instagram, but that depends entirely on who their best customer is. More channels are available if the business genuinely calls for it.
**V1 — Guide:** **Eight posts a month, and two of those get boosted.** So there's a real, steady rhythm — roughly a couple posts a week, not a burst in March and silence until August — and money goes behind the two that earned it.
**V1 — Guide:** **All the content and all the graphics are included.** Written for them, designed for them. The owner isn't sending us photos every week or approving a bill for artwork. It's in there.
**V1 — Guide:** **And they're contacted every month for approval before anything runs.** This one's worth saying twice in a meeting, because it answers the fear an owner won't voice — that some stranger is going to post something dumb under their name. Nothing goes out that they haven't seen. They get the plan for the month, they can change it, cut it, or add to it, and then we go.
**V1 — Guide:** On top of that: their search listing cleaned up and verified, with posts published there too. And a radio spot or an old TV commercial can be turned into a video ad — a natural fit for anybody already buying traditional media. Plus targeted paid social reaching people well beyond their own followers.
**V1 — Guide:** What does the owner get? Four things. They **stay top of mind** with people who don't need them today but will. They **sound like themselves, only better** — a real voice, written thoughtfully, planned ahead, instead of a page that's either dead or shouting. They **talk with customers** instead of at them — questions answered, comments returned. And they **reach past their own followers**, which unpaid posting alone can't do.
**V1 — Guide:** Under the hood, the whole thing rests on one honest fact: these platforms limit how many people see a business's free posts, on purpose, because they sell access to the rest. So the winning play is content good enough to earn engagement, plus money behind the pieces that prove themselves. Not one or the other.
**V1 — Guide:** So picture a family garden center. Thirty years in the same spot, everybody's grandmother buys tomatoes there. Before: a page with a last post from November, a comment from a woman asking whether they carry a certain rose, and nobody had answered her in six days.
**V1 — Guide:** After: posts going up regularly — hanging baskets arriving, what to plant this week, the owner's dog asleep in the greenhouse, which honestly outperformed everything. Comments answered same day. And when the spring hours post took off on its own, a little money went behind it so it landed in front of half the county.
**V2 — Scene:** *"Saw on Facebook your baskets were in — figured I'd better hurry."*
**V1 — Guide:** — and that turned into a normal way for people to walk through the door. Nothing about the garden center changed. They'd always had the best baskets in town. The town just started hearing about it on time. *(Illustrative — not a promise of specific numbers.)*

### SEGMENT 7 — Recap + Quiz Bridge *(8:55–9:30)*
**ON-SCREEN:** The three mechanics recap as animated text — 1: right rooms · 2: worth seeing · 3: money behind it — with the real screenshots behind them and the green box tapping each one last time. A *Quiz →* button slides up.

**V1 — Guide:** Three parts, one more time. One: the right rooms — start from who their best customer is, then pick, and don't try to be everywhere. Two: show up worth seeing — organization plus proactive, thoughtful writing, and actually answering people. Three: money behind the winners — post first, see what works, then spend on the proven ones.
**V1 — Guide:** And if one line sticks from this whole lesson, make it this: **the voice is the program.** Anybody can post. Writing something worth reading, in their voice, a month ahead, every month — that's the thing they're buying.
**V1 — Guide:** And remember the pairing, because it's how you'll sell most of these: management and social advertising are a one-two punch. Management keeps them present and gives the ads somewhere credible to land. Advertising reaches the strangers. We've got a whole lesson coming on the ad side — bring the two together and it's a much easier conversation than either one alone.
**V1 — Guide:** That's social media marketing. Quick ten-question check to lock it in. No pressure — you already know this.

## SECTION C — Quiz (10 Questions)
**ON-SCREEN:** One card per question, four tappable options; correct choice highlights green on select. **V2 — Scene** reads each question stem aloud.

**1.** A client sells commercial equipment to other businesses. Which channel is the most natural fit?
A) TikTok, because the videos are short
B) Snapchat, because it's private
C) LinkedIn, because it's the professional, business-to-business room ✅
D) Pinterest, because people plan purchases there

**2.** Why does a strategist write thoughtful content instead of making every post promotional?
A) Because promotional posts are against platform rules
B) Because thoughtful posts are faster to write
C) Nobody follows a business to be sold to repeatedly — content that's useful or interesting earns attention, so the occasional ask actually lands ✅
D) Because promotional content can't be boosted

**3.** Eight posts go out in a month and two get boosted. Why pick those two *after* publishing rather than in advance?
A) Because boosting only works on older posts
B) Because the platform requires a waiting period
C) It doesn't matter — the timing is arbitrary
D) Because you can see which posts people actually responded to, then put the money behind the proven ones ✅

**4.** "Reach" means…
A) How many followers an account has
B) How many actual people saw the post ✅
C) How far away the customers live
D) How many platforms a business is on

**5.** What is an "organic post"?
A) A post about health or natural products
B) A post that has been boosted
C) A post that only followers can see
D) Anything a business posts without paying to promote it ✅

**6.** The "algorithm" is best described as…
A) The platform's system for deciding who sees what, based partly on early engagement ✅
B) A tool businesses buy to schedule posts
C) The total number of likes a post receives
D) A rule requiring businesses to post daily

**7.** Which business is an especially strong fit, and why?
A) A concrete supplier selling to three longtime contractors
B) A business with no customers yet
C) A garden center, because it's visual and always has something seasonal to announce ✅
D) A company that only bids on government contracts

**8.** An owner says social media doesn't work — she posted three times and barely anyone saw it. What's actually going on?
A) Her followers have all left the platform
B) Platforms show free posts to only a fraction of followers, so good posts can go unseen without paid help ✅
C) She posted at the wrong time of day, and that's the whole issue
D) Nothing — if a post doesn't perform, the business isn't a fit

**9.** Why are social media management and social media advertising described as a one-two punch?
A) Because they're sold by two different departments
B) Because advertising replaces the need to post
C) Because management is cheaper than advertising
