# 201 — New Content and Quiz
## Targeted Digital Display · Segment 8 "Into the Room" + replacement quizzes

**What this is.** For every module in the **201 pillar only**, this file contains two things to drop into the existing lesson script:

1. **A new narrated Segment 8 — "Into the Room"** — inserted between the existing Recap and the quiz. Roughly 400–470 spoken words, about 3 minutes.
2. **A replacement quiz**, built on the 201 blueprint so it tests the new segment.

**101 modules are unchanged.** Online Visibility and Content Marketing keep their existing seven-segment structure and quizzes.

---

## HOW TO USE THIS FILE

**In the existing lesson script:**
- Rename Segment 7 from *"Recap + Quiz Bridge"* to just *"Recap"*, and delete its final quiz-bridge line
- Insert the new **Segment 8** after it
- Replace **Section C** wholesale with the new quiz
- In the written Field Notes, mark the narrated objections and lines with **★** so trainers know what reps already heard

**Segment 8's fixed shape — five beats:**
| # | Beat |
|---|---|
| 1 | Framing — name the shape so it doesn't feel like a list |
| 2 | The objection they'll hear most. **V2 speaks it, V1 answers** |
| 3 | The objection that'll catch them out |
| 4 | **The argument to lead with** — the strongest feature, given room, framed against what a traditional seller already knows |
| 5 | Three sentences, each tied to a moment · then the one thing to settle at the first meeting · then the quiz bridge |

**201 quiz blueprint:** Q1–3 mechanics · Q4–6 terms · Q7 fit · Q8 features/benefits · **Q9–10 Segment 8**

**Answer-length rule.** Correct answer is the longest option in **no more than 2 of 10**, shortest in **no more than 3**. Write the correct answer, then deliberately write at least one distractor longer than it.

# 12 · SEARCH ENGINE MARKETING — New Content and Quiz

### SEGMENT 8 — Into the Room
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of a rep walking into a small business. Objections appear as animated text as V2 speaks them. Then a split builds — *every other ad interrupts* against *this one was requested* — **green box** on the second. Then three sentences in green boxes. Then *Quiz →*.

**V1 — Guide:** Before the quiz, let's get you ready for the meeting. Two questions you'll be asked, the argument I'd lead with, and one thing to settle before you leave.

**V1 — Guide:** First, the one you'll hear most.

**V2 — Scene:** *"I tried Google Ads once. It didn't work."*

**V1 — Guide:** Don't argue. Ask two questions instead. Did the ad point at your homepage? And were there negative keywords?

**V1 — Guide:** Nearly every failed search campaign fails on one of those two, and both are management problems rather than problems with the tactic. They didn't buy something bad. They ran something nobody was minding.

**V1 — Guide:** Second, the one that will catch you out if you try to win it.

**V2 — Scene:** *"The minute I stop paying, it all disappears."*

**V1 — Guide:** That's true. Say so.

**V1 — Guide:** This is rented attention, and agreeing with them builds more credibility than any answer you could invent. Then put it where it belongs: *"That's exactly why it sits next to the things you own — your website and your earned rankings. This is the fast lane while those build."*

**V1 — Guide:** Now the argument I'd lead with, because nothing else you sell can claim it.

**V1 — Guide:** **They asked for you.**

**V1 — Guide:** Think about your whole career. Every spot you've ever sold interrupted something — a song, a show, a drive home. You got good at buying attention from people who were busy doing something else.

**V1 — Guide:** This is the only thing in the catalogue that doesn't interrupt anybody. Somebody sat down, typed the exact thing your client sells, and asked to be shown options. Your client's ad is the answer to a question that was already asked.

**V1 — Guide:** That's not a better version of what they've been buying. It's the opposite of it. **Open with that.**

**V1 — Guide:** Three sentences worth knowing word for word. To frame the buy: **"Every other ad you buy interrupts somebody. This one shows up because they asked."** On cost: **"You pay nothing to be seen. You pay when somebody chooses you."** And for the client worried about outspending a bigger competitor: **"You don't have to outspend him. You have to be more specific than him."**

**V1 — Guide:** And the one thing to settle before you leave. Clients judge this on clicks, then feel cheated when clicks don't feel like customers. Get there first. *"Clicks are the cost, not the result. What we'll look at together is calls and forms."* Say it in month one and you won't be arguing in month three.

**V1 — Guide:** That's search engine marketing. Quick ten-question check.

# 13 · TARGETED DISPLAY — New Content and Quiz

### SEGMENT 8 — Into the Room
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of a rep in a meeting. Objections as animated text. Then a visual of 100 site visitors with 95 walking away — **green box** on the 95. Then three sentences. Then *Quiz →*.

**V1 — Guide:** Before the quiz — two questions you'll be asked, the argument I'd lead with, and one thing to settle before you leave.

**V1 — Guide:** First, the objection you'll hear on nearly every call.

**V2 — Scene:** *"Nobody clicks those ads."*

**V1 — Guide:** They're right. Agree with them immediately, then keep going.

**V1 — Guide:** Only about a third of what this produces comes from somebody clicking. The rest are people who saw it, remembered the name, and came back on their own later. So the honest answer is: *"You're right, and it still works — but if we judge it on the click column, we'll both be wrong about it."*

**V1 — Guide:** Second, the one that needs careful words.

**V2 — Scene:** *"Isn't this a bit creepy?"*

**V1 — Guide:** Have this answer ready, and don't improvise around it. **"There's no name, no phone number, no personal information. It's that a device looked at boats. And we cap how often somebody sees it, because annoying people isn't the goal."**

**V1 — Guide:** That's accurate and it's enough. Reps get into trouble on this question by embellishing.

**V1 — Guide:** Now the argument I'd lead with, and it's the strongest one in the display family.

**V1 — Guide:** **You already paid for these people once.**

**V1 — Guide:** Around ninety-five percent of the people who visit a business's website leave without doing a single thing. No call, no form, nothing. And the client paid to get every one of them there — through search, through social, through a listing, through a billboard.

**V1 — Guide:** Ninety-five out of a hundred, gone. This is the only tactic you sell that goes and gets them back.

**V1 — Guide:** Frame it that way and you're not selling banner ads. You're selling a second shot at people they've already bought once.

**V1 — Guide:** Three sentences to know cold. To frame the buy: **"Search catches the demand. Display creates it."** For the client who has tried banners before: **"You already paid to get those people once. This is your second shot at them."** And for the small business worried they can't compete: **"This is the same targeting a national brand buys, at a Main Street budget."**

**V1 — Guide:** And the one thing to settle before you leave — this is the most-cancelled tactic in the catalogue, always for the same reason. Say it first. *"Clicks on this will look low, and that's normal. What we'll watch together is returning visits and total leads."* A client told that in month one does not cancel in month three.

**V1 — Guide:** That's targeted display. Quick ten-question check.

# 13a · DYNAMIC DISPLAY — New Content and Quiz

### SEGMENT 8 — Into the Room
**ON-SCREEN:** Objections as animated text. Then one ad template filling with three different products for three different viewers — **green box** on the changing panel. Then three sentences. Then *Quiz →*.

**V1 — Guide:** Before the quiz — two questions you'll get, the argument I'd lead with, and one thing to settle before you leave.

**V1 — Guide:** First, and this comes up because you probably already sold them display.

**V2 — Scene:** *"Isn't this the same thing you sold me last time?"*

**V1 — Guide:** Fair question, and the answer is one sentence. *"Same delivery, different ad. That one picks who sees your ad. This one changes the ad to match whoever's looking."*

**V1 — Guide:** Second, the one that will catch you out, and it's specific to this product.

**V2 — Scene:** *"Is it going to show trucks I've already sold?"*

**V1 — Guide:** Don't wave that away, because it's the one real risk here. Be straight: that's exactly what feed accuracy is for, and it's the thing to watch. If they've got a feed, it updates itself. If they haven't, they tell us when something big changes.

**V1 — Guide:** Handling that honestly in the first meeting is what stops it becoming a complaint in month two.

**V1 — Guide:** Now the argument I'd lead with.

**V1 — Guide:** **A generic ad isn't a weak ad. It's an ignored ad.**

**V1 — Guide:** You've spent your career writing one message for everybody, because that's what the medium allowed. One spot, one audience, one version. That was never a flaw — it was just the limit.

**V1 — Guide:** This doesn't have that limit. One template, and the ad assembles itself around the person looking at it — the truck he was searching, the service she was reading about. Two people see the same campaign and two different ads.

**V1 — Guide:** So you're not selling a better banner. You're selling the end of a compromise your clients have lived with for thirty years.

**V1 — Guide:** Three sentences to know cold. To separate it from regular display: **"Targeted display picks who sees your ad. This one changes the ad to fit them."** To make the case: **"A generic ad isn't a weak ad — it's an ignored ad."** And to remove the barrier: **"You don't need a feed, a new website, or an integration to start."**

**V1 — Guide:** And the one thing to settle before you leave — two things, actually, and they're both quick. The click column will look like every other display product, so pre-frame that. And agree who tells us when the inventory changes.

**V1 — Guide:** That's dynamic display. Quick ten-question check.

# 14a · GEOFENCE TARGETING — New Content and Quiz

### SEGMENT 8 — Into the Room
**ON-SCREEN:** Objections as animated text. Then a map with a fence drawn around a building, a device leaving, and the ad continuing to appear over a thirty-day bar — **green box** on the tail. Then three sentences. Then *Quiz →*.

**V1 — Guide:** Before the quiz — two questions you'll be asked, the argument I'd lead with, and one thing to settle before you leave.

**V1 — Guide:** First, and you will get this one almost every time.

**V2 — Scene:** *"Isn't this a bit creepy?"*

**V1 — Guide:** Have the words ready. Don't improvise, because improvising is how reps overstate what this does.

**V1 — Guide:** **"Nobody gets a name, a number, or an identity. It's a device that entered an area, and we cap how often the ad shows."**

**V1 — Guide:** Then add the part that usually settles it: most people find useful local advertising convenient rather than intrusive, and the research says so.

**V1 — Guide:** Second, the one that sounds like a dealbreaker and isn't.

**V2 — Scene:** *"They'll be long gone by the time the ad runs."*

**V1 — Guide:** That's what the tail is for. You can keep reaching that device for up to about thirty days after it leaves — which is exactly the window where a big decision actually gets made. Nobody buys a truck in the parking lot. They buy it three weeks later.

**V1 — Guide:** Now the argument I'd lead with.

**V1 — Guide:** **Where somebody has been tells you what they need.**

**V1 — Guide:** You've spent your career buying audiences by rough description — the people who listen at this hour, who watch this programme, who drive past this board. Useful, and always a guess about intent.

**V1 — Guide:** This isn't a guess. Somebody walked into a competitor's showroom. Somebody stood in a hospital waiting room. Somebody spent Saturday at the home and garden show. That's not a demographic — **that's a person telling you what's on their mind by where they put their feet.**

**V1 — Guide:** Three sentences to know cold. To open: **"If you can name the building your customers walk out of, we can advertise to them."** For the client who wants to understand it: **"It's handing out flyers in a parking lot — except you don't have to stand there, and you can keep reaching them for weeks after they drive off."** And when a big weekend is coming: **"A tournament brings four thousand of the right people into town. Let's talk to those four thousand instead of the whole county."**

**V1 — Guide:** And the one thing to settle before you leave. Same click pre-frame as all display — but on this one you have a better option. **If offline visit measurement is available, sell it.** It's the closest advertising gets to counting a walk-in, and it's the strongest retention tool in the whole display family.

**V1 — Guide:** That's geofence targeting. Quick ten-question check.

# 14b · EVENT TARGETING — New Content and Quiz

### SEGMENT 8 — Into the Room
**ON-SCREEN:** Objections as animated text. Then a one-day event bar beside a thirty-day campaign bar — **green box** on the longer one. Then three sentences. Then *Quiz →*.

**V1 — Guide:** Before the quiz — two questions you'll get, the argument I'd lead with, and one thing to settle before you leave.

**V1 — Guide:** First, the objection that comes up whenever the client is already involved in the event.

**V2 — Scene:** *"I've already got a booth there."*

**V1 — Guide:** Good — this makes the booth work harder, and say it that way rather than treating it as competition.

**V1 — Guide:** *"Your card goes into a tote bag with thirty others. The ad is still there in week three."* The booth gets the handshake. This keeps the conversation going after everybody's gone home.

**V1 — Guide:** Second, the one that sounds like an objection and is actually your best argument.

**V2 — Scene:** *"The show's only one day."*

**V1 — Guide:** **"The event is one day. The campaign is a month. And the decision happens in that month."**

**V1 — Guide:** Nobody books a wedding venue at the bridal expo. They walk the show, collect brochures, go home, and decide over the next three weeks — while every business they met has gone completely silent.

**V1 — Guide:** Now the argument I'd lead with.

**V1 — Guide:** **The event does your targeting for you.**

**V1 — Guide:** Think about how hard you normally work to find a qualified audience. Demographics, dayparts, geography, best guesses about who's in market. You build an audience out of assumptions.

**V1 — Guide:** Nobody wanders into a bridal expo by accident. Nobody spends a Saturday at a home and garden show unless something in their house is on their mind. **The people in that building pre-qualified themselves by turning up.** Somebody else did your targeting, and they did it better than any data set could.

**V1 — Guide:** Three sentences to know cold. To open: **"Nobody wanders into a bridal expo by accident."** For the client without a booth: **"You don't need a booth to advertise to everybody in the building."** And to make the case for the tail: **"Every business at that show goes silent the day it ends — right when people start deciding."**

**V1 — Guide:** And the one thing to settle before you leave — this one's different from the other display products. Event campaigns end naturally, so a client can drift away without ever cancelling. **Sell from the calendar, not from one event.** Book the year's shows at the first meeting and you have a programme instead of a one-off.

**V1 — Guide:** That's event targeting. Quick ten-question check.
