# Targeted Display Ads — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Targeted Digital Marketing (Room #13 in the Digital House)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime:** 1803 counted spoken words ≈ **12:01** at 150 wpm *(GBP reference = 967 / 6:27)*
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.
**Spine — the one sentence a trainee must repeat cold:** *Search catches the demand. Display creates it.*

## SECTION A — The Reusable Tactic Lesson Template
*(Identical across all modules. Only the content changes — the beats never do.)*

**Voice convention:** **V1 — Guide** carries the teaching. **V2 — Scene** delivers only in-world lines and the quiz prompts.

**On-screen kit:** animated text · real photos and product screenshots · green callout boxes drawn over real screenshots. **No line illustrations, icon cards, mascots, or drawn metaphors.** Spoken analogies stay spoken.

| # | Segment | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Hook + What It Is** | Relatable moment → one-sentence definition |
| 2 | **Why It Matters** | The stakes + the evidence it works |
| 3 | **Who It's Great For** | 3 categories, one reason each + who it's wrong for |

## SECTION B — Targeted Display: Full Script

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is
**ON-SCREEN:** Real phone screenshot of a news article with a banner ad for a local business alongside it. **Green box** on the ad. Then the same brand's ad appearing on a different site, then in an app — three real screenshots in sequence, green box following it each time.

**V2 — Scene:** *"Okay, this is getting weird. I looked at one boat."*
**V1 — Guide:** Everybody's had that moment — you visit a website once, and for two weeks that company is everywhere you go online. Not a coincidence, not spying. That's **targeted display**: banner and image ads shown to chosen people as they move around websites and apps. You're buying **presence.**
**V1 — Guide:** And here's the first thing to be clear about, because it's the question every advertiser asks and it's different from search ads. **With display, the advertiser pays when the ad is served — when it loads on somebody's screen in front of them.** That's called an **impression**, and one impression means one person had one opportunity to see that ad. It's sold on a **cost-per-thousand basis** — you buy impressions a thousand at a time.
**V1 — Guide:** Not for clicks. Not for calls. For the ad being *seen.* Search ads work the opposite way — nothing owed until somebody clicks. Mixing those up is how a rep sets a client up to be disappointed.
**V1 — Guide:** Here's the analogy, and you already sell it: display is the billboard of the internet. Nobody pulls over for a billboard — it works by being *there,* over and over, until the name is familiar.
**V1 — Guide:** Which is the sentence to remember: **search catches the demand, display creates it.**

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of somebody browsing on a couch, phone in hand. Then a real website analytics view with a **green box** on the bounce/exit figure. Then three text statistics animate in one at a time as large numbers. Then a text line: *the click column is not the scoreboard.*

**V1 — Guide:** So why should an advertiser actually spend money on this? Four reasons, and these are the ones to say out loud.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: they already paid to get those people once.** Roughly ninety-five percent of people who land on a business's website leave without doing a thing — that's not a broken website, that's how people shop. But every one of those visitors cost something to get there. This is the only tactic that goes back for a second shot at them.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: people forget, and big purchases take weeks.** Somebody looks at travel trailers Sunday night, means to come back, and life happens. By the time they're ready they've forgotten which lot it was. Display holds the business in front of them across those weeks — exactly when most local advertisers go silent.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: it gets a business known *before* the need shows up.** Search waits for somebody to type something. Display doesn't. When the water heater finally dies, the name that comes to mind is the one they've been seeing for two months. That's not being findable — that's being *thought of.*
**V1 — Guide:** **Four — and this lands with owners: it's the same machinery the national brands use.** A business on Main Street can put its ad in front of a specific kind of person, in a specific set of towns, on the same system a national retailer buys. Ten years ago that wasn't true, and most owners still assume it's out of reach.
**V1 — Guide:** And the evidence: people shown a display ad after visiting a business's website are substantially more likely to come back and do something — research puts that lift around seventy percent. Same person, same business. They were just reminded.
**V1 — Guide:** One caution: most people who respond **never click.** They see it, keep scrolling, and days later type the name in themselves or walk in — roughly two of three responses happen that way. The ad worked and left no fingerprint. Normal here, normal for a billboard, and a rep who doesn't get that loses the account in month three.

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For
**ON-SCREEN:** Three real photos, one at a time — an RV or boat lot, a furniture showroom or jewelry counter, a newly opened storefront with a banner. Each type's name and reason type in beside it.

**V1 — Guide:** Three easy targets.
**V1 — Guide:** **Big-ticket, long-decision businesses.** RV and boat dealers, furniture, jewelry, remodeling, elective medical. People shop these for weeks across several visits — display keeps the business in the conversation the whole time.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses already getting website traffic that isn't converting.** Easiest to qualify — if people show up and leave, there's a ready-made audience to bring back.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses that need to be *known*, not just found.** New locations, a rebrand, a category nobody searches until the moment they need it. Getting familiar ahead of time is the play.
**V1 — Guide:** Honest flip side: no website traffic means almost nothing to retarget — fix that first. And if they need the phone ringing this week, that's a search-ad conversation. Say so; they'll trust you on the next thing.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics
**ON-SCREEN:** (1) Two columns build as text — *AUDIENCE: who the person is* / *CONTEXT: what they're reading* — with the sheet's five targeting types sorting into them. (2) A real auction-style visual: one page load, many bidders, milliseconds → *programmatic = how it's bought*. (3) The impression math builds as plain numbers, then a frequency figure, then reach → *what makes it work*. Closes on text: *Who you aim at · How it's bought · What makes it land.*

**V1 — Guide:** Three moving parts, and the first two get mixed up constantly — even by people who've sold this a while.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: who you're aiming at.** Two completely different ways to decide who sees an ad, and every campaign uses both.
**V1 — Guide:** **Audience targeting is about the person.** You chase a human being based on what they've done, and the ad follows them wherever they go. The strongest version is **retargeting** — a small piece of code on the site called a **pixel** notes that somebody visited, and that person then sees the ad as they move around online. No name, no phone number, no personal information. Just: this device looked at boats. We can also chase people by what they've been *searching* — somebody typing travel-trailer questions for two weeks is a good bet even if they've never heard of this dealer.
**V1 — Guide:** **Contextual targeting is about the surroundings.** You're not chasing a person — you're buying the *page.* Next to an article on towing capacity. In the outdoor and camping section. Or a hand-picked list of sites worth being on. It doesn't care who's reading; it cares what they're reading.
**V1 — Guide:** Easiest way to hold it: audience targeting follows the person, contextual targeting waits in the right room. Most campaigns run both, because they cover each other's blind spots.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: how it gets bought — and this is what "programmatic" means.** Here's the correction that makes you sound like you know the business: **programmatic is not a type of targeting.** People say "programmatic targeting" constantly and it's close to nonsense. Programmatic is the *buying method.*
**V1 — Guide:** Think about how you sell a schedule now — a conversation, a rate, an insertion order, humans on both ends. That works fine for one station.
**V1 — Guide:** Now imagine buying one ad slot, on one page, for one person, on one visit — across tens of thousands of websites, all day. No human can do that, so it's automated. The instant somebody loads a page, an auction runs in the time it takes to blink: right kind of person, right kind of page, what's it worth. Win, and the ad appears — all before the page finishes loading.
**V1 — Guide:** That's programmatic. It's the machinery, not the strategy, and it's why a small-town business can advertise across thousands of sites without negotiating with any of them.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: what they're actually buying.** This part is arithmetic, and a lot of you already understand it. If you've sold outdoor, impressions and cost-per-thousand are how you've always priced. If you've sold radio or TV, it's the same instinct as reach and frequency.
**V1 — Guide:** Display is sold on a **cost-per-thousand** basis: impressions bought in blocks of a thousand. So the math is simple — at ten dollars per thousand, a thousand-dollar month buys a hundred thousand impressions.
**V1 — Guide:** But a hundred thousand impressions is *not* a hundred thousand people, and that's the whole game. Divide impressions by how many times each person sees it and you get actual human beings reached. A hundred thousand impressions, five views each, is about twenty thousand people.
**V1 — Guide:** And that leads to the most practical thing in this lesson — the mistake you'll be asked to make. **The fastest way to waste a display budget is to aim it at too many people.** Spread a modest budget across a whole region and everybody sees the ad once, which is the same as nobody seeing it. Narrow the audience — tighter geography, tighter targeting — and that same budget hits a smaller group enough times to actually register. Reach isn't the goal. Being *remembered* is the goal.
**V1 — Guide:** Then the housekeeping: geography so money stays where they can serve, a daily schedule, cross-device so one person counts once instead of three times, conversion tracking, and weekly refinement — cutting what isn't working, and capping repetition before familiar turns into irritating.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and definition animate in as text. **Green box** on real screenshots where visible — an ad placement, the two-column audience/context text, a report showing frequency.

**V1 — Guide:** Five terms and you're fluent.
**V1 — Guide:** **Impression** — one showing of the ad to one person, on one screen. This is what the advertiser is buying: opportunities to be seen. It's sold by the thousand.
**V1 — Guide:** **Audience targeting** — choosing who sees the ad based on the *person*: what they've browsed, what they've searched, that they visited the website. The ad follows them.
**V1 — Guide:** **Contextual targeting** — choosing who sees the ad based on the *page*: what the article is about, what category it's in, which site it's on. The ad waits there.
**V1 — Guide:** **Programmatic** — the automated auction system that buys the ad space, one impression at a time, in milliseconds. Not a targeting type. It's how the buying happens, not who you're after.
**V1 — Guide:** **Retargeting** — showing ads specifically to people who already visited the website. Sometimes called remarketing. It's the single most-asked-about piece of this product, and the one clients understand fastest, because they've all experienced it.

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature list animates in. Then four benefit lines: *Bring back the ones who left · Reach people before they shop · Stay in front through a long decision · See what it produced.* Payoff: two real screenshots — **before** (a site with high traffic and few leads) and **after** (the same brand's ad appearing across sites, plus a report showing returning visits) — and a real photo of an RV lot. Small tag: *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** What's in it? Audience and keyword analysis up front. Ad design built to be noticed at a glance. Audience targeting — retargeting and search-behavior — plus contextual targeting by page content, subject matter, and hand-picked site lists. Geography and a daily schedule. Cross-device. Conversion tracking. Weekly refinement including frequency management. And reporting on how the audience behaved.
**V1 — Guide:** What does the owner get? They **bring back the people who already left.** They **reach people before they start shopping.** They **stay in front through a long decision** — exactly when most businesses go quiet. And they **see what it produced**, including returns that never showed as a click.
**V1 — Guide:** So picture a local RV dealership. Before: decent traffic all spring — people browsing floor plans on a Sunday night — almost none of it turning into anything. The owner figured the website was broken.
**V1 — Guide:** After: everybody who looked at a floor plan saw that dealership's ad for the next couple of weeks. New prospects got reached while reading camping and towing content. And instead of spraying the whole region once, the targeting was pulled in tight enough that the same people saw it several times a week — which is the only reason anybody remembered.
**V2 — Scene:** *"I kept seeing you all over the place — figured I'd finally come look."*
**V1 — Guide:** — and notice how that sale shows up in a click report. It doesn't. He didn't click anything. He just remembered. *(Illustrative — not a promise of specific numbers.)*

### SEGMENT 7 — Recap + Quiz Bridge
**ON-SCREEN:** Recap text — 1: bring them back · 2: find the right ones · 3: keep it tight. *Quiz →* button slides up.

**V1 — Guide:** Three parts. One: who you aim at — audience targeting follows the person, contextual targeting waits in the right room, and good campaigns use both. Two: programmatic — the automated auction that buys it, one impression at a time. That's the machinery, not the strategy. Three: impressions — bought by the thousand, and the budget has to be aimed narrowly enough that the same people see it enough times to remember.
**V1 — Guide:** And three things to carry in. **Search catches the demand; display creates it.** The advertiser pays when the ad is seen, not clicked — say that early. And reach without frequency is money on the floor: aim at fewer people, more often.
**V1 — Guide:** That's targeted display. Quick ten-question check. No pressure — you already know this.

## SECTION C — Quiz (10 Questions)

**1.** What's the difference between audience targeting and contextual targeting?
A) Audience targeting is automated; contextual targeting is done by hand
B) Audience targeting chases the person wherever they go; contextual targeting places the ad based on what the page is about ✅
C) They're two names for the same thing
D) Contextual targeting only works on mobile devices

**2.** A client asks what "programmatic" means. What's the accurate answer?
A) A type of audience targeting based on browsing history
B) A premium placement package on major websites
C) The automated auction system that buys ad space one impression at a time, in milliseconds ✅
D) A contract term for how long ads run

**3.** A client has a modest budget and wants to blanket the whole region. Why push back?
A) Regional targeting costs more per impression
B) The platforms won't allow a wide geography
C) Everyone would see the ad once, nobody would remember it — narrow the audience so the same people see it often enough to register ✅
D) Wide targeting is only available on search ads

**4.** With display advertising, what is the advertiser paying for, and how is it sold?
A) Each click, billed individually
B) Each sale the ad produces
C) A flat monthly fee regardless of delivery
D) Each impression — the ad being served in front of a person — sold on a cost-per-thousand basis ✅

**5.** A campaign delivers 100,000 impressions at a frequency of 5. Roughly how many people were reached?
A) 100,000
B) 500,000
C) 20,000 ✅
D) 5,000

**6.** "Retargeting" means…
A) Redesigning the ad creative partway through a campaign
B) Re-aiming a campaign at a different town
C) Showing ads specifically to people who already visited the website ✅
D) Bidding again on an auction you lost

**7.** Which business is an especially strong fit, and why?
A) A business with no website and no web traffic
B) A company needing the phone to ring this week
C) A wholesaler with three contract customers
D) An RV dealership, because people shop for weeks across several visits before deciding ✅

**8.** A client says nobody clicks his display ads, so they must not work. What's the best response?
A) Agree, and recommend cancelling
B) He's right that few people click — but most who respond come back on their own later, which is how this tactic works ✅
C) Promise more clicks next month
D) Explain that clicks can't be measured on display

**9.** Why does cross-device targeting matter?
A) It lets the ad run on television
B) It increases the number of impressions sold
C) The same person uses a phone at lunch and a laptop at night, and should count as one person ✅
D) It's required for conversion tracking to function

**10.** In the RV dealership example, what actually changed?
A) They lowered prices on travel trailers
B) They rebuilt their website from scratch
C) They stopped advertising in the spring
D) Past visitors kept seeing the ads, new prospects were reached in related content, and the ads stayed local and on-schedule ✅

## SECTION D — AE Field Notes *(not narrated)*

**How it's sold.** An add-on, almost never a lead-in. It needs a website with traffic and a pixel installed before it can do its best work. The natural sequence is website → search ads → display. Selling display to a business with no traffic is selling the second half of a machine.

**Bundles with:**
- **SEM** — the cleanest pairing in the catalog and the easiest to explain: search catches people already looking, display reaches everyone else and brings back the ones who left. Sell them as one strategy, not two line items.
- **Website / landing pages** — display needs a destination and a pixel. No pixel, no audience.
- **Social media** — display keeps the name familiar between social touches.
- **Reputation management** — a strong star rating raises what every returning visitor is worth.

**Objections and responses:**
- *"Nobody clicks those ads."* → "You're right, and it still works. Only about a third of the results come from a click — the rest are people who saw it and came back on their own. If we judge this on the click column, we'll both be wrong about it."
- *"Isn't this creepy?"* → "There's no name, no phone number, no personal information — just that a device looked at boats. And we cap how often somebody sees it, because annoying people isn't the goal."
- *"I tried banner ads years ago and got nothing."* → Ask whether there was a pixel and whether it was targeted at all. Untargeted banners bought on volume are a different product from this.
- *"Why not put it all into search?"* → "Search only reaches people already looking. That's a ceiling. Display is how you get to the people who'll be looking next month."

**How the advertiser pays — say this early.** Display is bought on impressions: the advertiser pays when the ad is served onto somebody's screen, whether or not they click. One impression equals one opportunity to be seen by one person. This is the opposite of search ads, where nothing is owed until somebody clicks. Reps should state the model plainly at the first meeting — never the rate — because a client who thinks he's buying clicks will feel cheated by month two.

**Lines for the room:**
- *"Search catches the demand. Display creates it."*
- *"Ninety-five percent of the people who visit your website leave without doing anything. This is how you get a second shot at them."*
- *"You already paid to get those people once. This is your second shot at them."*
- *"This is the same targeting a national brand buys — at a Main Street budget."*
- *"You're paying for the ad to be seen, not clicked — and most of the people it works on never click a thing."*

**Retention trap — pre-frame at the first meeting.** This is the single most-cancelled tactic, and always for the same reason: the client watches the click column and sees almost nothing. Say it before they can — *"Clicks on this will look low, and that's normal. What we'll watch together is returning visits and total leads, because most of what this does never shows up as a click."* A client who was told this in month one doesn't cancel in month three.

**Writer's notes:**
- **Statistics sources:** ~95% of first-time website visitors leave without converting (widely cited across 2026 display benchmark compilations); retargeted visitors roughly 70% more likely to convert (industry benchmark, cited across multiple 2026 retargeting compilations); approximately one-third of retargeting conversions are click-driven with two-thirds view-through (2026 retargeting benchmark reporting citing Google Ads data). **All three are aggregated industry benchmarks, not audited measurements — attribute as "industry benchmarks show" and re-verify annually.** They were chosen because they prove one idea: *display works by presence, not clicks.*
- **Deliberately excluded:** all CPM figures and click-through rates, the ad-set design fee, the monthly minimum, and the contract term. Per standing direction, lessons state the *payment model* (per impression) but never rates or ad-performance metrics.
- **⚠️ Possible error on the sales sheet:** the collateral appears to show a **10% CTR** estimate for display. Industry display click-through runs closer to **0.1%** — roughly a hundredfold difference. This is likely ".10%" rendering as "10%," but as printed it is not defensible and a rep quoting it will be challenged. **Verify and correct before this sheet goes out again.** Excluded from the script regardless, since it's a performance estimate.
- **Note on consistency with the SEM module:** that lesson teaches that display converts under 1% versus search at ~8%. This module reconciles it head-on rather than contradicting it — the two tactics are taught as different jobs, with the SEM stat reframed as the reason display is measured differently. Keep both framings aligned if either is revised.
- **Payoff vertical (RV dealership)** is not on the do-not-reuse list. Updated list: diner, roofer, flooring co., hearing clinic, insurance agency, storage co., garden center, medical clinic, auto repair shop, pest control co., **RV dealership**.
