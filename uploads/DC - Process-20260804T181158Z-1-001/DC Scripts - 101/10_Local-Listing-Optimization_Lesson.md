# Local Listing Optimization — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Online Content Marketing (Room #10 in the Digital House)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime target:** ~7 minutes + 10-question quiz *(see runtime note in Section B)*
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.

## SECTION A — The Reusable Tactic Lesson Template
*(Use this exact shape for all ~25 tactic modules. Only the content changes — the beats never do.)*

Two questions before you write: **"What's the plain-English definition?"** and **"What are the 3 core mechanics?"** Everything else follows the beats.

**Voice convention (all modules):**
- **V1 — Guide:** the teaching spine. Carries every segment.
- **V2 — Scene:** short in-the-world lines only — a customer's search, a quoted reaction, a review snippet, and the quiz prompts. Its job is to signal a "mode switch" and keep the ear awake.
- *(Optional V3 later if you want a distinct quiz host — but two voices covers everything below.)*

**On-screen rule — the visual kit (all modules):** keep it clean and real. Three ingredients only:
1. **Animated text** — key words and short lines build/fade in as V1 speaks. This is the workhorse.
2. **Real photos** — actual screenshots of the product (a real listings audit showing errors, real directory pages with mismatched info, a real dashboard) and real photography of people/businesses (an older patient at a clinic desk, a shop owner on the phone, a storefront). No clip-art, no cartoon figures.
3. **Green highlight box** — a green callout box drawn over a real screenshot to point at a specific section (same style as the sales sheet: box the wrong phone number, box the old address, box the duplicate listing), with a text label animating in beside it.

**No cheesy line illustrations, icon/emoji cards, or metaphor graphics** (no glowing houses, oil-can drawings, map pins with faces, little walking figures). Any analogy the Guide speaks stays *spoken only* — we don't draw it. Quiz cards being tappable is UI, not illustration, so that stays.

**Quiz blueprint (same every tactic):** Q1–3 = the 3 mechanics · Q4–6 = terms · Q7 = who it's good for + why · Q8 = why-it-matters scenario · Q9–10 = features/benefits (one may use the Segment-6 payoff).

**Answer placement rule (all modules):** vary which letter is correct — roughly even across A, B, C, and D, with no repeating pattern. If the correct answer sits at B nine times out of ten, trainees stop reading the options and start pattern-matching, and the quiz measures nothing. Also keep the wrong answers plausible; obviously silly distractors have the same effect.

**Payoff rules (Segment 6):** pick the easiest-to-picture business from Segment 3; keep outcomes plain and clearly *illustrative* — no invented numbers, no real names.

## SECTION B — Local Listing Optimization: Full Script

> **Runtime note:** about 7:10. The extra time over the 6:30 standard is Mechanic 3, which carries the retention argument. That's deliberate — this is the product clients cancel at six months because they think it's finished, and the fix for that is a rep who framed it correctly *at the first meeting.* Don't trim Mechanic 3.
>
> **⚠️ Stat note — read before recording.** Two numbers get spoken in this script and they are not equally solid.
> - **The "billions in lost sales" figure** is a widely repeated industry *estimate* (commonly cited around ten billion dollars a year in the US). It shows up in vendor and trade material without a clear primary study. The script says "industry estimates" out loud rather than asserting it as research. Keep that hedge.
> - **The "sixty-one changes a year" figure** could not be verified against any independent source. It appears to originate from partner or vendor sales material. The script therefore attributes it as a rule of thumb from the platform side and pivots immediately to a concrete list a rep can defend on their own. **If you have the underlying source, add it and let reps cite it. If not, consider replacing the number with "dozens of small changes a year," which is defensible and loses nothing.**
> - **The number worth adding:** a BrightLocal consumer survey found roughly two-thirds of consumers would stop using a local business after finding incorrect information in an online directory. That's properly sourced and lands harder on a one-location owner than an industry aggregate. It's in Segment 2.
>
> **Directory count:** the sales sheet says 70+; internal notes say about 80. The script says "seventy-plus" to match the collateral a client will be holding. Align these before recording.

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is *(0:00–0:45)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of an older woman standing outside a dark, empty office suite, phone in hand. Then three real directory screenshots for the same business side by side — three different phone numbers, two different addresses, one wrong set of hours. A **green box** draws around each mismatch. Then a real audit view showing a long list of flagged errors, green box on the error count.

**V2 — Scene:** *"I drove all the way out there. Nobody was even in the building."*
**V1 — Guide:** That's a real customer who wanted to give a business money and couldn't find it. She looked it up, got an address, drove there — and it was the old office. They moved two years ago.
**V1 — Guide:** So here's the thing that surprises everybody. That business *did* update their address. They updated it on Google. What they didn't know is that their information also lives on seventy-plus other directories, apps, and databases — map apps, voice assistants, review sites, GPS systems, places they've never heard of and never signed up for. And most of those still had the old address.
**V1 — Guide:** So, plainly: **Local Listing Optimization** is making sure a business's name, address, and phone number are correct and consistent everywhere online — not just on Google — and then keeping them that way.
**V1 — Guide:** Those three pieces have a nickname you'll hear constantly: **NAP.** Name, address, phone. Sounds trivial. It is the single most boring, most important data in a business's entire digital presence.

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters *(0:45–1:40)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photos — a customer squinting at a phone, a disconnected-number screen, a closed door with a "hours" sign that doesn't match the listing. Then a real search results page with **green boxes** on conflicting information from different sources. Stakes text builds: *they walk away → it poisons everything else → it's already wrong today.*

**V1 — Guide:** So why does a business need this? Three reasons.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: wrong information doesn't confuse people, it loses them.** A customer who hits a disconnected number or drives to the wrong address doesn't try harder — they go to the competitor. Consumer research on this is pretty blunt: roughly two out of three people say they'd stop using a local business after finding incorrect information in an online directory. Two out of three.
**V1 — Guide:** And it adds up to real money. Industry estimates put the sales lost every year to wrong, missing, or incomplete listing information in the billions of dollars nationally. Now — "billions" is a fine credibility number, but don't stop there, because an owner with one location doesn't care about a national total. Do the local math with him instead. Ask how many calls a week come in off search, and what an average customer's worth. Then ask what happens if some slice of those calls hit a dead number. That's *his* number, and it's the one that lands.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: bad data quietly poisons everything else he's buying.** This one's important, and it's the argument nobody else in the market is making. Search engines cross-check a business against every source they can find. When the name, address, and phone don't match, the search engine isn't sure which version is true — so it trusts the business less and ranks it lower. Which means his search work, his listing work, his ad spend, all of it is working uphill against his own bad data. Fix this and everything else he's already paying for works better.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: it's wrong right now, and he has no idea.** That's not a guess. It's essentially always true, and it's why the audit sells this thing better than any pitch you could write.

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For *(1:40–2:20)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Three real photos appear one at a time — a business with a new sign or a moving truck, a multi-location or franchise storefront, a clinic or restaurant front door. Each business type's name and its one-line reason type in beside the photo.

**V1 — Guide:** So who do you picture pitching this to? Three easy ones.
**V1 — Guide:** **Anybody who's changed something.** Moved, got a new phone number, rebranded, changed hours, changed owners, added a suite number. Every one of those changes is scattered across dozens of places that never got the memo. If they've changed anything in the last five years, they've got a problem.
**V1 — Guide:** **Multi-location businesses and franchises.** Every location has its own set of listings, so the problem multiplies — and the odds that all of them are right everywhere are essentially zero.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses people have to find or call.** Restaurants, clinics, retail, trades with a shop. When the whole transaction depends on showing up at the right place at the right time, wrong data isn't a nuisance, it's a lost sale and an annoyed customer.
**V1 — Guide:** Notice the thread: if a customer has to locate them or reach them, the data has to be right everywhere — not just where the owner happened to look.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics *(2:20–5:00)*
**ON-SCREEN:** (1) A real audit report, **green box** moving down a list of flagged errors → label *the audit*. (2) A real dashboard with one record, then arrows-free cuts to several real directory pages all showing matching info → label *one source of truth*. (3) A calendar of small changes building as text, then a re-run audit showing *new* errors appearing → label *the maintenance*. Closes on text: *Find it · Fix it everywhere · Keep it fixed.*

**V1 — Guide:** How does this actually work? Three moving parts. Just three.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: the audit — find out how wrong it already is.** We scan the directories and show the owner exactly what's out there under his name. And this is the easiest demo in the entire catalog, because it's not a pitch — it's his own information on a screen, wrong.
**V1 — Guide:** You will watch owners get genuinely rattled by this. An old phone number that rings a fax machine. A location closed three years ago still listed as open. His name spelled two different ways. A listing he's never seen in his life. You don't have to convince anybody of anything. You just show them.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: one source of truth — fix it once, push it everywhere.** Instead of the owner hunting down seventy-plus logins he doesn't have, we set up one master record and connect it directly into those directories' databases. Change it in one place, it flows out everywhere.
**V1 — Guide:** And while we're in there, we do more than the bare minimum. Duplicate listings get cleaned up — those are two or three records for the same business, splitting up his reviews and confusing search engines. The listings get filled out properly: hours, photos, services, products, video where the directory supports it. And the Google Business Profile gets optimized directly, since that's the one customers see most.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: the maintenance — and pay attention here, because this is the part that decides whether the client stays.** Let me be direct with you about a problem we run into constantly.
**V1 — Guide:** A client signs up. We clean everything up. Six months later he looks around, sees his information is correct everywhere, feels great about it — and cancels. Because it's fixed. Right?
**V1 — Guide:** No. And here's the analogy to use, because it's exact: **this is the oil in your car.** You don't stop changing your oil because the engine's running smooth. The engine running smooth *is the oil doing its job.* Cancel it, and nothing bad happens on Tuesday. Something bad happens in eight months, quietly, and by then it's expensive.
**V1 — Guide:** Because business data doesn't hold still. On the platform side the rule of thumb is that a typical business generates something like sixty-one data changes in a year — and honestly, you don't need the number, you need the list. Hours change for a holiday. A phone line gets added. A staff member updates something. A customer "suggests an edit" and a directory accepts it. A directory re-imports old records from a data wholesaler and overwrites what we fixed. Another site scrapes stale information and republishes it. That's **organic creep** — the slow drift back toward wrong.
**V1 — Guide:** And here's the part to say out loud to a client: the day this stops, the drift starts again, and it doesn't drift back to *neutral.* It drifts back to whatever the internet last believed, which is usually the old stuff we just cleaned up.
**V1 — Guide:** So say this at the *first* meeting, not at renewal: *"About six months from now, you're going to look at this and think it's finished, and wonder why you're still paying me. I want to tell you now — that feeling is the product working. This isn't a repair, it's an oil change on a schedule."* An objection you predicted out loud has almost no power six months later.
**V1 — Guide:** And you've got proof, which is the best part. The audit isn't a one-time thing — you can run it again. So run it before that renewal conversation and show him the *new* errors that showed up since. That's not a sales argument. That's a receipt.
**V1 — Guide:** The reframe underneath all of it: he isn't buying a cleanup. He's buying **accuracy.** And accuracy isn't something you buy once — it's a condition you maintain.
**V1 — Guide:** That's it. Find it, fix it everywhere, keep it fixed.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms *(5:00–5:45)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and its one-line definition animate in as text, one at a time. **Green box** on the real screenshots where relevant — the NAP block on a listing, a citation on a third-party site, two duplicate listings side by side.

**V1 — Guide:** Five terms so you sound like you know your stuff — because now you do.
**V1 — Guide:** **NAP** — name, address, phone number. The three pieces that have to match everywhere. If you remember one acronym from this lesson, it's this one.
**V1 — Guide:** **Citation** — any mention of a business's name, address, or phone on another website. A directory listing is a citation. So is a chamber of commerce page. Search engines count them up like references.
**V1 — Guide:** **Data aggregator** — the wholesalers of business information. They collect business data in bulk and sell or feed it to lots of directories at once. Which is why one bad record in the wrong place can spread to a dozen sites without anybody touching them.
**V1 — Guide:** **Duplicate listing** — two or more records for the same business. Splits the reviews, splits the signals, and makes a search engine unsure which one is real.
**V1 — Guide:** **Organic creep** — our name for the slow drift back toward wrong information over time. Nobody breaks it on purpose. It just erodes, which is exactly why it needs somebody watching.

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff *(5:45–6:40)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature names animate in as a text list, green box ticking to each on the real dashboard and directory screenshots. Then four benefit lines build as text: *Customers can actually find them · One place to change everything · Protects everything else they buy · Stays right instead of drifting.* For the payoff: two real clinic-listing screenshots side by side — **before** (old address, old phone, duplicate record) and **after** (matching everywhere, filled out, photos) — plus a real photo of an older patient at a clinic front desk. A small text tag reads *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** So what's in it? A full audit showing exactly where the business stands today. Consistent name, address, and phone pushed across seventy-plus of the directories that matter — the big ones everybody knows and a lot they don't. Enhanced listings where the directory allows it: services, products, photos, video. Duplicate cleanup. Their Google Business Profile optimized directly. Ongoing monitoring, because that's the whole point. And reporting so they can see the state of it rather than take our word.
**V1 — Guide:** What does the owner get? Four things. **Customers can actually find and reach them** — the simplest benefit in the catalog. **One place to change everything**, instead of seventy-plus logins he'll never track down. **Everything else he buys works better**, because consistent data makes search engines trust him. And it **stays right instead of drifting.**
**V1 — Guide:** Under the hood, here's the simple version: search engines and AI tools decide how much to trust a business partly by whether its story matches everywhere they look. Matching data is trust. Mismatched data is doubt. That's the whole mechanism.
**V1 — Guide:** So picture a small medical clinic that moved across town. Before: the new address on their own website and on Google — but the old address still live on a dozen other directories, an old phone number that rang nowhere, and a duplicate listing from the previous office splitting their reviews in half. Patients were showing up at an empty suite. Older patients, mostly, and they weren't calling to complain — they were just going somewhere else.
**V1 — Guide:** After: one record, correct, everywhere. Duplicate cleared. Hours and photos filled in. And somebody watching it, so when a directory quietly re-imported the old address a few months later, it got caught and corrected instead of sitting there for a year.
**V2 — Scene:** *"Found you right where it said you'd be."*
**V1 — Guide:** — which is a strange thing to celebrate, and exactly the point. When this works, nothing happens. Nobody gets lost. Nobody gets a dead number. Silence is the deliverable. *(Illustrative — not a promise of specific numbers.)*

### SEGMENT 7 — Recap + Quiz Bridge *(6:40–7:10)*
**ON-SCREEN:** The three mechanics recap as animated text — 1: the audit · 2: one source of truth · 3: the maintenance — with the real dashboard behind them and the green box tapping each area one last time. A *Quiz →* button slides up.

**V1 — Guide:** Three parts. One: the audit — show them their own wrong information; it sells itself. Two: one source of truth — fix it once, push it to seventy-plus directories, clean the duplicates, fill the listings out. Three: the maintenance — because data drifts, and drift goes backward to the old stuff, not to neutral.
**V1 — Guide:** And the two lines to carry into a room. First: it's the oil in your car — the engine running smooth is the product working. Second, and say it at the *first* meeting: "in six months you'll think this is finished and wonder why you're still paying me — let's talk about that now." Then run the audit again before renewal and show them what drifted. That's how this client stays.
**V1 — Guide:** That's Local Listing Optimization. Quick ten-question check to lock it in. No pressure — you already know this.

## SECTION C — Quiz (10 Questions)
**ON-SCREEN:** One card per question, four tappable options; correct choice highlights green on select. **V2 — Scene** reads each question stem aloud.

**1.** Why is the audit such an effective sales tool?
A) It predicts how much revenue the client will gain
B) It shows the owner his own incorrect information on a screen, so you don't have to convince him of anything ✅
C) It guarantees top rankings
D) It removes the need for any ongoing work

**2.** An owner says he already fixed his address on Google. Why isn't that enough?
A) Google doesn't accept address changes
B) He needs to fix it on his website instead
C) His information also lives on seventy-plus other directories, apps, and databases that never got the update ✅
D) It is enough — Google feeds every other site automatically

**3.** A client cancels after six months because his listings are all correct now. What's the best response?
A) Agree that the work is complete
B) Offer to re-clean the listings at a discount
C) Explain that directories rarely change anything
D) It's the oil in the car — data drifts back to the old information, so accuracy is maintained, not purchased once ✅

**4.** "NAP" stands for…
A) Name, address, phone number ✅
B) New account profile
C) Network access point
D) Name and physical address

**5.** A "data aggregator" is…
A) A tool that counts how many listings a business has
B) A company that collects business data in bulk and feeds it to many directories at once ✅
C) A type of duplicate listing
D) Software that writes business descriptions

**6.** What problem does a duplicate listing cause?
A) It doubles the business's visibility
B) None — extra listings are always helpful
C) It splits reviews and signals, and makes search engines unsure which record is real ✅
D) It only affects paid advertising

**7.** Which business is an especially strong fit, and why?
A) A business that has never changed its name, address, phone, or hours
B) An online-only seller with no physical location or phone
C) A one-person consultancy with no public address
D) A clinic that moved across town, because its old address is still live on dozens of directories ✅

**8.** A customer calls the number she found in a map app and it rings nowhere. What does she most likely do next?
A) Search for a different number for that business
B) Call the competitor instead — and she may not use that business again ✅
C) Drive to the location to check
D) Wait and try again tomorrow

**9.** Why does fixing listings make a client's other digital spending work better?
A) It doesn't — the two are unrelated
B) Because it lowers advertising costs directly
C) Because directories give discounts to managed listings
D) Search engines trust a business more when its information matches everywhere, so it ranks better ✅

**10.** In the clinic example, what actually changed?
A) The clinic moved back to its old location
B) The clinic hired more staff
C) One correct record everywhere, the duplicate cleared, and somebody watching so a later re-import got caught ✅
D) The clinic stopped accepting new patients

**Answer key:** 1-B · 2-C · 3-D · 4-A · 5-B · 6-C · 7-D · 8-B · 9-D · 10-C
