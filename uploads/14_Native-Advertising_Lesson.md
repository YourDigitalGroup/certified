# Native Advertising — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Targeted Digital Marketing (Room #21 in the Digital House)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime:** 1296 counted spoken words ≈ **8:38** at 150 wpm
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.
**Spine — the one sentence a trainee must repeat cold:** *Don't advertise the car. Offer ten car-buying tips.*

## SECTION B — Native Advertising: Full Script

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is
**ON-SCREEN:** Real screenshot of a news article with a recommended-content row underneath it. **Green box** around one item reading *10 Car Buying Tips — Make buying a car easy and fun with these smart tips.* Then a green box on the small *Sponsored* label above it. Then two panels side by side: *looks like an article* / *looks like a social post*, labeled.

**V2 — Scene:** *"Ten car buying tips? Yeah, alright, I'll read that."*
**V1 — Guide:** Notice what he didn't say. Nobody in history has said "a car dealership ad? Let me click that." But "ten car buying tips" — that he'll read. And the dealership paid for it.
**V1 — Guide:** So, plainly: **native advertising** is a paid ad built to look and read like the content around it. On a news site it looks like another article. It's clearly labeled as sponsored, and it earns the click by offering something useful instead of asking for something.
**V1 — Guide:** If you've ever sold an advertorial in a newspaper, or a sponsored segment on the air, you already understand this exactly. Same idea, on the internet, at scale.
**V1 — Guide:** And here's how it differs from our social display product, because both are "native" in style. Social display is built to look like **a social post.** This one is built to look like **an article.** Different costume, same principle — wear the clothes of the page you're on.
**V1 — Guide:** It's bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis**, and it comes in display and video formats. The line to carry: **don't advertise the car — offer ten car-buying tips.**

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters
**ON-SCREEN:** A trust ladder builds as animated text with percentages — *native on editorial sites · branded content · search ads · social ads · display banners · pop-ups*, descending, **green box** at the top. Then text: *the page lends you its credibility.*

**V1 — Guide:** So why should an advertiser do this? Three reasons.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: where an ad sits changes how much people believe it.** Nielsen publishes a trust index on ad formats and the ranking is stark. Native on editorial sites sits at the top — around eighty percent say they trust it. Search and social ads land in the fifties. Display banners, around thirty percent. Pop-ups, in the teens.
**V1 — Guide:** Same business, same claim: move it from a banner to a sponsored article on a credible site and you've roughly doubled how believable it is. That costs nothing but the format.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: people actually look at it.** Research puts native ads at roughly fifty percent more likely to be looked at than a banner. Which follows — a banner announces itself and eyes skip it. This looks like the thing they came to read.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: the traffic is better traffic.** Somebody who clicks "ten car buying tips" is telling you they're thinking about buying a car — nobody clicks that by accident. Fewer clicks, but every one has raised a hand.
**V1 — Guide:** One honest note that's also a strength. These ads are **labeled as sponsored** — required, not optional. Reps worry the label ruins it. It doesn't: research is consistent that clear labeling *helps* trust, and hiding it backfires badly. Treat it as a statement of confidence.

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For
**ON-SCREEN:** Three real photos, one at a time — a senior living community common area, a financial or legal office, a home improvement project mid-work. Each type's name and reason type in beside it.

**V1 — Guide:** Three easy targets.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses whose customers need educating first.** Senior living, financial services, legal, insurance, medical procedures, solar. The customer doesn't know enough yet to shop, so an ad that teaches gets further than one that sells.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses with a long, emotional decision.** Anything where somebody researches for weeks and worries about getting it wrong. Native content meets them in the worrying stage, which is much earlier than most advertising reaches.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses with a big-ticket purchase people research.** Vehicles, home improvement, equipment, travel. There's always a "things to know before you buy" article to be written, and that article is the ad.
**V1 — Guide:** Honest flip side: this needs something worth reading. No expertise to share means no native angle, and a thin one gets clicked once and remembered badly. And if a client needs the phone ringing this week, this isn't it — native works early, not at the buzzer.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics
**ON-SCREEN:** (1) A real page with the ad blending into the content, **green box** on the unit and a second on the *Sponsored* label → *wear the page's clothes*. (2) Two headlines side by side — *Visit Our Showroom Today* with a red-free plain look, versus *10 Car Buying Tips* boxed in green → *earn the click*. (3) The tips headline clicked, opening a real page that actually delivers tips, then a green arrow to a soft call to action → *keep the promise*. Closes on text: *Wear the clothes · Earn the click · Keep the promise.*

**V1 — Guide:** Three moving parts.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: wear the page's clothes.** The ad matches the format of wherever it appears — same layout, same type, same shape as the content around it, sitting inside the page rather than in a box off to the side. Display or video.
**V1 — Guide:** And it always carries the **sponsored label.** That's the deal: it looks like the page, and it tells you it's paid.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: earn the click with the headline.** This is where native campaigns are won and lost, and it's the part a rep can actually coach a client on.
**V1 — Guide:** A headline that pitches — "Visit Our Showroom Today" — gets ignored, because it's an ad wearing a costume. A headline that offers something useful gets read. "Ten car buying tips." "What to ask before you sign a lease." "How to know when it's time."
**V1 — Guide:** The test is simple: would somebody click this if they'd never heard of the business? If the answer is no, it's not native content — it's a banner in a wig.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: keep the promise on the other side.** Here's the mistake that wastes the whole budget. Somebody clicks "ten car buying tips" and lands on an inventory page with a finance calculator. That person feels tricked, leaves immediately, and now thinks a little less of the business.
**V1 — Guide:** So the page has to deliver what the headline offered. Give them the ten tips. *Then* the soft ask. You earn the right to sell by being useful first, and that order can't be reversed.
**V1 — Guide:** Then the housekeeping: audience segments identified, geographic targeting, a daily ad schedule, cross-device delivery, a conversion pixel so we can see what happened, weekly refinement, and reporting.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and definition animate in as text, with green boxes on the sponsored label and the content-style headline.

**V1 — Guide:** Four terms and you're fluent.
**V1 — Guide:** **Native advertising** — a paid ad built to match the form of the content around it, so it reads as part of the page rather than an interruption.
**V1 — Guide:** **Sponsored label** — the disclosure that the content is paid. Legally required, and worth knowing it helps rather than hurts. Never promise a client their ad will look like real editorial with no label.
**V1 — Guide:** **Content-first headline** — a headline that offers something useful instead of pitching. The whole skill of this tactic in one phrase.
**V1 — Guide:** **Qualified traffic** — visitors who are more likely to be genuinely interested, because they chose a subject rather than tapping something by accident. Fewer clicks, better ones.

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature list animates in. Four benefit lines: *Borrow the page's credibility · Get looked at instead of skipped · Better traffic, not just more · Reaches people while they're still learning.* Payoff: a real senior-living-community photo beside a native ad headline on a news site. Small tag: *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** What's in it? Native ad units matched to the pages they run on, display or video, with the sponsored disclosure. Audience segments identified. Geographic targeting. A daily schedule. Cross-device delivery. Conversion pixel tracking. Weekly refinement. And reporting.
**V1 — Guide:** What does the owner get? They **borrow the credibility of the page** they appear on — the most trusted ad format there is. They **get looked at instead of skipped.** They get **better traffic rather than just more of it.** And they reach people **while they're still learning**, which is well before most advertising shows up.
**V1 — Guide:** So picture a small senior living community. Beautiful place, wonderful staff, almost impossible to advertise — nobody wants to think about it until they have to, and by then they're overwhelmed and calling whoever comes up first.
**V1 — Guide:** Before: an ad with a photo of the building and the phrase "Now Accepting Residents." Which reaches somebody at exactly the wrong moment, and asks for a decision nobody's ready to make.
**V1 — Guide:** After: a native article headlined "How to know when it's time to talk to your parents about extra help." It ran on news and health sites, aimed at adults in their fifties in the county. And when they clicked, they actually got the article — five honest signs, written plainly. At the bottom, a quiet line about touring the community.
**V2 — Scene:** *"I read that article three times before I called anybody. When I finally did, I called you."*
**V1 — Guide:** — and that's what native does that nothing else does. It got there months early, and it was useful before it asked for anything. *(Illustrative — not a promise of specific numbers.)*

### SEGMENT 7 — Recap + Quiz Bridge
**ON-SCREEN:** Recap text — 1: wear the page's clothes · 2: earn the click · 3: keep the promise. *Quiz →* button slides up.

**V1 — Guide:** Three parts. One: wear the page's clothes — matched to the format, display or video, always labeled as sponsored. Two: earn the click with a headline that offers something useful, not a pitch. Three: keep the promise — deliver what the headline said, then make the ask.
**V1 — Guide:** And two things to carry in. **Don't advertise the car; offer ten car-buying tips.** And the test for any native headline: **would somebody click this if they'd never heard of the business?**
**V1 — Guide:** That's native advertising. Quick ten-question check. No pressure — you already know this.

## SECTION D — AE Field Notes *(not narrated)*

**How the advertiser pays.** Bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis**, with separate rates for display and video formats. Impression arithmetic is taught once, in Targeted Display.

**How this differs from Social Display (Room 16).** Both are native in style and both run on the open web. **This one is built to look like an article or a content recommendation. Social display is built to look like a social post.** Contrast them by what the ad *looks like*, never by product name — and note that social display carries live follower and engagement counts, which this doesn't.

**How it's sold.** The opener is a question about expertise: *"What do your customers get wrong before they come to you?"* Every good answer is a native headline. Owners enjoy this conversation, because it asks them about the thing they actually know.

**Then set the expectation about content.** This tactic needs something worth reading, and the client is usually the source of it. Decide upfront who's writing the piece and what page the click lands on — a native campaign pointed at a homepage will underperform and it'll look like the tactic's fault.

**Bundles with:**
- **Targeted display** — same buying basis and machinery. Native does the persuading early; display does the reminding after.
- **Website / landing pages** — close to mandatory here. The promise has to be kept somewhere, and that somewhere is usually a purpose-built page.
- **SEO/AEO and content** — if the client is already producing content, native is the distribution for it. Strong natural pairing.
- **Social display** — the same native instinct in a different costume; run both and the client shows up in two forms across the open web.

**Objections and responses:**
- *"Isn't this deceptive?"* → No, and be firm: the label is required and it's there. Native is honest advertising that respects the reader's attention. Deceptive is a banner shouting at somebody who didn't ask.
- *"The label will kill it."* → "The research says the opposite. Clear labeling helps trust, and hiding it backfires. Lean into it."
- *"I don't have anything to write about."* → Ask what customers get wrong. If there's genuinely nothing, this is the wrong tactic — say so.
- *"Isn't this the same as the social-looking display you sold me?"* → Same instinct, different costume. That one looks like a social post with likes on it. This one looks like an article.
- *"Fewer clicks than my banners."* → "Correct, and better ones. Somebody who clicked an article about car buying is shopping for a car. Somebody who tapped a banner may have been trying to close it."

**Lines for the room:**
- *"Don't advertise the car. Offer ten car-buying tips."*
- *"Would somebody click this if they'd never heard of you? If not, it's a banner in a wig."*
- *"You earn the right to sell by being useful first."*
- *"What do your customers get wrong before they come to you? That's your ad."*

**Retention note.** Two risks. **Content fatigue** — one article runs out of road, and a native program needs fresh angles, which is a natural recurring conversation rather than a problem. And **the landing-page trap**: if the click doesn't lead somewhere that keeps the promise, results look bad and the client blames the media. Fix that at setup, not at renewal.

**Writer's notes:**
- **Statistics sources:** the trust ranking is from **Nielsen's 2026 Consumer Advertising Trust Index**, which places native advertising on editorial sites highest at about 81%, branded content around 74%, search advertising around 57%, social advertising around 54%, display banners around 32%, and pop-ups around 14%. **This is strong, current, name-brand sourcing — cite Nielsen if a rep is challenged.** The figure that native ads are viewed roughly 53% more often than banners is attributed to the Native Advertising Institute and is widely reproduced. The disclosure findings — that clear labeling improves trust and concealment produces backlash — appear consistently across peer-reviewed native advertising research and 2026 industry analysis. Attribute as "research shows" and re-verify annually.
- **Deliberately excluded:** both CPM figures, the click-through estimate, the monthly minimum, and the contract term. Nielsen's brand-consideration multiple was also excluded, since a performance multiple reads as a promised result.
- **The disclosure point is a genuine compliance item, not just craft.** FTC guidance requires clear disclosure that native content is paid, and non-compliance has been a documented industry problem. Reps must never suggest the label can be minimised, hidden, or waived. The script frames it as a strength, which is both accurate and the easier sell.
- **Payoff vertical (senior living community)** is new. Updated list: diner, roofer, flooring co., hearing clinic, insurance agency, storage co., garden center, medical clinic, auto repair shop, pest control co., RV dealership, used-car lot, physical therapy clinic, wedding/event venue, veterinary clinic, private school, clothing boutique, brewery/taproom, home builder, paint store, coffee drive-thru, technical college, **senior living community**.
