# Dynamic Display Ads — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Targeted Digital Marketing (Room #13a in the Digital House — the companion to Targeted Display)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime:** 1322 counted spoken words ≈ **8:49** at 150 wpm
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.
**Spine — the one sentence a trainee must repeat cold:** *Targeted display picks who sees the ad. Dynamic display changes the ad to fit them.*

## SECTION B — Dynamic Display: Full Script

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is
**ON-SCREEN:** Real phone screenshot of somebody searching *"used crew cab under 20,000."* Then two real dynamic ads side by side from the same dealer — one showing a white crossover with its price, one showing a used pickup with its price. **Green boxes** on the two different vehicles, label *same advertiser, two different ads.*

**V2 — Scene:** *"Used crew cab, under twenty thousand."*
**V1 — Guide:** Somebody types that on a Tuesday night. Somebody else, three streets over, types "new white crossover." Both of them end up seeing an ad from the same dealership — but they don't see the same ad.
**V1 — Guide:** One sees a used pickup with a price on it. The other sees a white crossover. Nobody built those two ads by hand — the ad assembled itself, in the moment, around what that person was looking for.
**V1 — Guide:** So, plainly: **dynamic display** is display advertising where the ad itself changes to match the person seeing it. Same campaign, same budget, but the picture, the product, and the price shift to fit whatever that individual is researching.
**V1 — Guide:** And here's the cleanest way to hold the difference, because you'll be asked: **targeted display picks who sees the ad. Dynamic display changes the ad to fit them.** They're cousins, they run on the same machinery, and they're sold the same way — this is also bought on a cost-per-thousand impression basis, just like targeted display.
**V1 — Guide:** One more thing, and it's the part that surprises people: this does **not** require somebody to have visited the client's website first. That's what makes it different from the retargeting you already know about.

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters
**ON-SCREEN:** A real generic banner beside a real dynamic ad showing a specific vehicle and price — **green box** on the price. Then three statistics build as large animated numbers. Then text: *generic isn't weak — it's invisible.*

**V1 — Guide:** So why should an advertiser do this instead of a regular display ad? Three reasons.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: a generic ad isn't a weak ad — it's an ignored ad.** People have trained themselves to skip past anything that looks like wallpaper. Around eight in ten consumers say they ignore marketing messages that aren't relevant to them. Not dislike — *ignore.* It may as well not have run.
**V1 — Guide:** The flip side is just as strong: close to ninety percent say they'd rather see ads matched to what they're interested in, and roughly seven in ten now expect it. Showing somebody the exact truck they were shopping for, at a price, isn't a clever trick — it's meeting an expectation everybody already has.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: it reaches people at the beginning, not the end.** This is early in the buying journey — the research phase, weeks before anybody walks onto a lot. Most advertising shows up once somebody's already decided. This is there while they're still deciding, when a specific price on a specific vehicle can still change the outcome.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: it works without a website visit and without hooking into their systems.** Normally, personalized ads require a person to have been on the client's site first. This doesn't. It runs off what people search for and the content they're reading, which means the audience isn't limited to the handful who already found them.
**V1 — Guide:** And on the client's side, it's simple. There's no requirement to rebuild anything or plug in an inventory system before it can start. If they've got a feed, great, we'll use it. If they don't, it still runs.

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For
**ON-SCREEN:** Three real photos, one at a time — a used-car lot, a powersports or equipment lot, a furniture or appliance showroom. Each type's name and reason type in beside it.

**V1 — Guide:** Three easy targets.
**V1 — Guide:** **Anybody selling from an inventory.** Car dealers first — that's what this was built around — but also powersports, equipment, boats, appliances, furniture. When every unit has its own photo and price, there's something to work with.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses whose customers shop by specifics.** Not "a truck" — a *used crew cab under twenty thousand.* Color, model, new versus used, certified. The more precisely people describe what they want, the better this performs, because those exact words are what triggers the right ad.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses whose stock changes constantly.** When what's on the lot this week is different from last week, a hand-built banner is out of date the day it launches. This updates itself.
**V1 — Guide:** Honest flip side: a business with one product and one price doesn't need this — regular targeted display does the same job with less machinery. Say so. Smaller sale, better relationship.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics
**ON-SCREEN:** (1) A blank ad template with slots highlighted, then three finished versions filling in → *the ad builds itself*. (2) A real search phrase and a real article, arrows-free cut to the matching ad → *what triggers which ad*. (3) A feed of inventory, then a map radius and schedule → *keeping it accurate*. Closes on text: *Builds itself · Triggered by intent · Kept accurate.*

**V1 — Guide:** Three moving parts.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: the ad builds itself.** Instead of designing a finished banner, we build a **template** — the dealership's logo, colors, and layout, with empty slots where a photo, a description, and a price will go. Then, the instant the ad is served, those slots get filled with the specific item that matches the person seeing it.
**V1 — Guide:** One template, thousands of versions. Nobody sits and designs an ad for every vehicle on the lot, which is exactly why this is possible at a local budget.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: what triggers which version.** This is the piece to understand well, because it's the differentiator.
**V1 — Guide:** The system watches two signals: what somebody has been **searching for**, and what they're **reading** right now. Typed "certified used truck" and reading truck reviews? They get the used truck ad. Researching new crossovers? They get a crossover. The words become the instruction for which version to assemble.
**V1 — Guide:** And say this part plainly, because it's a genuine advantage: **none of that requires them to have visited the client's website.** Retargeting can only chase people who already showed up. This reaches people who've never heard of the business yet — which is a much bigger pool.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: keeping it accurate and controlled.** If the client has an inventory system or a product feed, we can connect to it so the ads reflect what's actually on the lot. That's optional — it runs without one — but when it's there, the ads stay current on their own.
**V1 — Guide:** Then the usual housekeeping: geography, a daily schedule, cross-device so one person counts once, conversion tracking, weekly refinement. And one thing specific to this product — accuracy. An ad showing a vehicle that sold last week isn't just a wasted impression, it's a bad first impression.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and definition animate in as text, with green boxes on the template slots and a feed view.

**V1 — Guide:** Five terms and you're fluent.
**V1 — Guide:** **Dynamic creative** — an ad that assembles itself in the moment instead of being designed in advance. That's the whole product in two words.
**V1 — Guide:** **Ad template** — the fixed frame: logo, colors, layout, with empty slots waiting to be filled.
**V1 — Guide:** **Product feed** — a file listing what the business has to sell, with photos, descriptions, and prices. It's what fills those slots. Optional here, but useful when it exists.
**V1 — Guide:** **Search signal** — the words somebody typed that tell the system which version of the ad to build. This is the trigger.
**V1 — Guide:** **Buying journey** — the stretch between "I'm thinking about it" and "I'm buying it." This tactic lives at the front of that stretch, while search ads live at the end.

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature list animates in. Four benefit lines: *Every viewer sees something relevant · Reaches people who never visited · Stays current on its own · Works early, before they decide.* Payoff: two real screenshots — **before** (a generic dealership banner) and **after** (three dynamic ads showing different vehicles and prices) — plus a real photo of a used-car lot. Small tag: *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** What's in it? Ad templates in the business's look. Real-time assembly driven by search signals and content. Keyword and contextual targeting. Geography and a daily schedule. Cross-device. Conversion tracking. Weekly refinement. Reporting. Plus optional connections to inventory or product feeds — with nothing required to be built before it starts.
**V1 — Guide:** What does the owner get? **Every viewer sees something relevant**, instead of one banner aimed at everybody. They **reach people who've never visited the website.** The ads **stay current on their own** as stock changes. And they're **working early**, while people are still deciding.
**V1 — Guide:** So picture a used-car lot in a town of thirty thousand. Before: one banner, the dealership name, a photo of the building, "Great Deals on Quality Pre-Owned." It ran for months and said nothing to anybody in particular.
**V1 — Guide:** After: the person searching used crew cabs saw a used crew cab with a price. The person shopping certified sedans saw a certified sedan with a price. Same budget, same campaign — the ad just started speaking to one person at a time.
**V2 — Scene:** *"I saw the actual truck. That's why I drove out here."*
**V1 — Guide:** — and that's the difference between advertising a business and advertising a thing somebody wants. *(Illustrative — not a promise of specific numbers.)*

### SEGMENT 7 — Recap + Quiz Bridge
**ON-SCREEN:** Recap text — 1: builds itself · 2: triggered by intent · 3: kept accurate. *Quiz →* button slides up.

**V1 — Guide:** Three parts. One: the ad builds itself from a template, so one design becomes thousands of versions. Two: what somebody searched and what they're reading decides which version they see — and no website visit is required. Three: keep it accurate, with a feed if they've got one, plus the usual geography, schedule, and tracking.
**V1 — Guide:** And the line to carry in: **targeted display picks who sees the ad; dynamic display changes the ad to fit them.** Same machinery, same cost-per-thousand impression basis, different job.
**V1 — Guide:** That's dynamic display. Quick ten-question check. No pressure — you already know this.

## SECTION D — AE Field Notes *(not narrated)*

**How the advertiser pays.** Same as targeted display — bought on a **cost-per-thousand impression basis**, paying for the ad to be served whether or not anybody clicks. The impression math is taught in the Targeted Display module; here it's a one-line statement so reps don't hear it twice.

**How it's sold.** Almost always an upgrade conversation, not a first sale. The natural path is: sell targeted display, then move the inventory-based clients to dynamic. Easiest opener is the side-by-side — the generic banner next to two dynamic ads showing real vehicles and prices. It sells itself on sight.

**Bundles with:**
- **Targeted display** — same buying machinery. Frequently run together: dynamic for the inventory, standard display for brand and retargeting.
- **SEM** — dynamic works the research phase, search ads capture the person once they're ready. Clean story to tell.
- **Website / landing pages** — the click has to land somewhere useful even though a site visit isn't required to *serve* the ad.

**Objections and responses:**
- *"Isn't this the same as the display you already sold me?"* → "Same delivery system, different ad. That one picks who sees your ad. This one changes the ad to match whoever's looking."
- *"I don't have inventory software / a feed."* → "You don't need one. It runs off what people search and read. If you do have a feed later, we can plug it in and the ads update themselves."
- *"Will it show trucks I already sold?"* → Be straight: that's exactly what feed accuracy and weekly refinement are for, and it's the one thing to watch on this product.
- *"My inventory changes too fast for advertising."* → That's the argument *for* this, not against it. A hand-built banner is stale on launch day.

**Lines for the room:**
- *"Targeted display picks who sees your ad. This one changes the ad to fit them."*
- *"A generic ad isn't a weak ad — it's an ignored ad."*
- *"You're not advertising a dealership. You're advertising the truck he's been looking for."*
- *"You don't need a feed, a new website, or an integration to start."*

**Retention note.** Same click-column risk as all display — pre-frame at the first meeting that this is an impression product measured on returns and total leads, not clicks. Additional watch item: inventory drift. Set the expectation early that the client tells us when something big changes, or connects a feed.

**Writer's notes:**
- **Statistics sources:** roughly 8 in 10 consumers ignore irrelevant marketing messages (Attentive global consumer study, widely reported); close to 90% prefer personalized over generic ads (IAB, via 2026 targeted-advertising compilations); ~71% expect personalized interactions with ~76% frustrated when they don't get them (McKinsey, widely reproduced). **All are survey findings, not audited measurements — attribute as "research shows" and re-verify annually.** Chosen because all three prove one idea: *relevance is the difference between an ad that registers and one that's wallpaper.*
- **Deliberately excluded:** the CPM figure, the click-through estimate, the monthly minimum, and the contract term. Per standing rules the payment *model* is named and the rate is not; the impression arithmetic lives only in the Targeted Display module.
- **Correction to an earlier flag:** this sheet clearly shows a 0.10% click-through estimate, which strongly suggests the "10%" previously flagged on the Targeted Display sheet was an OCR artifact dropping a leading zero, not an error on the collateral. No action needed.
- **Sheet tension worth confirming:** the copy says no inventory integration is required, while the panel lists several inventory integrations. The script resolves this as "optional, and useful when it exists." Confirm that's how it's actually sold.
- **Payoff vertical (used-car lot)** is new. Updated list: diner, roofer, flooring co., hearing clinic, insurance agency, storage co., garden center, medical clinic, auto repair shop, pest control co., RV dealership, **used-car lot**.
