# Targeted Landing Pages — Lesson Script
**Pillar:** Online Visibility (Room #7 in the Digital House)
**Audience:** Media AEs and agency personnel learning digital, selling to small local businesses in towns under 100k
**Format:** Browser module — animated text + real photos/screenshots + green-box callouts + voice-over (multi-voice). No line illustrations or metaphor graphics.
**Runtime target:** ~6.5 minutes + 10-question quiz
**Voice:** Plain-spoken, analogy-first, define every term, "you've got this" energy. No pricing, no internal specifics.

## SECTION A — The Reusable Tactic Lesson Template
*(Use this exact shape for all ~25 tactic modules. Only the content changes — the beats never do.)*

Two questions before you write: **"What's the plain-English definition?"** and **"What are the 3 core mechanics?"** Everything else follows the beats.

**Voice convention (all modules):**
- **V1 — Guide:** the teaching spine. Carries every segment.
- **V2 — Scene:** short in-the-world lines only — a customer's search, a quoted reaction, a review snippet, and the quiz prompts. Its job is to signal a "mode switch" and keep the ear awake.
- *(Optional V3 later if you want a distinct quiz host — but two voices covers everything below.)*

**On-screen rule — the visual kit (all modules):** keep it clean and real. Three ingredients only:
1. **Animated text** — key words and short lines build/fade in as V1 speaks. This is the workhorse.
2. **Real photos** — actual screenshots of the product (real landing pages on a phone, a real search results page) and real photography of people/businesses (an insurance agent at a desk, a contractor's truck, a small-town main street). No clip-art, no cartoon figures.
3. **Green highlight box** — a green callout box drawn over a real screenshot to point at a specific section (same style as the sales sheet: box the town name in the headline, box the Call button), with a text label animating in beside it.

**No cheesy line illustrations, icon/emoji cards, or metaphor graphics** (no glowing houses, map pins with faces, fishing-net drawings, little walking figures). Any analogy the Guide speaks stays *spoken only* — we don't draw it. Quiz cards being tappable is UI, not illustration, so that stays.

| # | Segment | Time | Job | On-screen feel | Maps to your 3 Qs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Hook + What It Is** | 0:00–0:45 | Relatable moment → one-sentence definition | Real screenshot; green box + text label animate in | **① What is it?** |
| 2 | **Why It Matters** | 0:45–1:30 | The stakes for a real small business, before any mechanics | Real photo (person on phone / small business); stakes text builds | (the "so what") |

**Quiz blueprint (same every tactic):** Q1–3 = the 3 mechanics · Q4–6 = terms · Q7 = who it's good for + why · Q8 = why-it-matters scenario · Q9–10 = features/benefits (one may use the Segment-6 payoff).

**Answer placement rule (all modules):** vary which letter is correct — roughly even across A, B, C, and D, with no repeating pattern. If the correct answer sits at B nine times out of ten, trainees stop reading the options and start pattern-matching, and the quiz measures nothing. Also keep the wrong answers plausible; obviously silly distractors have the same effect.

**Payoff rules (Segment 6):** pick the easiest-to-picture business from Segment 3; keep outcomes plain and clearly *illustrative* — no invented numbers, no real names.

## SECTION B — Targeted Landing Pages: Full Script

> **Naming note for the writer:** "landing page" gets used two different ways in this catalog, and reps *will* mix them up. In the website module it means a page built for one ad or offer with the distractions stripped out. Here it means a page built for one service in one town, aimed at search. Same craft, different job. Segment 1 addresses the overlap head-on in two lines — keep them.

### SEGMENT 1 — Hook + What It Is *(0:00–0:45)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Real phone screenshot of a search for *"flood insurance in Springfield."* A **green box** draws around a result whose headline reads almost exactly that back, label *page built for this search.* Then a second screenshot of a generic "Our Services" page listing eight things with no town named anywhere — **green box** on the word *Services*, label *page built for nobody in particular.*

**V2 — Scene:** *"Flood insurance in Springfield."*
**V1 — Guide:** Somebody types that. Very specific — a thing, and a town. Now, two businesses come up. One page says "Flood Insurance in Springfield" right across the top. The other says "Our Services," with a list of eight things and no town mentioned anywhere on it.
**V1 — Guide:** Same quality of company. Same prices. One of them just got the click.
**V1 — Guide:** So, plainly: a **targeted landing page** is a page built for one specific service in one specific town, written to match what somebody actually typed, with one obvious next step on it.
**V1 — Guide:** Here's the analogy that'll land for you, because you already do this for a living: you'd never cut one generic spot and run it in six different towns without ever saying a town name. You'd mention the town. Same idea — one page per service, per town.
**V1 — Guide:** Quick heads up, because two things share this name. In the website world, "landing page" usually means a page built for one ad or one offer. Here it means a page built for one search. Same craft, different job — and if somebody asks, "a page built to do one thing" covers both.

### SEGMENT 2 — Why It Matters *(0:45–1:30)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Real photo of a small-business owner in a truck or at a desk. Then a real map view with a **green box** around one town where the office sits, and lighter boxes around five surrounding towns, label *serves all six · shows up in one.* Then two real page screenshots side by side — a generic services page and a town-specific page — with green boxes on what's different. Stakes text builds: *invisible in most of their market → specific beats general → the homepage can't do this job.*

**V1 — Guide:** So why does a business need these? Three reasons.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: most businesses are invisible in most of the area they serve.** Here's the thing nobody notices. A plumber's office is in one town, but he'll happily drive to six. Search engines mostly know about the one. So five out of six towns' worth of customers never see him — not because he can't do the work, but because nothing on his website ever says he'll come out there.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: specific beats general, every single time.** When somebody types a thing plus a place, they're not browsing — they're ready. And the page that answers that exact question wins, because search engines want to hand people the closest match, and so does the human doing the searching. A page called "Services" is a closest match to nothing.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: the homepage can't do this job.** A homepage has to introduce the whole company to everybody — every service, every town, all at once. That's a real job, and it's the opposite of this one. Sending a specific searcher to a homepage is handing somebody who asked one clear question a brochure and telling them to go find it.
**V1 — Guide:** So the stakes are pretty plain: they're already doing the work in those towns. They're just not showing up for it.

### SEGMENT 3 — Who It's Great For *(1:30–2:15)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Three real photos appear one at a time — a contractor's truck on a rural road, a clinic or insurance office with a visible list of services, a small-town business on the edge of a market. Each business type's name and its one-line reason type in beside the photo.

**V1 — Guide:** So who do you picture pitching this to? Three easy ones.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses that serve a bunch of towns.** Plumbers, HVAC, electricians, roofers, insurance agencies, attorneys, anybody with a driving radius. The gap between where their office is and where they'll actually go is exactly the opportunity.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses with several distinct services.** An insurance agency selling flood, fire, life, and auto. A clinic with four different treatments. A contractor doing roofing, siding, and gutters. Every one of those is a different search by a different person — and right now they're all crammed onto one page.
**V1 — Guide:** **Businesses whose office is in the wrong town.** This one's a favorite. Somebody's shop sits ten minutes outside the bigger market they want, so they lose every search to the folks physically inside it. A page built for that town is how they get in the conversation.
**V1 — Guide:** Notice the thread: if what they sell — or where they sell it — can be split into pieces, every piece deserves its own page.

### SEGMENT 4 — How It Works: 3 Core Mechanics *(2:15–4:00)*
**ON-SCREEN:** One real page screenshot set throughout; a **green box** moves for each mechanic. (1) A grid animates in as text — three services down the side, two towns across the top, six cells filling in — label *the grid*. (2) Green box moves across a real page: the headline with service and town in it, a mention of a nearby landmark, the service-area list → label *match the search*. (3) Green box lands on a single Call button, then a booking button, then directions → label *one action*. Closes on text: *A page for each · Match the search · One clear action.*

**V1 — Guide:** How does this actually work? Three moving parts. Just three.
**V1 — Guide:** **One: the grid.** This is the whole idea, and it's simpler than it sounds. Write their services down one side. Write the towns they serve across the top. Every square where those two meet is a page. Flood insurance in Springfield. Flood insurance in Franklin. Fire insurance in Springfield. And so on.
**V1 — Guide:** That's it. That's the product. Instead of one page trying to rank for everything everywhere — which means it ranks for nothing in particular — you get a page aimed at each real search a real person makes.
**V1 — Guide:** One honest note here, and it matters. This is not find-and-replace with the town name swapped out. Search engines are good at spotting a page that's just a copy, and thin, cookie-cutter pages can do more harm than good. Each page has to actually be about that service in that town — what they do there, how far they'll go, what's nearby. More pages isn't the goal. More *real* pages is.
**V1 — Guide:** **Two: match the search.** Once a page exists, it has to sound like what somebody typed. The headline says the service and the town. The writing mentions the area — the neighborhoods, a landmark, the towns nearby. The page states plainly that they serve that area.
**V1 — Guide:** And there's a bonus that comes free. As these pages stack up, the website as a whole starts to look like an expert on that subject rather than a business that mentions it once. Search engines notice that. So each page helps itself *and* helps the rest of the site.
**V1 — Guide:** **Three: one clear action.** Every one of these pages exists to get one thing to happen. Call. Book. Get directions. That's why we call it a landing page instead of just a page — somebody lands, and there's one obvious next move sitting right there, not buried under a menu of nine other options.
**V1 — Guide:** And this is the part that separates it from ordinary search work. Search work gets somebody to the site. These pages are built to get somebody to *act* once they're there.
**V1 — Guide:** That's it. A page for each, match the search, one clear action.

### SEGMENT 5 — Common Terms *(4:00–4:45)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Each term and its one-line definition animate in as text, one at a time. **Green box** on the real screenshot where relevant — the headline for the two keyword types, the service-area line, the Call button for conversion.

**V1 — Guide:** Five terms so you sound like you know your stuff — because now you do.
**V1 — Guide:** **Landing page** — a page built to do one thing. Here, that's answering one search and getting one action.
**V1 — Guide:** **Service keyword and location keyword** — the two halves of what somebody types. "Flood insurance" is the service half. "In Springfield" is the location half. These pages are built where the two meet, and that's why the sales sheet shows a grid.
**V1 — Guide:** **Search intent** — what the person actually wants. Somebody typing one broad word is looking around. Somebody typing a service plus a town is ready to call. Matching intent just means giving the ready person the specific thing.
**V1 — Guide:** **Service area** — the towns a business actually serves, as opposed to the one town its office sits in. The gap between those two is the whole opportunity here.
**V1 — Guide:** **Conversion** — when a visitor does the thing the page was built for. Calls, books, comes in. Traffic is people arriving. Conversion is people acting. Say that difference out loud in a meeting and you'll sound like you've been doing this for years.

### SEGMENT 6 — Features, Benefits & The Payoff *(4:45–6:00)*
**ON-SCREEN:** Feature names animate in as a text list, green box ticking to each element on the real page screenshot. Then four benefit lines build as text: *Show up where they already work · Match what people typed · Every page strengthens the site · Built to make them act.* For the payoff: two real insurance-agency screenshots side by side — **before** (one "Our Services" page, no towns named) and **after** (a page for each service in each town, with a Call button up top) — plus a real photo of an agent at a desk. A small text tag reads *illustrative example.*

**V1 — Guide:** So what's in it? Custom pages built for each service in each town they serve — written for that combination, not copied. Headlines and writing built around the actual words people search. Local detail on the page: the area, nearby towns, a plain statement of where they'll go. One clear action on each page — call, book, or directions. Pages built so they strengthen the whole site as they accumulate. And reporting, so you can see which pages are pulling and which need work.
**V1 — Guide:** What does the owner get? Four things. They **show up in towns where they already work** but were invisible. They **match what people actually typed**, so more of those people click. **Every new page makes the whole site stronger** on that subject. And every page is **built to make somebody act**, not just to sit there.
**V1 — Guide:** Under the hood it's one simple idea: search engines are trying to hand somebody the closest match to what they asked. A specific page beats a general one, every time. It's not a trick — it's just being the most useful answer.
**V1 — Guide:** So picture a small insurance agency. Office in one town, licensed across the whole county, quietly writing policies in six towns for thirty years. Before: one page called "Our Services," with flood, fire, life, and auto in a bulleted list, and not one town name anywhere on the site. If you lived two towns over, nothing about that page suggested they'd help you.
**V1 — Guide:** After: a page for flood in each town, fire in each town, life in each town. Each one naming the area, mentioning what's nearby, with a phone number at the top instead of the bottom.
**V2 — Scene:** *"Honestly, I didn't even know you all covered out here."*
**V1 — Guide:** — and that started being a normal way for calls to open. They'd been covering out there for three decades. Their website just finally said so. *(Illustrative — not a promise of specific numbers.)*

### SEGMENT 7 — Recap + Quiz Bridge *(6:00–6:30)*
**ON-SCREEN:** The three mechanics recap as animated text — 1: the grid · 2: match the search · 3: one action — with the real page screenshot behind them and the green box tapping each area one last time. A *Quiz →* button slides up.

**V1 — Guide:** Three parts, one more time. One: the grid — services down one side, towns across the top, a page for every square. Two: match the search — the headline and the writing sound like what somebody typed, and each real page makes the whole site stronger. Three: one clear action — call, book, or directions, right there where they land.
**V1 — Guide:** And the one line to carry into a room: they're already doing the work in those towns, they're just not showing up for it.
**V1 — Guide:** That's targeted landing pages. Quick ten-question check to lock it in. No pressure — you already know this.

## SECTION C — Quiz (10 Questions)
**ON-SCREEN:** One card per question, four tappable options; correct choice highlights green on select. **V2 — Scene** reads each question stem aloud.

**1.** An HVAC company offers three services and works in four towns. Using the grid idea, how many pages does that suggest?
A) One page listing all three services
B) Twelve — one for each service in each town ✅
C) Four — one per town, listing everything
D) Three — one per service, no towns named

**2.** Why does a targeted landing page need the town named in the headline and the writing?
A) So the business can charge more for the page
B) It doesn't matter where the town is mentioned
C) Because towns must legally be listed on a website
D) So the page matches what the person actually typed and reads as the closest answer ✅

**3.** What makes these "landing" pages rather than just extra pages?
A) Each one is built to get one clear action — call, book, or directions ✅
B) They only work on mobile phones
C) They replace the homepage
D) They can only be reached from an ad

**4.** "Search intent" means…
A) The number of times a keyword appears on a page
B) How much a business is willing to spend
C) What the person searching actually wants — browsing versus ready to call ✅
D) The order results appear in

**5.** In "roof repair in Franklin," which part is the location keyword?
A) "Roof repair"
B) "In Franklin" ✅
C) Both halves equally
D) Neither — that's a service keyword only

**6.** A business's "service area" is…
A) The section of the website listing its services
B) The room where the work gets done
C) The single town where its office is located
D) All the towns it actually serves, which is usually more than where its office sits ✅

**7.** Which business is an especially strong fit, and why?
A) A plumbing company whose office is in one town but who drives to six ✅
B) An online-only retailer that ships nationwide
C) A business with one service and one location and no plans to grow
D) A company with no website

**8.** A roofer works in five towns but his site never names any of them. What's most likely happening?
A) Search engines assume he covers the whole state
B) Nothing — town names don't affect search
C) People in four of those towns don't find him, because nothing says he'll come out there ✅
D) His homepage automatically ranks in all five

**9.** Why is it a problem to build these pages by copying one page and swapping the town name?
A) It isn't — that's the fastest way to do it
B) Search engines can spot copied, thin pages, and those can do more harm than good ✅
C) Copying is fine as long as the headline changes
D) Because each page has to be a different color

**10.** In the insurance-agency example, what actually changed?
A) They started selling different kinds of insurance
B) They opened offices in five new towns
C) They lowered their premiums
D) They already served those towns — the website finally said so, with a page for each service in each town ✅

**Answer key:** 1-B · 2-D · 3-A · 4-C · 5-B · 6-D · 7-A · 8-C · 9-B · 10-D
